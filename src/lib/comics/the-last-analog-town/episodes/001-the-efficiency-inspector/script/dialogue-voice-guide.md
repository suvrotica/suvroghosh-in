# Album 001 dialogue and turn-taking guide

Status: binding editorial guide; dialogue approval remains pending.

## The spoken-aloud rule

Every human line must sound like something this person would say to the person in front of them,
at this moment, while doing the pictured action. The panel description carries explanation. A
speaker must not narrate information that both people already know merely to inform the reader.

Human dialogue may use contractions, fragments, unfinished thoughts, brief corrections, silence,
and interrupted sentences. Grammatical completeness is not a virtue by itself. Unit C-17 is the
deliberate exception: Cecil remains formal, complete and procedural.

## Address and name rule

- A speaker may use a name or title when first getting someone's attention, calling across a space,
  re-entering an exchange after a gap, singling someone out in a group, warning them, or applying
  deliberate emotional pressure.
- After two people have established an exchange, they answer one another directly. They do not keep
  repeating names or titles.
- A new page does not automatically reset the conversation. If the location, action and participants
  continue across the page turn, the exchange continues too.
- An answer must react to the preceding line before introducing new information. If it could be
  moved to another scene unchanged, it is probably exposition rather than conversation.
- Titles signal relationship. Riju may begin formally with “Director Vale” when anxious or in
  public; Vale's “Mr Dhar” is purposeful, not verbal punctuation. Familiar principals generally do
  not address one another by full name.

## Balloon rhythm

- Prefer one spoken intention per balloon.
- Give the last few words the comic beat: the correction, threat, joke, admission or reveal.
- Split a breathless anxious line only where the speaker would actually pause.
- Do not make adjacent balloons paraphrase each other.
- Let reaction shots and visible action replace spoken explanation.
- Keep most human balloons under twenty words. Longer lines need a dramatic reason and a readable
  breathing point.
- Do not shrink lettering to rescue overwritten dialogue. Rewrite the line.

## Principal voices

### Ila Dastidar

Short, exact and politically alive. She asks pointed questions and names the person who pays for a
decision. Her driest line often lands after everyone else has finished explaining. When danger
rises, she becomes quieter and more concrete. She does not lecture about “human judgement”; she
identifies the patient, worker, street or deadline a rule has ignored.

### Riju Dhar

Bright, eager and a little too fast. When anxious he begins technically, adds a qualification, then
translates himself. His early dialogue seeks approval; his later dialogue accepts responsibility in
plain, short sentences. He is allowed to stumble, self-correct and say “I thought—”. He must never
sound as if he has read the panel description.

### Babul-da

Unhurried, practical and socially observant. He describes what happens next and lets the
contradiction expose itself. His jokes are rarely announced as jokes. He uses “dada” or “didi” only
where the relationship earns it, and does not sprinkle forms of address through an established
exchange.

### Dr Ananya Rook

Concrete, clinical and compassionate. She distinguishes what she saw from what she suspects. She
uses a clear physical comparison when it genuinely helps, then stops. Anger makes her more precise,
not more grandiloquent. She does not speak like an infallible doctor or a policy paper.

### Director Vale

Polished, calm and recognisably human. Vale can contract ordinary verbs and answer a joke; formality
intensifies only when speaking for the institution. Their danger lies in reasonable premises and
controlled omissions, not robotic diction. A documented failure changes their next sentence even
when it does not change their mission.

### Unit C-17 (“Cecil”)

Formal, literal and procedural. Cecil does not normally use contractions. It states status,
confidence and inconsistency precisely. Its humour comes from applying a valid rule to an impossible
reality. As the album progresses, its questions may become more discriminating, but never turn into
human sentiment or cute-machine chatter.

## Review pass for every scene

1. Mark who is trying to get what from whom.
2. Read only the human balloons aloud, without panel descriptions.
3. Remove repeated names after attention has been established.
4. Remove facts visible in the art or already understood by both speakers.
5. Make each response acknowledge the preceding move.
6. Check that covering the speaker label would still leave the principal voices distinguishable.
7. Check that the shortest possible line has not erased character, hesitation or subtext.
8. Leave the scene as `dialogueApproved: false` until the revised lettering has been read on the
   assembled page at normal size.
