The problem with familiar AI language is not that every fashionable word is forbidden. It is that the words often arrive before the writer has identified an actor, action, mechanism, measurement or limit. Choose a rewrite that states what actually happens.

<!-- resource-copy:start -->

## Metaphors that need a real object

1. **Delve** — Ask whether the work is reading, comparing, testing, tracing or explaining. Try “examine the error logs,” “compare the three studies,” or “trace the claim to its source.” Keep _delve_ for a deliberately sustained investigation, not every paragraph that goes beyond an introduction.
2. **Tapestry** — Name the relationship instead of admiring its complexity. Try “a history shaped by migration, labour and language” or “three systems with overlapping dependencies.” Keep _tapestry_ when the interweaving itself is the subject and the threads have been identified.
3. **Landscape** — Specify whether you mean a market, research field, regulatory environment or set of tools. Try “the current speech-recognition market” or “published evidence on clinical summarisation.” Keep _landscape_ for a genuine overview that maps distinct regions or actors, and for literal terrain.

## Claims of magnitude and novelty

4. **Transformative** — State the before-and-after change. Try “reduced review time from two hours to twenty minutes” or “allowed nurses to retrieve records without calling the archive.” Keep it only when the underlying practice, capability or outcome materially changes.
5. **Paradigm shift** — Name the old model, the new model and the evidence that practice moved between them. Try “teams now review generated code before execution instead of writing every first draft manually.” Keep the phrase for a field-wide change in governing assumptions, not a product update.
6. **Game-changing** — Identify whose game changed and by what rule. Try “makes same-day transcription affordable for small clinics” or “removes the previous memory limit.” Keep it for rare changes that alter competitive or operational possibilities, preferably after evidence exists.
7. **Cutting-edge** — Give a date, version or technical distinction. Try “uses the July 2026 release,” “supports multimodal input,” or “matches the best reported benchmark under this test.” Keep it when the edge is defined and current, not as a decorative synonym for new.
8. **Revolutionary** — Describe the discontinuity rather than announcing a revolution. Try “eliminates the manual matching step” or “makes a task possible on consumer hardware for the first time.” Keep it for changes broad and durable enough to justify the historical claim.
9. **Pivotal** — Say what depended on the event. Try “the approval allowed the trial to begin” or “this finding changed the migration plan.” Keep _pivotal_ when a decision or outcome genuinely turned on that point.

## Vague actions

10. **Leverage** — Replace it with the actual use: “use retrieval to cite source passages,” “analyse the audit table,” “reuse the existing component,” or “borrow against the asset.” Keep _leverage_ for literal mechanical or financial leverage, and where the advantage being amplified is explicit.
11. **Unlock** — Name the barrier removed. Try “decrypt the archive,” “give analysts access to previously siloed records,” or “reduce inference cost enough for offline use.” Keep it when something was genuinely inaccessible and the mechanism of access matters.
12. **Harness** — State what is controlled and for what work. Try “route model outputs into the review queue” or “use waste heat to warm the building.” Keep it for power, energy or a difficult force being deliberately controlled; otherwise _use_ is usually enough.
13. **Navigate** — Identify the decisions or obstacles. Try “compare the three licensing options,” “complete the appeals process,” or “choose among conflicting clinical guidelines.” Keep it when the path really is constrained, branching or uncertain.
14. **Empower** — State the new authority, resource or capability. Try “allow editors to reject generated changes,” “give patients access to their test history,” or “fund local teams to choose suppliers.” Keep it when power or decision rights actually move.
15. **Optimize** — Supply the objective and constraints. Try “minimise latency while keeping recall above 0.90” or “reduce energy use without lowering room ventilation.” Keep it for a defined optimisation problem, not as a general promise to improve.
16. **Streamline** — Name the steps removed or combined. Try “replace three approvals with one,” “pre-fill the patient identifier,” or “run validation and formatting in the same command.” Keep it when a process becomes demonstrably simpler rather than merely faster in a demo.

## Smoothness, strength and reach

17. **Seamless** — Describe the boundary and what the user must still do. Try “sign-on carries between the two applications” or “the import preserves headings but still requires image review.” Keep it only when a transition is genuinely unnoticeable under tested conditions.
18. **Robust** — Name the stress or variation survived. Try “continued to parse all fixtures after fields were reordered,” “remained calibrated across the three hospitals,” or “recovered after a network interruption.” Keep _robust_ in engineering and statistics when robustness has an explicit test.
19. **At scale** — Give the scale. Try “for 20,000 concurrent sessions,” “across 140 clinics,” or “for ten million documents within six hours.” Keep it when behaviour at a stated volume, geography or organisational breadth differs from a small trial.
20. **Innovative** — Identify what is new in context. Try “combines an offline index with on-device speech recognition, which the previous workflow did not support.” Keep it when novelty has been compared against a relevant baseline, not when the work is merely competent.
21. **Ecosystem** — List the interdependent participants and exchanges. Try “vendors, hospitals, regulators and patients exchange data and incentives through…” Keep it when feedback, dependency or co-evolution matters; use _industry_, _toolset_ or _community_ when that is all you mean.
22. **Holistic** — Enumerate the dimensions considered. Try “assesses symptoms, medication, housing and caregiver support” or “reviews cost, security, staffing and maintenance.” Keep it when the scope intentionally crosses boundaries and omissions are acknowledged.
23. **Comprehensive** — Say what the coverage includes and excludes. Try “covers every public API endpoint as of version 4.2, excluding administrator-only routes.” Keep it when completeness has been checked against a defined universe.
24. **Impactful** — Name the effect and recipient. Try “reduced missed appointments,” “changed the board's decision,” or “helped first-time users complete the form.” Keep it only if _impact_ is the measured subject; otherwise the concrete verb is stronger.
25. **Multifaceted** — List the relevant facets. Try “the failure had technical, contractual and staffing causes.” Keep it when several distinct aspects will actually be examined, not as a warning that reality is complicated.

## Throat-clearing that delays the sentence

26. **In today's rapidly evolving world** — Begin with the dated change. Try “Since the January 2026 regulation…” or “Model context windows doubled during the last release cycle…” If no specific change follows, delete the phrase. Keep it only when the rate of change is itself analysed.
27. **It is important to note** — State the important fact and why it changes interpretation. Try “The trial excluded children, so its safety results do not apply to paediatric care.” Keep the signpost sparingly when a long argument genuinely risks hiding a decisive qualification.
28. **Shed light on** — Name what became known. Try “The interviews identified why nurses bypassed the alert” or “The experiment showed that temperature, not prompt length, caused the variance.” Keep the metaphor when the evidence clarifies only part of an uncertainty rather than resolving it.

## Usage notes

Do not perform a blind global replacement. “Robust standard error,” “ecological landscape,” “financial leverage” and “optimisation objective” can be exact technical language. The test is not whether a model uses the phrase often; it is whether the sentence would let a careful reader recover the actor, action, evidence and boundary.
