Decay is not one brown stain, rain is not one grey curtain, heat is not merely “scorching”, and delay is not always passive. Choose the word that identifies a material process, weather pattern, bodily condition or administrative action you can actually observe.

## Decay and material neglect

1. **Weathered** — Altered by prolonged exposure to sun, rain or wind: softened paint, roughened timber, dulled stone. It can imply endurance as readily as damage.
2. **Faded** — Reduced in colour or contrast by light, washing or time. A faded sign may remain sound; fading alone does not mean rot.
3. **Tarnished** — Lost surface lustre through a thin chemical film, especially on metal. It suggests discolouration rather than deep structural loss.
4. **Oxidised** — Chemically combined with oxygen. Rust is iron oxidation, but copper, aluminium and pigments oxidise differently; name the material when possible.
5. **Corroded** — Gradually consumed or weakened by chemical or electrochemical action. Unlike _weathered_, it can imply loss of section and performance.
6. **Rust bloom** — A local reddish-orange emergence where corrosion reaches or stains a surface: “Rust blooms around the exposed reinforcement.”
7. **Spalling** — Flaking, breaking or bursting away from a masonry or concrete face. In reinforced concrete it may reveal corrosion beneath and should not be treated as decorative patina.
8. **Delamination** — Separation of bonded layers, such as plaster from masonry, veneer from board or paint from its substrate. The surface may sound hollow before it falls.
9. **Efflorescence** — A pale crystalline deposit left when water carries dissolved salts to a porous surface and evaporates. It is evidence of moisture movement, not the same thing as mould.
10. **Mildewed** — Marked or smelling of superficial fungal growth in persistent damp. Use for cloth, paper, paint and rooms; do not make it a synonym for every musty smell.
11. **Rotting** — Undergoing biological decomposition, especially in timber or other organic matter. _Softened_, _punky_ or _fibrous_ can describe the texture more precisely.
12. **Neglected** — Denied expected care, cleaning or repair. This names a maintenance history or social judgement, not a specific material mechanism; show the blocked drain, unsealed joint or unrepaired crack that earns it.

## Rain and the movement of water

13. **Drizzle** — Fine, closely spaced droplets falling steadily. It reduces visibility and accumulates quietly rather than striking with force.
14. **Shower** — Rain of limited duration, often with a distinct beginning and end. It may be light or heavy; brevity is the useful distinction.
15. **Squall** — A sudden, sharp increase in wind, often carrying rain. The gust front and abruptness matter more than the total rainfall.
16. **Downpour** — Sustained heavy rain that overwhelms attention and sometimes drainage. It is stronger and less momentary than a passing shower.
17. **Cloudburst** — Exceptionally intense, localised rain over a short period. Reserve it for an event that is genuinely sudden and concentrated, not any wet afternoon.
18. **Driving rain** — Rain forced obliquely by wind against faces, windows or walls: useful when exposure direction affects comfort or leakage.
19. **Rain-slick** — Covered by a thin, reflective and potentially slippery film: “The rain-slick road doubled the tail-lights.” It describes a surface, not rainfall.
20. **Waterlogged** — Saturated beyond useful drainage or air space. Soil, fabric and a playing field can be waterlogged; a street under standing water is more plainly flooded.
21. **Pooling** — Collecting in shallow local depressions because a surface lacks fall or an outlet is blocked. The noun _ponding_ is common in roofing and drainage work.
22. **Runoff** — Water flowing over a surface rather than soaking in. Name its source and destination when consequence matters: “Roof runoff crossed the entrance.”
23. **Seepage** — Slow passage of water through pores, joints or small openings, often without a visible jet. A spreading damp patch may reveal it.
24. **Wicking** — Capillary movement through a porous material, sometimes upward or sideways against gravity. It explains why a cloth, wall base or paper stack grows wet beyond the contact point.

## Heat, air and stored warmth

25. **Sun-baked** — Dried, hardened or intensely warmed by direct sun: a terrace, clay surface or exposed seat. It combines exposure and accumulated effect.
26. **Heat-soaked** — Warmed through after sustained exposure, so that cooling the surface does not immediately cool the object or room.
27. **Radiant heat** — Heat felt from a hot surface or the sun without relying on warmed air: “The west wall radiated heat after dusk.”
28. **Heat shimmer** — Visible optical wavering caused by layers of air at different temperatures. It is an observed effect, not a measure of temperature.
29. **Close** — Uncomfortably warm with little air movement, often before rain. In British and Indian English it describes the room or weather rather than physical distance.
30. **Muggy** — Warm and humid in a way that hinders sweat evaporation. Colloquial and bodily; more specific than simply _hot_.
31. **Stifling** — So hot, airless or humid that breathing and concentration feel constrained. Stronger than _close_; it reports experienced discomfort.
32. **Stagnant air** — Air with little perceptible movement, allowing heat, odour or pollutants to linger. Unlike _still_, it usually carries a negative judgement.
33. **Sweltering** — Suffering or causing oppressive heat. It suits people and conditions, but not a precise technical account.
34. **Glare-heated** — A useful compound for a bright exposed surface whose reflected light intensifies perceived heat: “The glare-heated platform offered no visual rest.” Use sparingly and support it with observable detail.
35. **Heat trapped overnight** — Plain language for buildings and streets that release stored daytime heat too slowly to provide nocturnal relief.
36. **Thermal lag** — The delay between an external temperature change and its effect inside a material or space. It can be helpful or harmful depending on climate, construction and timing.

## Bureaucracy, delay and evasion

37. **Backlog** — Accumulated work awaiting action. It names volume and delay but not the cause; staffing, prioritisation or a broken process may sit behind it.
38. **Bottleneck** — A stage whose limited capacity constrains the entire flow: “Every application waits for one signature.” Identify the stage instead of blaming “the system” in general.
39. **Procedural delay** — Time added by required steps, sequencing or notice periods. The phrase is neutral until the writer shows that the procedure is unnecessary, misused or disproportionate.
40. **Deferral** — A decision to postpone action or decision: “The committee deferred the vote until costings arrived.” Unlike mere delay, it has an identifiable act and usually a stated condition.
41. **Referral loop** — Repeated transfer between offices or people, none accepting authority to conclude the matter. Map the loop if possible.
42. **Buck-passing** — Colloquial assignment of responsibility to somebody else to avoid ownership. It is an accusation; show who transferred what decision to whom.
43. **Remit dispute** — Disagreement over which unit has the authority or duty to act. Sometimes legitimate, sometimes a convenient border skirmish that leaves the case untouched.
44. **Procedural evasion** — Use of formal steps to avoid the substance of a request or duty: answering the wrong question, demanding irrelevant proof or restarting a completed sequence.
45. **Paper compliance** — Production of required forms, policies or records without the corresponding practice or outcome. It distinguishes documentary appearance from operational reality.
46. **Administrative silence** — Absence of acknowledgement, decision or explanation after a request enters a process. Give dates and attempted contacts so the phrase carries evidence.
47. **Discretionary hold** — A pause imposed through an official's permitted judgement rather than an automatic rule. State the authority, reason and review route where known.
48. **Institutional inertia** — Persistence of an established practice because changing incentives, habits and dependencies is difficult. It is broader than one person's reluctance and should not become an excuse for leaving mechanisms unnamed.

Metaphor can clarify texture, but melodrama can erase mechanism. A “rotting institution” tells less than a referral loop, an unowned decision and a six-month backlog; similarly, “angry rain” tells less than wind direction, intensity and where the water went.
