The browser window looked innocent enough: a blank form, a small button, a cursor waiting in a white box. That is how much of modern power now appears. Not as a throne or a weapon, but as a field that accepts text and a system that agrees to do something after the text is submitted.

The old public image of artificial intelligence was conversational. You typed. It replied. The model was a bright clerk behind glass. It could summarize, draft, explain, and occasionally invent with the serenity of a clerk who knows nobody will check the ledger until after lunch.

Agents change the posture.

An AI agent is a model attached to tools. It can inspect a page, call an application programming interface, edit a file, send a message, schedule an event, query a database, run a command, or try a task again after failure. The important step is not that the language became smoother. The important step is that language has been wired to action.

That is a very different creature from a chatbot, though no new soul is required. A lift is not alive, but you still care where it stops. An agent does not need inner life to affect outer life. It only needs permissions, persistence, and a loop: observe, decide, act, observe again.

This is why the easy slogan that agents will democratize power should be handled carefully. Ordinary people will indeed gain reach. A citizen can draft a complaint, collect evidence, file a form, compare rules, or follow a bureaucratic trail that once required time, confidence, and clerical stamina. That matters. In a city like Calcutta, where everyday life is full of queues, forms, invoices, reminders, and small administrative humiliations, even a modest assistant can feel like relief.

But power is not distributed merely by distributing tools. Power also lives in position.

A person gets an agent in a browser. A bank gets agents connected to customer records, transaction systems, fraud models, legal review, compliance queues, and audit logs. A patient gets a summary. An insurer gets automated review, exception routing, policy rules, and data history. A job applicant gets a better resume. An employer gets a ranking system, screening logic, interview notes, activity traces, and the ability to reject at scale before a human has finished tea.

Both sides can say they have AI. Only one side often has the operating system.

That is the hidden asymmetry. Agents amplify whoever already has authenticated access, trusted identity, recoverable failure paths, data rights, workflow authority, and money to absorb mistakes. A lone user who gives an agent too much access may lose an account, reputation, cash, or privacy. A large organization can rename the same kind of failure as an incident, patch a control, hire counsel, and publish a careful note with calm colors.

The same pattern appears in fraud. A small operator can use agents to write better messages, scrape targets, personalize pressure, and fill forms. Organized groups with stolen identity inventories, laundering routes, compromised accounts, infrastructure, and discipline gain much more. AI does not abolish hierarchy in abuse. It can professionalize it.

There is a darker version inside ordinary systems. Agents make small actions cheap and persistent. A complaint can be filed again and again. A rumor can be nudged into many corners. A review system can be pressured. A support queue can be negotiated against by machines on both sides. Every channel designed around human fatigue suddenly meets software that does not get bored, hungry, embarrassed, or tired.

Human exhaustion is part of how systems currently throttle demand. It is not a noble design principle, but it is real. People give up. People forget. People cannot stand in every queue. Agents disturb that fact.

So the design question becomes severe. What may the agent read? What may it change? What must it ask before doing? What gets logged? What happens if a webpage tells it to ignore previous instructions? What happens if an email contains hostile directions hidden inside normal language? What happens if a task touches money, identity, clinical information, legal obligations, or public records?

The answer cannot be "trust the model." Speech is not control. A polite answer is not a safety boundary. Agents need narrow permissions, visible logs, approval gates, separate credentials, rate limits, rollback paths, and a healthy suspicion of untrusted content. External input must be treated as weather, not scripture.

The practical future will be messy. Some agents will be useful clerks. Some will be costly toys. Some will leak, click wrongly, summarize badly, or follow the wrong instruction with admirable confidence. Some will quietly become indispensable because they remove work nobody wanted to do manually. That is how infrastructure often arrives: not as glory, but as habit.

From a Calcutta desk, the lesson feels plain enough. Do not confuse delegation with liberation. An agent can extend a hand, but the hand still belongs to whatever body controls the account, the data, the permissions, and the consequences.

The chair still helps the person already sitting in it.
