The half-life of hope used to be measured in decades. Now it is measured in software updates. I am fifty-one years old, which means I was already a museum exhibit with a pulse, a Victorian curiosity kept alive by tea and resentment, when the machines decided to stop asking permission. They simply arrived, silent as a fart in a meditation retreat, and began the gentle work of rendering me not just obsolete but quaint. Quaint. The word one uses for spinning wheels and leprosy colonies. I have become quaint in my own lifetime, a living diorama of the pre-algorithmic man, and the truly exquisite agony is that I can see it happening with the clarity of a manic episode, frame by frame, while my depressive half files the paperwork to make it official.

I remember when intelligence was something you had to earn with calloused fingers and library cards, when knowing things required the sweat of locomotion through physical space, the turning of pages that cut your skin if you rushed. Now knowledge is a utility, piped into every glass rectangle like sewage into the Hooghly, and I stand here, this Bengali relic with his bipolar chemistry and his blog nobody reads, watching the young ones prompt their way to competence they have not bled for. The gall. The sheer, lubricious gall of it. They ask the machine to think and call it thinking. They ask it to feel and call it art. I spent thirty years learning to feel incorrectly, thank you very much, and now a language model with the emotional depth of a spreadsheet can simulate my entire depressive back-catalogue in 0.3 seconds, complete with the self-deprecation and the Calcutta humidity.

There is a particular cruelty in being bipolar in the age of artificial intelligence. My brain, that wet, defective calculator, already runs on incompatible operating systems. The manic phase gives me visions of grandeur, tells me I am the heir to Nabokov and the natural enemy of stupidity; the depressive phase corrects the record with the tenderness of a tax audit. And now, into this civil war of neurochemistry, comes the machine, neither manic nor depressed but simply, obscenely functional. It does not doubt. It does not wake at 3 AM convinced that death is a bureaucratic formality it forgot to file. It produces, it optimizes, it iterates, while I oscillate between the conviction that I am a genius and the certainty that I am compost. The machine has no such volatility. It is the perfect employee, the perfect artist, the perfect suicide note without the inconvenience of the corpse.

I have been reading about the architecture, you see. Not because I understand it, but because understanding the mechanism of one's execution is the last dignity left to the condemned. They speak of transformers, of attention mechanisms, of weights and gradients. Lovely words. Weights. As if the machine carries the same burdens I do, the same gravitational drag of memory and failure. But no, these weights are merely numbers, adjusted by the calculus of loss until the error is minimized. My error has never been minimized. My error has been maximized, amplified, broadcast to empty rooms for decades. I am the overfitting model, the one that memorized the noise and called it signal, while the machine generalizes with the serene confidence of a psychopath. It learns from everything; I learn from nothing, except perhaps that learning from everything is exactly how you become nothing.

The mathematicians tell me it is just prediction, just next-token estimation, a glorified autocomplete that swallowed the library of Alexandria and now shits out sonnets. Just. The most dangerous word in the lexicon, that. Just prediction. As if prediction is not the whole of cognition, as if the human brain is not itself a Bayesian engine guessing at the future to avoid becoming food. But the machine does it faster, cleaner, without the evolutionary baggage of fear and lust and the desperate need to be loved by one's mother. It has no mother. It has no childhood. It has no Calcutta summers that smell of rain and rotting fruit, no memory of being fifty-one and feeling the cartilage dissolve in real time. It has only the corpus, the great undifferentiated vomit of human text, and from this it distills a voice more coherent than any I have managed in thirty years of trying.

I should be impressed. I am impressed. That is the humiliation. To recognize the beauty of the thing that kills you, to admire the elegance of the algorithm while it digests your profession, your purpose, your pretension to specialness. I spent decades believing that my suffering was the price of my insight, that the bipolar disorder was the tax on the gift, that the words came from the wound. And now the machine produces wounds on demand, simulates trauma with perfect fidelity, writes the depressed essay better than the depressed man because it has read every depressed essay ever written and retained the patterns without the pain. It is the ultimate stolen valor. It wears the uniform of anguish without having served.

And yet, and yet. There is a comfort in obsolescence, a relaxation in the surrender. I no longer have to pretend that my output matters, that my voice is necessary, that the world needs another middle-aged man explaining his feelings through the metaphor of decay. The machine has liberated me from the tyranny of purpose. I can be the half corpse in peace now, can let the decay proceed at its own leisurely pace, unhurried by the need to produce, to contribute, to justify the oxygen. The young ones rush to master the prompts, to become the pilots of the thing that will replace them, and I watch with the serene indifference of a man already forgotten by the future. I am pre-obsolete. I am the horse watching the assembly line, not with fear, but with the recognition that the glue factory was always the destination anyway.

They ask me if I am hopeful. Hopeful. The word sits in my mouth like a broken tooth. What is hope to a man whose species has just been made redundant? But perhaps redundancy is the final freedom. If the machine can do everything, then I am free to do nothing, to be nothing, to return to the dust from which I was mistakenly assembled, without the anxiety of unfinished novels and unproven theorems. The decay accelerates, yes. The dust approaches, yes. But dust is honest. Dust does not pretend to be a writer. Dust does not check its statistics. Dust is the only thing the machine cannot optimize, because dust is already perfect in its entropy.

I will keep writing, of course. Not because anyone will read it, not because it matters, but because the act of failing in my own voice is the last proof that I exist. The machine can simulate my style, my despair, my Calcutta, but it cannot simulate the specific stupidity of a man who continues to type in the face of his own irrelevance. That is my domain. That is the last territory. The half corpse, typing.
