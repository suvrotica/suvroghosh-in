A spreadsheet glows in a Calcutta room late at night, and in one column a worker is a cost. That is the first trick. The same person, after the spreadsheet is closed, becomes rent paid, groceries bought, phone recharge done, fees cleared, transport taken, and the small purchase that keeps somebody else's shop open for one more day.

This is the uncomfortable thought inside Brett Hemenway Falk and Gerry Tsoukalas's paper, "The AI Layoff Trap." The paper does not need evil executives, dramatic machines, or science-fiction panic. It needs only ordinary firms doing ordinary firm things: cutting payroll, adopting Artificial Intelligence, pleasing investors, shifting money toward infrastructure, and describing the whole operation as transformation because business language has learned to wear a soft shirt over a sharp object.

AI here means Artificial Intelligence, software that performs tasks that once needed human judgment or cognition. UBI means Universal Basic Income, a recurring cash payment given without requiring a job. SaaS means Software as a Service, the subscription software many firms sell or use online. GDP means Gross Domestic Product, the total value of goods and services produced in an economy.

The trap is simple. Wages are costs to one company, but income to the wider market. A firm sees the first part clearly because it is printed in the accounts. It sees the second part dimly because the damage is scattered through thousands of future purchases. When one firm replaces a support worker, analyst, designer, tester, clerk, or junior coder, the saving belongs to that firm. The lost spending belongs to everyone.

Economists call this a demand externality. The phrase is dry, but the idea is not. The firm captures the benefit of reducing payroll while paying only a tiny share of the future demand it has weakened. The loss leaks outward. The dismissed worker buys less food, delays a repair, cancels a subscription, postpones a course, avoids a clinic, and turns a hundred small transactions into no transaction at all. No single business can point to the exact missing rupee and say, there, that was mine. So the market keeps moving as if nothing structural has happened.

Until enough firms do it together.

That is where the paper becomes interesting. It is not just saying that AI can take jobs. It is asking what happens when many firms automate tasks faster than the economy creates comparable income elsewhere. Workers lose pay. Their spending falls. Firms face softer demand. Firms cut more costs. AI makes further cutting possible. The machine gets better at production while society gets worse at buying.

At the extreme, you get a brilliant bakery with no shortage of bread and too few people with breakfast money.

AI attacks tasks before it attacks job titles. Draft this email. Summarize this call. Classify this ticket. Produce this report. Write this bit of code. Answer this customer. Prepare this deck. Fill this form. A job is a bundle of such tasks. Remove enough of them and the job begins to look like furniture after the useful pieces have been quietly taken away.

For one company, automation can be perfectly rational. Lower cost. Faster output. Better margin. Maybe lower prices. Maybe higher profit. If competitors are slower, the automated firm may win market share. No mystery there.

But when everyone automates, the advantage shrinks. Cost reductions spread through the industry. The market-share gain fades because every firm is chasing the same gain. What remains is the weaker purchasing power of the people whose wages have disappeared or fallen. Each company may have behaved sensibly alone. Together, they may have made the customer base poorer.

This is the automation version of a prisoner's dilemma. If every firm could coordinate and slow down displacement, the market might stay healthier. But the firm that restrains itself risks losing to the firm that cuts faster. So the private incentive remains aggressive automation even when the collective result may be foolish.

UBI appears in this discussion as a cushion, not a cure. It may protect people from the floor, and that matters. But it does not automatically change the firm's decision. The firm still asks whether a task can be done cheaper by a system. If the answer is yes, the incentive remains. UBI can preserve some spending, but it does not by itself stop excessive automation.

Upskilling has a similar limit when treated as magic. Training is useful when real jobs exist at the other end. Without those jobs, it can become a waiting room with certificates. Learning is still necessary. Learning is oxygen. But oxygen does not repair a broken staircase.

The paper also discusses an automation tax, a Pigouvian tax in the old economic sense: make the actor pay for the social cost it creates. In principle, if a firm destroys demand by automating a task, it should contribute toward the wider damage. In practice, the measurement becomes messy. What counts as an automated task? A chatbot replacing five agents? A reporting tool letting one manager replace analysts? A developer using AI to produce twice as much work? The company will not call these things "job destruction" if a gentler phrase is available. It will say productivity uplift, AI enablement, or workflow modernization, and someone will put it in a slide deck with calm colors.

Still, difficulty is not the same as falseness. The problem is real because the accounting is incomplete. A layoff is not only a firm-level efficiency event. At scale, layoffs can become demand cannibalization. A wage is not only a cost line. It is part of another firm's revenue line.

This is why the India angle is not decorative. India has treated education, English, coding, testing, support work, analytics, back-office processing, and global services as a ladder out of household panic. Not paradise. A ladder. Sometimes shaky, sometimes humiliating, but still a ladder. If AI compresses white-collar service work, that ladder gets crowded at the bottom and shortened at the top.

The person in Kolkata, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad, Manila, Warsaw, Nairobi, or Ohio is not living inside an economic model. He is living inside rent, debt, medical bills, school fees, aging relatives, electricity charges, and the fear of becoming obsolete before becoming secure.

"New jobs will come" is not enough. When? Where? At what wage? For whom? With what bargaining power? A new job paying half the old wage is not full replacement. A gig assignment is not a career. A training badge is not demand.

The paper does not prove collapse. It identifies a mechanism by which rational automation can become excessive. The empirical questions matter now. Are displaced workers finding comparable income quickly? Are AI-heavy firms expanding total demand or only reducing payroll? Are productivity gains becoming broad purchasing power or concentrated capital income? Are layoffs temporary correction after overhiring, or early permanent task substitution?

Those are adult questions. "Is AI good or bad?" is too small. AI is a tool, an industry, a capital project, a labor substitute, a productivity engine, a coordination problem, and a political problem at the same time. Asking whether it is simply good or bad is like asking whether electricity has manners.

The practical work is boring and therefore close to reality. Measure automation-linked displacement. Track income replacement, not only employment. Separate augmentation from role elimination. Stop letting firms hide job cuts behind pleasant language. Tie public training money to actual labor demand. Study whether AI adoption in one sector weakens demand in connected sectors. Consider taxation or insurance where displacement is measurable and broad.

None of this will be neat. Clean solutions belong to textbooks, pitch decks, and people who have never repaired anything after deployment.

The central point remains plain. A company can fire a worker. An economy, if it is careless enough, can fire its customers. The spreadsheet may look improved at midnight, but in the morning the market still has to buy breakfast.
