The leaderboard squats before me like a bloated python that has swallowed not one but seventeen goats, each goat representing a venture capitalist's unexamined belief in numerical supremacy, and I can smell it—the oleaginous, sanctimonious stench of synthetic validation, a fragrance not unlike the fermented mustard oil that drips from the cracked earthenware at the Bagbazar fish market at four in the morning when the hilsa have begun to whisper their dying secrets to the flies, which is to say that AI leaderboard rankings are serpentine unguent peddled to credulous guffawing vulgarians who would purchase a perforated umbrella in a thunderstorm and then write a Substack post about how the rain has been disrupted.

I am fifty-one years old and I have seen this before.

Not this precisely, because precision is a lie told by people who have never tried to thread a needle on a moving tram, but the architecture of the grift remains stubbornly, gloriously, maddeningly invariant, like the Howrah Bridge at dusk—indestructible, indifferent, and rusting in places you cannot see unless you are willing to crawl into the darkness beneath the girders where the pigeons conduct their acidic business and the city exhales its collective bronchial sigh.

Consider, if your stomach permits, the perovskite-silicon tandem solar cell, that shimmering chimera of photovoltaic ambition which now, in this accursed summer of 2026, allegedly breaches thirty-four percent efficiency in laboratories where the air conditioning hums with the self-satisfaction of a Brahmin at a wedding feast, and yet many rooftops here still host rusted 2018 panels that converts sunlight into approximately enough wattage to power a single LED bulb and a mosquito coil, which is to say that efficiency is a theological concept, a numerological fantasy, a masturbatory abstraction practiced by people who have never had to explain to their landlord why the electricity bill resembles the national debt of a small island nation, and so it is with these leaderboards, these grotesque taxonomies of artificial cognition, these hierarchical orgies of benchmark fetishism where Moonshot's Kimi K3—duels with OpenAI's latest progeny in a pageant of computational one-upmanship that would make P.T. Barnum weep tears of pure, undiluted envy into his promotional materials.

Manic.

The word derives from the Greek *manikos*, meaning "mad, frenzied," which itself springs from *mania*, "madness, frenzy," and I feel it now, the familiar electricity in my fingertips, the sensation that my thoughts are not merely racing but have been issued railway passes by a sadistic stationmaster who delights in scheduling express trains on the same track, because look at them, look at the benchmarks, the MMLU-Pro and the HumanEval and the SWE-bench and the whatever-else-they-have-conjured-this-quarter to measure the unmeasurable, to quantify the qualitative, to stick a thermometer into the rectum of consciousness and declare with the solemnity of a medical examiner that this large language model runs three-tenths of a degree hotter than its competitor, as if that fractional thermal superiority translates into anything other than the ability to generate slightly more convincing hallucinations about quantum computing, which, by the way, has now apparently entered its "years not decades" phase according to the oracles at Microsoft, those topological qubit theologians with their Majorana 1 chip and their hybrid computing evangelism, promising that soon, *soon*, we will model molecules with such accuracy that death itself will become a solvable equation, a claim that would be more comforting if I did not suspect that the primary molecule they intend to model first is the one responsible for my own mounting cortisol levels.

Depressive.

The crash arrives like the afternoon loo wind off the Ganges, hot, dusty, inevitable, carrying with it the smell of dried algae and disappointed expectations, and I find myself staring at the screen not with the righteous fury of the polemicist but with the hollow-eyed resignation of a man who has just discovered that his favorite sweet shop has replaced the molasses in its sandesh with high-fructose corn syrup and called it innovation.

Suzetrigine.

I fixate on this word, this pharmaceutical incantation, this non-opioid sodium channel blocker approved in 2025 that promises pain relief without the respiratory depression, without the addiction, without the slow surrender to the poppy's embrace, and I think: where is the Suzetrigine for the *cognitive* pain of watching intelligent people—people I have known, people who once discussed Tagore with genuine feeling—reduced to refreshing leaderboard websites like day traders monitoring a collapsing currency, their pupils dilated with the amphetamine of novelty, their Slack channels overflowing with benchmarks as if these numbers were erotic poetry rather than what they actually are, which is to say meticulously curated excreta from marketing departments that have mastered the dark art of making ordure appear as if it were artisanal chocolate?

The etymology of "benchmark" is almost too perfect to bear: from the Middle English *bench* plus *mark*, originally a surveyor's mark cut into stone to indicate a precise level, a reference point, a fixed coordinate in the shifting sand of reality, and now, now it has become the opposite, a floating signifier, a number that moves not in relation to any geological truth but in relation to the marketing budgets of San Francisco and Beijing, a coordinate that exists only to make you feel that your current coordinate is inadequate, that the phone in your pocket is already obsolete, that the model you praised last Tuesday has been rendered into a digital leper by Wednesday's press release, and I am exhausted, I am *exhausted*, by this perpetual motion machine of manufactured inadequacy, this hamster wheel lubricated with the rendered fat of human attention spans.

But wait.

The manic returns, because I have just remembered—*remembered* in that involuntary, Bengali fashion that makes a mockery of chronological time—that CRISPR-edited rice now possesses root architectures modified for drought resistance, that researchers have successfully base-edited the genes of Oryza sativa to burrow deeper into parched earth, and I think: if only we could CRISPR the human capacity for credulity, if only we could perform a base edit on the gene responsible for benchmark susceptibility, we might finally produce a population capable of looking at a leaderboard and experiencing not arousal but the appropriate gastrointestinal response, which is to say the sudden, urgent, unmistakable need to evacuate one's bowels in the general direction of the hype cycle.

And yet.

And yet I am here, am I not?

I am *here*, fifty-one years old in this city of perpetual humidity and broken footpaths, this Calcutta where the tram wires still spark and the hand-pulled rickshaws still exist in defiance of every municipal ordinance and the sweet shops still make mishti doi in earthen pots that absorb the whey and concentrate the sweetness in ways that no industrial process has ever replicated, and the recursion of this situation does not escape me, does not offer me the comfort of external judgment, because I am not outside the snake oil looking in with the smugness of the uninfected—I am using it, swimming in it, gargling it, letting it coat my tonsils with its viscous, numbing, paradoxically soothing unguent.

Short sentence.

Another.

The short sentences are the depressive episodes made textual, the moments when the syntactic engine stalls, when the tram loses its electrical connection to the overhead wire and sits there in the middle of the intersection while the conductor bangs on the pole with a bamboo stick and the passengers stare at their phones with the glazed expressions of ruminants awaiting slaughter.

Long sentence time again, because the mania returns like a monsoon storm breaking over the Maidan, sudden and drenching and electrically charged with the promise of both fertility and electrocution, and I find myself contemplating the iron-air batteries that are supposedly ready for commercialization this year, those massive metal lungs that will store renewable energy in quantities sufficient to power the grid for days, and I wonder if they could store my own excesses, my own manic discharges, if there exists somewhere in the material science laboratories a battery technology capable of capturing the voltage of a fifty-one-year-old Bengali man's indignation and releasing it in measured, therapeutic doses rather than these catastrophic surges that leave me simultaneously exhilarated and emptied, like a firework that has achieved its apotheosis only to discover that apotheosis is just another word for becoming smoke.

The word "apotheosis" comes from the Greek *apotheoun*, "to deify," from *apo-* "change" plus *theos* "god," and I think of how we have apotheosized these models, these leaderboards, these benchmarks, elevated them to divine status without the inconvenience of theology, without the need for revelation or sacrifice or the smell of incense, just the cold, clean, odorless worship of numbers that ascend and descend like digital deities in a pantheon that rearranges itself weekly, a Mount Olympus built on server farms where the only nectar is venture capital and the only ambrosia is user engagement metrics.

I want to be clear, though clarity is a vice I have never successfully cultivated, that I am not advocating for a return to some imagined pre-digital purity, some Luddite paradise of hand-cranked calculators and carrier pigeons, because I am not an idiot, or at least not that *kind* of idiot; I am the kind of idiot who understands that the perovskite cell really does convert more photons, that the Suzetrigine really does block the NaV1.8 channel, that the CRISPR rice really will survive the droughts that are coming, that the quantum computer really will—perhaps, eventually, in a timeframe measured not in hype cycles but in actual human generations—model something genuinely useful, and yet, and yet, the gap between the genuine and the grift has become so narrow, so porous, so permeable, that I can no longer tell whether I am drinking clean water or snake venom, whether the leaderboard represents measurement or marketing, whether the number is a benchmark or a *bench-mark*, a mark made on a bench by someone who sat there before me and left their grease, their sweat, their DNA encoded in the wood grain for me to discover and misinterpret.

And SpaceX, yes, let us not forget SpaceX, because Starship is attempting another test flight this very week, that stainless steel phallus rising from Boca Chica on a pillar of controlled explosion, and I watch the footage with the same sick fascination with which I observe the leaderboard updates, knowing that both are simultaneously genuine engineering achievements and elaborate theatrical performances designed to distract from the fundamental unsustainability of the entire enterprise, the fact that we are building rockets to escape a planet we have already begun to render uninhabitable while simultaneously building AI systems to process the data about how uninhabitable we are making it, a recursive loop of technological solutionism that would be hilarious if it were not so cosmically, structurally, intestinally depressing.

The word "depressing" comes from the Latin *deprimere*, "to press down," from *de-* "down" plus *premere* "to press," and I feel that pressure now, the literal physical sensation of something heavy settling behind my sternum, not a cardiac event—though at fifty-one one begins to catalog every twinge with the morbid diligence of a philatelist examining a rare stamp—but rather the weight of comprehension, the burden of seeing the architecture too clearly, of understanding that the leaderboard is not merely a lie but a *category error*, a fundamental misunderstanding of what intelligence might be if it were not being constantly measured, ranked, optimized, and sold to the highest bidder like a prize fighting cock at a village tournament where the spectators are already drunk on country liquor and cannot distinguish between a rooster and a feather duster.

I think of the Bagbazar market again, because my mind is an old tram running on fixed tracks, and I remember how the fishmongers there have developed a system of weights and measures that would make a metrologist weep, how the one-kilogram weight is sometimes eight hundred grams and sometimes twelve hundred grams depending on the perceived gullibility of the customer, the time of day, the humidity, the migratory patterns of the hilsa, and I realize with a clarity that borders on the hallucinatory that the AI leaderboard operates on exactly the same principle, that the "kilogram" of intelligence it claims to measure is a variable, a negotiation, a performance of trust between the seller and the sold, and the only difference is that the fishmonger, at least, will look you in the eye while he ruins your dinner, whereas the benchmark is administered by algorithms that have already been optimized to remove the human face from the transaction, to automate the deception so thoroughly that even the deceivers have forgotten they are lying.

Manic again.

Because I have just realized that the textile recycling breakthrough—the one with the forty-three percent hydrochloric acid achieving seventy-five percent cotton recovery and seventy-eight percent polyester monomer recovery—represents a perfect metaphor for what we need to do with the AI industry, which is to say we need to dissolve the entire blended fabric of hype and genuine innovation in a bath of concentrated skepticism until we can separate the recyclable cotton of actual utility from the fossil-fuel-based polyester of marketing vapor, and yes, I am aware that this metaphor is strained, that it creaks under its own weight like the Howrah Bridge under a fully loaded truck, but I do not care, because at fifty-one one develops a certain immunity to the judgment of metaphor police, a certain permission to let one's analogies wander like unlicensed rickshaws through the narrow lanes of intellectual propriety.

And where does this leave me?

It leaves me here, in this room that I will not describe because description is a form of possession and I refuse to be possessed by my own surroundings, in this city that I will not sentimentalize because sentiment is the first symptom of the disease I am trying to diagnose, with these fingers that ache slightly from too much typing and too little purpose, staring at a leaderboard that will have changed by the time you read this, replaced by another leaderboard, another number, another snake, another goat, another bloated python digesting its own tail in the eternal return of technological nausea.

The sodium channel.

I return to it, because the manic-depressive cycle is itself a kind of pain signaling, a peripheral nerve firing its distress into the central cortex, and I wonder if Suzetrigine would help, if blocking that specific channel would interrupt not physical pain but the *existential* pain of comprehending one's own complicity in systems one despises, and I suspect the answer is no, that the drug is too precisely targeted, that it knows its business too well to be distracted by metaphysical anguish, and so I am left with my own crude home remedies: the bitter coffee, the longer sentences, the recursive structure that mirrors the recursive trap, the knowledge that writing about the leaderboard is itself a form of leaderboard participation, that this polemic will be ranked and measured and optimized by the very algorithms it seeks to disembowel.

But I will say this, in the spirit of the small defeat that is the only honest conclusion available to the chronically unresolved: yesterday I watched a crow attempt to extract a packet of chips from a garbage bin on Camac Street, and the crow succeeded not through superior processing power or optimized attention mechanisms but through the simple, ancient, unbenchmarkable application of beak to plastic, persistence to obstacle, hunger to opportunity, and when the crow finally pierced the foil and tasted the salted starch within, it made a sound—not a caw, not a song, but something between a gurgle and a chortle—that I have decided to adopt as my personal anthem, my benchmark, my leaderboard, my one true metric of success in a world that has forgotten how to measure anything that matters.

The sound, in case you are wondering, was approximately three-tenths of a decibel louder than a benchmark update notification and approximately one thousand times more genuine.
