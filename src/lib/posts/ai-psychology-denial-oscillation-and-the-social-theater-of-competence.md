The draft on the screen had the suspicious smoothness of a freshly ironed shirt. Too smooth, perhaps. A sentence that should have limped arrived walking straight. A paragraph that should have required twenty minutes of irritation appeared after one prompt, carrying itself with the confidence of someone who had skipped the queue.

This is where denial begins.

People use AI to summarize, rephrase, outline, debug, translate, compare, search, brainstorm, and rescue the middle of a workday from its own stickiness. Then they say they barely use it. They say it is mostly hype. They say it is immature. They say it is only a toy. Sometimes the same people, in a different mood, speak of AI as if a new mind has opened its eyes inside the data center.

The pendulum is exhausting: miracle, parrot, miracle, parrot.

The real problem is less dramatic. AI has become socially awkward before it has become philosophically settled. It changes the visible shape of work, and that embarrasses our inherited ideas about skill, authorship, effort, and merit.

If competence once meant producing a polished artifact from inside one's own head, then AI assistance feels like an accusation. Even when the worker supplies judgment, taste, correction, context, selection, and responsibility, the machine's involvement can feel like borrowed mind. So the user performs distance. He says it was only for grammar. She says it was just an outline. Another says the tool is overrated while quietly asking it to untangle tomorrow's task.

This is not only hypocrisy. It is status management.

AI disturbs the signal. For generations, polished language and quick synthesis indicated expensive training, reading, practice, and memory. Now some of those visible signs can be generated cheaply. That does not make the output fake. It means the old outward signs no longer tell the whole story.

We need three separate questions.

What can the system do?

What does it feel like to use it?

What does using it signal socially?

Capability is practical. A modern language model can transform text, propose code, compress documents, generate structure, compare alternatives, and imitate many forms of professional speech. It is uneven, sometimes wrong, and often overconfident. But unevenness is not uselessness. Many important tools begin life as erratic nuisances.

Experience is stranger. To the working user, AI often feels like collaboration. You offer half a thought and receive a usable shape. The system does not understand in the full human sense, with body, memory, responsibility, and consequence. But the user experiences help. That feeling matters because it changes habits.

Social meaning is the volatile part. In many offices, appearing self-sufficient still carries prestige. AI use can feel like admitting weakness, laziness, or replaceability. So people invent private thresholds. If the machine wrote the whole thing, that counts. If it suggested titles, cleaned prose, explained a bug, compared options, or made a first plan, perhaps it does not count. Convenient.

The denial is not harmless. Hidden use prevents honest governance. If organizations design policy around declared purity, they will govern a fictional workplace. People will keep using AI in the shadows, and the worst combination will form: dependence without traceability.

The opposite melodrama is not better. Calling AI alive because it writes fluidly mistakes the surface of thought for the conditions of thought. A trail of elegant outputs does not prove inner life. But calling it a mere parrot can also be a defense mechanism, a way of preserving human superiority without examining operational consequence.

A thing need not be a person to reorganize a profession.

The useful posture is disciplined realism. Treat AI neither as a colleague nor as a calculator. It behaves like neither consistently enough. Treat it as a probabilistic cognitive subsystem that can help with generation, transformation, and exploration, but that must be bounded by validation, provenance, task design, and human accountability.

Separate generation from authorization. Let the system draft, summarize, and propose. Do not let it silently finalize work that carries real consequence. Make AI involvement visible where it matters. Redesign competence around judgment over output, not theatrical refusal of assistance. Reward the person who knows where the model is useful, where it is weak, what context it lacks, when it is flattering, and when to throw the answer away.

That last skill will matter.

The worker who refuses every tool may become slow without becoming deep. The worker who accepts every answer may become fast without becoming competent. The future belongs to neither purity nor surrender. It belongs to people who can direct the machine without hiding behind it.

Outside the window, Calcutta continues its ordinary instruction: everything is mediated. Water arrives through pipes, work through networks, money through systems, news through screens, language through other people's language. AI has not invented dependency. It has made one kind of dependency conversational.

That is why the denial feels so charged. The machine did not merely enter the workplace. It entered the mirror.
