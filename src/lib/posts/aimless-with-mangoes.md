The mango has gone soft near the stem.

That is how decline announces itself in Bengal. Not with an orchestra, not with collapse, but with a small brown truth in one corner. The fruit is still sweet. It is also past the stage at which it can pretend to be firm. I recognize the condition.

Aimlessness sounds like a failure when one is young.

At twenty, every life is expected to point somewhere. Career, country, money, reputation, love of work, visible progress, a target shining at the end of the lane. The arrow matters. Even if the arrow is foolish, it gives the day a flattering geometry.

By middle age, some arrows have snapped quietly.

Not all at once. Some were lost in work. Some in returning. Some in markets that changed shape. Some in the slow discovery that ambition often requires not only talent, but timing, sponsorship, appetite, health, stamina, packaging, and a willingness to keep smiling at rooms that smell faintly of performance.

Without the arrow, the day becomes suspiciously open.

This is not freedom in the expensive sense. There are still bills. The body still sends notices. The city still insists on heat, noise, repair, delay, and the little indignities of ordinary survival. Aimlessness with money is leisure. Aimlessness without enough money is a room where the walls ask questions.

Still, there is a small relief inside it.

If the target was never truly yours, losing it reduces the fraud. One no longer has to pretend every hour is part of a grand trajectory. One can eat a mango over the sink because plates create additional bureaucracy. One can admit that some days are not chapters. They are only days.

The mind resists this.

It has been trained to turn life into project management. Morning must have purpose. Work must have outcome. Reading must have use. Writing must build audience. Rest must optimize recovery. Even aimlessness gets assigned a productivity framework by people who cannot leave a human being unmonetized for five minutes.

But a mango does not need a mission statement.

It ripens, softens, sweetens, darkens, and eventually becomes unsuitable. Its dignity is not in efficiency. It is in being eaten before the small brown truth takes over. That seems like a limited philosophy, but many better-looking philosophies have done worse.

Calcutta understands partial pleasures.

A working fan for one hour. A decent fruit in summer. A payment that clears. A sentence that lands. A brief interval when the mind stops drafting accusations. These are not victories. They are provisions.

Perhaps aimlessness is frightening because it removes the excuse of future greatness.

If no great target remains, then one has to notice the present without using it as a runway. The sink. The fruit. The knife. The slightly sticky wrist. The sound of a pump somewhere stopping after too much effort. The day not redeemed, but not entirely wasted either.

The mango is too ripe now.

Still sweet.

I eat it standing up, and the future waits without instructions.
