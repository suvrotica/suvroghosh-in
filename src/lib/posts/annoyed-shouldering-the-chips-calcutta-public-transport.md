A Calcutta minibus in summer arrives with the sound of metal remembering every bad road it has survived.

It does not stop exactly. Stopping would imply agreement between machine, driver, road, passenger, and fate. The bus merely hesitates near the edge of the crowd, and in that hesitation a citizen must make several decisions at once: whether the foot can reach the step, whether the hand can find the rail, whether the bag will follow, whether dignity should be abandoned before boarding or afterward.

Then, somehow, you are inside.

Inside is too generous a word. You are included. A schoolbag presses into your ribs. A steel rod finds your shoulder. A man near the window guards his small square of air with the seriousness of a border official. The conductor hangs by the door and recites the route like a priest of combustion, except with less mercy and better lung capacity.

Calcutta public transport teaches philosophy without asking permission.

It teaches that space is negotiable. It teaches that patience is often only exhaustion with a polite face. It teaches that the middle class will complain about disorder while participating in it with great technical skill. It teaches that a person can be annoyed, comic, ashamed, tender, and faintly heroic within the same three stops.

The heat does the rest. Your shirt becomes evidence. Your back develops weather. The windows offer air only as rumor. Diesel, sweat, old vinyl, damp currency, and hot iron combine into a civic perfume for which no honest manufacturer would accept responsibility.

And still the bus moves.

This is the strange part. The thing works badly, but it works. A man gets down while another climbs in. Someone passes coins over three shoulders and the right ticket returns through the same human machinery. The conductor calculates fares faster than many software systems I have seen. An older passenger is given half a seat, then three-quarters of a seat, then a form of public sympathy that is not comfortable but is not nothing.

The driver, meanwhile, treats physics as a negotiable framework. He accelerates with suspicion, brakes with personal feeling, and steers around potholes by locating other potholes with stronger claims on the road. At each turn the passengers are rearranged into fresh alliances. By Rashbehari or Gariahat or Jadavpur, everyone has briefly learned the weight, laundry soap, and moral weather of everyone nearby.

This should be unbearable.

Often it is.

But a city shows itself truthfully in its public transport. Not in speeches. Not in glossy drone shots. Not in curated nostalgia. Watch the bus in the morning. Watch who pretends not to see. Watch who moves a little without being asked. Watch who protects a lunch carrier as if it contains state secrets. Watch who counts coins twice because two rupees still matter. Watch who stands through heat because sitting has become someone else's emergency.

That is Calcutta: irritable, improvised, tender for three seconds, then irritable again.

The real chip on the shoulder is not only the crowd or the heat. It is the knowledge that ordinary people have been trained to treat discomfort as civic destiny. If you complain, someone will call you soft. If you demand better buses, better stops, better sidewalks, better scheduling, better air, someone will explain that this is India and you must adjust. Adjustment has become our most successful public policy.

We adjust to broken roads.

We adjust to dangerous boarding.

We adjust to heat that enters the body like a second tax.

We adjust to being transported as if the city has confused passengers with cargo and cargo with philosophy.

The educated middle class is especially skilled at this. We mutter, calculate, squeeze through, protect the wallet, roll the eyes, and later discuss civilization over tea as if civilization were a distant project under review by another department. We want systems, but we also want someone else to be the first inconvenient citizen. So the bus keeps coming, the conductor keeps shouting, the passenger keeps leaping, and the complaint becomes another folded ticket in the pocket.

Still, I would be lying if I said there is no affection in the irritation.

A sudden breeze enters through a cracked window. A conductor returns exact change when he could have pretended to forget. A stranger says, "Dada, come forward," not warmly, not beautifully, but practically. For a moment the crowd becomes a temporary organism with rough manners and useful instincts. Then the driver brakes, the organism collapses into elbows, and affection loses balance.

That is the city's method. It gives a small mercy and immediately tests it.

By the time I get down, shirt defeated, shoulder sore, face arranged into the mature scowl of a Bengali man who has read enough and earned too little, I am not sure whether I have traveled or been processed. The bus pulls away before I have fully reached the pavement. Dust rises. Someone else runs toward it with faith, fear, and incorrect footwear.

Calcutta moves.

So do I.

Neither of us looks graceful doing it.
