The chair was there, but it was not offered. That is one of the small instruments by which hierarchy explains itself in India. Nobody shouts. Nobody needs to. The room simply adjusts its temperature around your usefulness.

When I returned from the United States in 2014, and when the startup life that followed thinned out by 2016, I learned again how quickly status can evaporate. The same person can enter the same country with the same education, same memory, same habits, and a very different social weight. A voice changes. A clerk pauses longer. A phone call is not returned. The old Indian scanner runs silently: job, money, English, address, contact, surname, shoes, usefulness.

Then it assigns posture.

Babu culture is not only government bureaucracy. The old file-guarding official remains a classic figure, but the habit has escaped the office. It sits in housing societies, schools, banks, private companies, clubs, clinics, party offices, contractors' desks, and every little gate where one person can delay another.

Power in India is often the power to pause.

"Come next week." "Sir is busy." "One more signature." "Small issue." These phrases look harmless from outside. Inside ordinary life they can become toll gates. A document, a payment, an approval, a bed, a transfer, a certificate, a repair, an admission, a file movement: each can depend on someone whose formal authority is small but practical authority is large.

Around that authority grows flattery.

Not because Indians are uniquely weak. That would be a lazy insult. People bend because bending works. When rules are uncertain and discretion is strong, dignity becomes expensive. The poor bend because survival is immediate. The middle class bends because delay can ruin a month. The rich often arrange for someone else to bend on their behalf.

Obsequiousness becomes an economy.

The VIP disease is the loudest version. A republic may speak of equal citizenship, but the corridor asks a different question: who sent you? That one sentence explains much of practical India. The queue exists, but the side door has better plumbing. The law exists, but the phone call has better legs.

The tragedy is that many people do not want fairness as much as they want access to unfairness. I include myself in that uncomfortable accusation. Everyone hates the queue-jumper until a relative says he knows someone. Then the same corruption is renamed help.

Language washes habits clean.

Respect becomes crawling. Network becomes patronage. Adjustment becomes surrender with better manners. Practicality becomes moral fatigue. And soon the institution cannot hear truth because every sentence has been bent before reaching power.

This is why flattery is not merely embarrassing. It is an information problem. A leader surrounded by praise loses the map. A boss who hears only agreement becomes dangerous. A clerk who cannot be questioned becomes a little state. Bad news must travel upward fast in a healthy system. In a sick one, bad news removes its shoes outside the door.

Calcutta adds its own costume. Here hierarchy may quote literature, hold meetings, and speak in cultured phrases while making someone weaker arrange the chairs. A committee can discuss equality for an hour and still treat the lowest-paid person like background furniture. Bengal did not abolish hierarchy. It taught hierarchy to speak gently.

The repair is not dramatic. Make queues visible. Publish timelines. Track files. Require written reasons. Reduce discretion. Protect people who report obstruction. Reward the junior person who brings bad news early. Teach children courtesy, but not crawling.

Most difficult of all, stop enjoying being flattered.

The babu is not only behind the desk. He is also inside us, waking when someone calls us sir with extra sugar. A republic is not built when everyone receives a flag. It is built when an ordinary person can enter an ordinary office and leave with the work done and the spine intact.

That sounds modest. In India, modest things can be revolutionary.
