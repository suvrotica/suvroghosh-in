The bucket makes a hollow sound when there is not enough water in it. It is a small sound, but a serious one. A city can hide many failures behind coffee shops, glass offices, and airport roads, but an empty bucket has no public relations department.

Bangalore has become one of the great Indian jokes about modernity: a technology capital that alternates between flooding and thirst. When it rains hard, roads become channels, basements surrender, and expensive neighborhoods remember that water follows gravity rather than brochure copy. When rain vanishes, tankers become royalty and every household begins calculating dignity by the liter.

This is not merely bad luck. Flood and scarcity are siblings in a badly planned city.

Erase wetlands. Narrow drains. Pave recharge zones. Overbuild beyond infrastructure. Depend on borewells as if groundwater were a private inheritance. Let tanker economies grow around municipal weakness. Then act surprised when water attacks in one season and abandons in another.

The city sells intelligence, but water exposes representation failure. Land has been represented as real estate, lakes as obstacles, drains as leftover space, and infrastructure as the boring thing to be discussed after valuation. Once a false model enters maps, approvals, loans, layouts, and political speech, the physical city begins obeying the lie.

The body finds out first.

A home is not fully modern if the tap is uncertain. A workplace is not truly world-class if the worker must attend a video meeting while rationing household water in his head. A million-dollar apartment depending on tanker supply is not a triumph of urban development. It is a luxury product standing on borrowed plumbing.

Sanitation makes the comedy sharper because it drags civilization down to its most honest scale. A person needs clean water, a working toilet, drainage that does not reverse, and a city that understands waste as an engineering problem rather than an embarrassment to be hidden behind walls. Without that, all the language of smart cities begins to smell faintly theatrical.

Bangalore is not alone. Calcutta has its own curriculum in drains, dampness, and pipe biography. Indian cities everywhere produce islands of competence floating in local dysfunction. Inside the campus: cloud migration, machine learning, biometric access, polished dashboards. Outside the gate: broken footpaths, improvised pipes, tanker numbers, and a resident welfare group arguing like a small constitutional crisis.

The problem is not that people waste water, though waste matters. The deeper problem is that individual guilt cannot repair destroyed recharge systems, leaking distribution, captured utilities, weak enforcement, and development that treats maintenance as an insult to ambition.

Maintenance has no garland moment. That is why we neglect it.

India likes inauguration. Operation is less glamorous. A drain working quietly for twenty years is civilization. A ribbon-cutting lasts one afternoon and photographs better.

The class map is always present. The rich outsource scarcity. The middle class negotiates with it. The poor carry it. The rich buy filters, pumps, tanks, tanker water, silence. The middle class writes angry messages, develops bladder discipline, and blames whichever group is easiest that week. The poor wait, walk, ration, fall ill, and become visible mainly when elections or outbreaks require them.

No serious city can build its future on this arrangement.

Bangalore does not need another speech about innovation before it needs honest hydrology, protected lakes, functioning drains, groundwater governance, wastewater reuse, floodplain discipline, and public data that cannot be massaged into comfort. A city that cannot move water correctly cannot hide behind software forever.

The bucket knocks again against the bathroom floor. Empty things have a way of becoming eloquent.
