A lit kitchen looks generous from another building.

That is the trick. From a distance, ordinary domestic life becomes almost ceremonial. A figure moving near the stove. A plate placed on a table. A television flickering blue against a wall. Someone calling from one room to another. Nothing grand. Nothing worth photographing. Yet through the window of one's own solitary room, that ordinariness can look like a country from which one has misplaced the passport.

Bengali normalcy is not as soft as nostalgia makes it.

Come closer and the warm house reveals its cracks: arguments, expenses, controlling habits, old resentments, unpaid maintenance, relatives with opinions, pressure cookers performing urgency, and the eternal politics of who did not help enough. A tea stall also looks romantic from across the road. Up close, the bench is cracked and the biscuit has witnesses.

Still, the tea is hot.

That is the problem. Ordinary household warmth may be compromised, noisy, unfair, and overmanaged, but it is warmth. A person living alone in middle age can become embarrassingly susceptible to it. Not in the sentimental way. More like a cold hand held near someone else's stove.

Screens have made this easier and stranger.

Other people display pieces of their domestic weather: food, rooms, festivals, errands, family jokes, little arguments, a child in the background, a plate of something fried. Much of it is staged. Some of it is dull. Yet even dullness has heat. It says: here are people who are expected somewhere within their own evening.

The solitary viewer borrows that expectation.

Not fully. One cannot eat from another person's plate through a screen. One cannot enter the room, sit badly in a corner, and be scolded for blocking the cupboard. But for a few minutes, the vacuum shifts. A household elsewhere becomes evidence that ordinary continuity still exists.

Calcutta is full of such borrowed warmth.

Balconies, windows, lanes, para gossip, evening lights, voices from behind curtains. The city does not understand privacy in the modern clean sense. It leaks lives into other lives. This is intrusive when one wants silence and merciful when silence has become too complete.

There is no neat conclusion here.

Household life is not salvation. Solitude is not always tragedy. Privacy can protect a person from the suffocating kindness of others. Company can bruise as easily as it comforts. The binary is false, as most binaries become false after they have paid rent for a few years.

What remains is the small fact of warmth.

A lit kitchen. A sound from another flat. Someone's ordinary meal. The knowledge that not every evening has to justify itself through achievement. Some evenings are only inhabited.

I borrow that for a while.

Not happiness.

Let us not be greedy.

Bearable is already a form of shelter.
