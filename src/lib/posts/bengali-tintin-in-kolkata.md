The garbage heap rises beside the lane with the confidence of an elected official. Banana peels, plastic packets, old flowers, fish scales, wet paper, paan stains, and civic resignation sit together in a damp republic. The smell does not request permission. It assumes jurisdiction.

Into this republic I have always wanted to drop Tintin.

Not the museum version of Calcutta, with tram bells polished by nostalgia and old men saying large things over small cups of tea. I mean the functioning city: cracked pavements, unfinished repairs, posters layered over older posters, water collected in places where water was not invited, street food frying beside public doubt, and politics leaning on every wall with the expression of a man who knows the file will never be found.

Tintin, that tidy Belgian boy-reporter with the upright hair and moral posture of a white envelope, would not know where to begin. His adventures usually require clues. Calcutta begins with smell.

In my version, he has become faintly Bengali by the second afternoon. The quiff remains, because some symbols have more stamina than reason, but his shirt has surrendered to humidity. His investigative urgency has been weakened by rice, mustard oil, and the heavy philosophical question of whether one should solve a mystery before or after a nap.

His little white dog adapts faster. This is believable. The dog understands fish, shade, and local opportunity. By evening, he is already trotting through the lane with the air of someone who has studied the supply chain and found a loophole.

The city, meanwhile, refuses to arrange itself like a clean adventure. No villain announces a plan. No neat map explains the route. The mystery is everywhere and therefore nowhere. Why is the drain blocked? Who approved the building? Why is the road cut open again? Why is the loudspeaker louder than the law? Why does the garbage heap keep returning after every official promise of removal, like a public servant with tenure?

In a European adventure, danger usually has a face. In Calcutta, danger often has a committee.

The beautiful thing about bringing a tidy hero here is that the city immediately defeats tidiness. Evidence is wet. Witnesses are busy. Everyone has an opinion and nobody has time. A tea seller gives the crucial clue but also overcharges by two rupees, which complicates the moral atmosphere. A local leader arrives with folded arms and says nothing can be done until the proper process is followed. The proper process has gone to lunch.

Tintin takes notes. The ink runs.

This is not hatred of the city. Hatred is too simple and too clean. Calcutta deserves something more accurate: irritation, loyalty, embarrassment, appetite, suspicion, and the old affection one feels for a relative who has once again stored electrical wires in a biscuit tin.

The city is not a villain. It is a mood with drainage problems.

That is why the adventure cannot proceed in the usual way. The garbage heap is not a clue leading to a secret. It is the secret. The bombast, the neglect, the improvisation, the feast beside decay, the sacred beside the soiled, the argument beside the tea stall, the heroic speech beside the leaking pipe: all of it is already the plot.

Tintin would try to solve Calcutta and discover, slowly, that Calcutta does not want to be solved in a final panel. It wants to be managed, survived, complained about, loved badly, repaired locally, neglected officially, and remembered with unreasonable tenderness once one has left the lane.

By the end of the day, our Bengali Tintin stands beside the heap, hair still brave, shirt defeated, notebook damp, shoes suspicious. The dog has found dinner. The city has found another way to continue.

The clue was never hidden.

It was fermenting in public.
