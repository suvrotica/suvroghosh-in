The blister is small, but the foot changes its whole manner of walking.

That is the strange thing about private pain. It may be invisible to the room and still reorganize the body. You stand differently. You speak less. You choose the chair nearest the wall. You avoid long explanations because every explanation sounds either too dramatic or too small. In Calcutta, where people are never fully alone and yet loneliness can sit beside you like unpaid rent, this becomes a very ordinary form of exhaustion.

The worst part is not always sadness.

Sometimes the worst part is the commentary. A hard little clerk inside the head keeps stamping the same paper: insufficient, foolish, late, useless, ridiculous. It does not offer an argument. It offers a verdict. The day has barely begun and already the court is in session.

I do not trust that court.

Its confidence is too cheap. It speaks with the authority of a municipal notice pasted badly on a damp wall. It says everything is final because it has no talent for nuance. It takes a missed call, a slow morning, a neglected task, a bad night's sleep, and turns them into a national policy of self-contempt.

The body gets dragged into it.

The chest tightens. The stomach becomes overinformed. The skin feels incorrectly attached. The room looks slightly too bright and also not bright enough. Messages arrive and become obligations. Silence arrives and becomes proof. You want company and cannot bear performance. You want rest and cannot stop thinking. You want the mind to sit down, and it keeps pacing.

There is a particular humiliation in being tired without looking wounded.

A visible injury receives practical treatment. A quiet internal abrasion asks you to continue as if nothing is happening. Pay the bill. Answer the message. Eat something. Fold the towel. Do not become an event. The world rewards manageability, and so one learns to become manageable in public while unraveling in private increments.

The friendless blister is not only the absence of friends. It is the absence of an easy witness.

Someone may love you and still not know the exact shape of the hour. Someone may ask, "What happened?" and the honest answer may be: nothing, which is also not true. Nothing happened, but the machinery inside the room changed. The air thickened. The old accusation woke up. The day became a staircase with each step slightly wrong.

I have learned not to build philosophy from those hours.

They are weather, not scripture. They may feel total, but feeling total is one of their tricks. A morning can issue a verdict that the afternoon quietly ignores. A thought can sound like truth because it arrived first. A body can report danger when the actual task is laundry, tea, and waiting.

This is not a cure. I dislike easy cures.

It is only a small practical refusal. Do not let the harshest hour write the biography. Do not let the blister decide the map. Move slowly if necessary. Answer one message. Drink water. Open the window. Let the tram bell, the traffic, the vendor's call, the ordinary city, enter as evidence that the court inside the head is not the only institution still operating.

The blister remains.

But the foot, stubbornly and without elegance, keeps finding the floor.
