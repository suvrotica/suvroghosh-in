The phone screen lights up in a dark Calcutta room before the tube light does. A headline appears first, then a face, then a chart with no source, then a video in which someone explains the collapse of civilization in a voice normally used to sell kitchen appliances. The ceiling fan moves hot air from one corner to another. The internet, meanwhile, moves certainty.

That is the first thing to notice.

The internet did not become foolish because machines became clever. It became foolish because too many people were trained to pass examinations without being trained to inspect claims. A person can hold a degree, know how to format a resume, speak in fluent professional English, and still be defenseless before a smooth paragraph that begins with the phrase "studies show." The trouble is not lack of information. The trouble is lack of digestion.

AI means artificial intelligence: software that can generate or analyze text, images, sound, code, and decisions by learning patterns from data. SEO means search engine optimization: the old craft of helping pages become easier for search engines to discover and rank. GDP means gross domestic product: the usual national measure of economic output, often treated as if it can measure the health of a civilization by itself.

These definitions matter because many modern lies wear acronyms like badges. The lie does not arrive with a torn shirt anymore. It arrives formatted, footnoted, optimized, and polite.

Bogosity used to sweat. It took effort to produce a fake pamphlet, a bad poster, a staged photograph, or a long conspiracy message. Someone had to sit down and do the work. Now the work can be automated. AI can produce one thousand plausible paragraphs before the tea cools. It can give a rumor the tone of a report, a prejudice the shape of analysis, and a sales pitch the voice of patient expertise.

The danger is not only that falsehoods will multiply. Falsehoods have always multiplied. The nearer danger is that rubbish will become elegant.

A clumsy lie alerts the mind. A fluent lie relaxes it.

That is why education matters. We taught many children to underline answers, not interrogate claims. We taught them to respect authority, which has its uses, but forgot to teach them that authority can be rented. A verified icon, a famous face, a dramatic soundtrack, a confident presenter, a screenshot of a logo, a paragraph beginning with "experts say" - these are not evidence. They are props.

The old web had many faults. It was slow, ugly, badly organized, and often written by people with mysterious fonts and no sense of spacing. But it still had the mood of a library with open windows. You searched. You compared. You read one page, then another. You could still get lost, but getting lost was part of the education. The feed changed that.

The feed does not ask whether something is true. It asks whether something will keep you staring.

That small change damaged public reason. Truth is slow. Anger is fast. Doubt takes effort. Outrage arrives already cooked. Social media learned this quickly. It discovered that people share what flatters them, frightens them, or gives shape to a resentment they already carry. A conspiracy theory is not merely a false explanation. It can also be a little chair on which a wounded ego sits and feels taller.

Correction then becomes difficult. You bring facts. The other person brings identity. You bring sources. He brings humiliation. You bring patience. He brings twenty-seven forwarded videos and a cousin who knows the real story.

The comedy is obvious. The danger is deeper.

A society that cannot tell evidence from decoration becomes easy to rule, sell to, and inflame. It buys fake cures, fake history, fake patriotism, fake expertise, fake outrage, fake intimacy, fake financial wisdom, and fake rebellion. It becomes a country of customers arguing with other customers while the platform counts the coins.

This is where enshittification enters. Cory Doctorow uses the word for the pattern by which platforms begin by serving users, then advertisers or business customers, and finally themselves. In plain language, the shop opens with good tea. Then the tea becomes weaker. Then the chairs become uncomfortable. Then shade costs extra. Then the owner sells your conversation to a man outside who wants to sell you a miracle belt.

By the time you complain, everyone you know is still sitting there.

That is the trap. Networked rot is sticky. People do not stay on bad platforms because they love them. They stay because their friends, work, memories, audience, and small economies of attention are already there. Leaving feels less like closing an app and more like moving house during a humid afternoon when the lift is broken.

AI will not replace this trap. It will furnish it.

The web is already filling with synthetic mush: pages written for ranking rather than understanding, images made for clicks rather than memory, comments generated to shape opinion, reviews that sound translated from sincerity into plastic, videos narrated by voices that are almost human enough to be trusted and not human enough to be ashamed.

Soon the question will not be only, "Is this information online?"

It will be, "Did a human mean this?"

After that, the question becomes harsher: "Does meaning matter if the machine can keep me looking?"

The answer should be yes. But the answer will need institutions, habits, and training. Attention is natural. Judgment is cultivated. Attention is what pulls the eye toward the glittering thing. Judgment is what asks who placed it there, who profits if I touch it, what is missing, and what would change my mind.

That last question is almost a civic technology by itself.

The internet attacks judgment by exhausting attention. Scroll long enough and everything becomes level: war, recipe, scandal, product review, flood, joke, rumor, advertisement, poem, political speech, celebrity quarrel, fake medical claim, tutorial, apology video, and another person explaining why everyone else is asleep. The mind cannot keep posture through that parade. It slumps.

Then the feed offers the next item.

Always one more.

The practical answer is smaller than the problem, but small answers are what we actually have. Teach children that a claim is not true because it is written well. Teach adults that anger is not evidence. Teach users that AI is a tool, not a priest, judge, witness, professor, or uncle. It can be useful, wrong, brilliant, shallow, and confidently lost in the same afternoon.

Ask simple questions. Who made this? Why now? What is missing? Who profits? Where is the primary source? What would prove this wrong?

Those questions sound dry. They are not. They are drainage.

Information systems are now part of public life. If a drain overflows, the neighborhood suffers. Nobody says drainage is a private matter between the pipe and its investors. We inspect it. We demand maintenance. We punish negligence when we can. Information drains also need maintenance: transparency, researcher access, limits on manipulation, real appeal rights, and public pressure.

That is risky, because governments can misuse the language of misinformation to protect themselves. A state can call criticism falsehood and call propaganda national interest. So the cure must be watched as carefully as the disease. Oversight without accountability simply becomes another machine.

There is no clean ending here. The old library will not return unchanged. The casino has opened inside the reading room. The shelves are still there, but the path to them is lined with glowing screens, sponsored urgency, machine-written confidence, and people shouting into cameras as if volume were a method.

Still, the library is not gone.

Inside the noise there are teachers, archives, manuals, lectures, data, code, old photographs, careful explainers, public documents, and quiet people who know what they are talking about. The treasure remains. The path is covered with glittering garbage.

A better education would teach people to walk through that garbage without eating it.

A better internet would stop rewarding those who dump it there.

A better AI culture would remember that fluency is not truth, speed is not wisdom, and scale is not civilization.

Until then, the phone will keep glowing in the hot room, offering another perfect sentence from nowhere. The pause before touching it is still a small form of freedom.
