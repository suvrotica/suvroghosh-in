The white light above the bed is too bright for mercy.

Some days the room stops being a room and becomes an accusation. Not a grand accusation with judges, polished wood, and historical speeches. Mine is cheaper. A hard bed. A plastic chair. A cup left too long on the table. The fan circling overhead like a clerk who has accepted defeat but not retirement. The walls closing in not dramatically, but administratively, as if they have received a memo.

And there I am.

Fifty-one. Single. Bengali. Lower-middle-class. Living on the damp edge of Calcutta, where the city seems to have come out in its old vest and forgotten to shave. Educated enough to know the names of systems. Poor enough to negotiate with the day rupee by rupee. Experienced enough to have spent years around American healthcare information systems, where data travels through wires wearing clean shoes while many lives outside the system limp along barefoot.

Now I sit here, trying to earn through consulting, month by month, invoice by invoice, like a man collecting rainwater in a cracked cup.

On difficult days I feel tied to something, though there is no rope. No guard. No locked door. The restraint is inside the skull, which is the most economical prison ever invented. No rent, no maintenance, open all hours.

The light burns above me.

It is not merely a light. It becomes a white question. What have you done? Where did the years go? Why does a small demand feel like a tiger entering the room wearing office shoes?

A sturdier fellow may laugh. Let him. The world is full of sturdy fellows, produced in bulk like plastic buckets. They say "just go out," "just take a chance," "just be positive," as if the mind were a switch near the door.

On. Off. Done.

The mind is not a switch. Some minds are more like old neighborhood transformers after a storm. They hum. They work, mostly. Then one wet leaf, one loose wire, one tiny accident, and the whole lane goes dark.

Fear is not always philosophical. Sometimes it is physical. It tightens the jaw. It sits in the stomach. It makes a simple errand feel like crossing a border without papers. It makes the future arrive not as tomorrow, but as a police jeep at midnight.

Then, for comic relief, I look at the news.

This is a mistake.

The news is no longer news. It is a traveling stage show that has lost proportion and kept the lights. Men with polished faces discuss destruction as if ordering snacks. Countries threaten each other with bureaucratic calm. Heat rises. Prices rise. Lies rise first, usually in business class. Some billionaire announces the future. Some politician announces the past. Some ordinary person is told to adjust, endure, produce documents, and smile.

At this point a small suspicion enters the room in rubber slippers.

What if I am not the unreasonable one?

What if the room looks like a cell because the world outside has become a larger cell with better lighting and advertising?

I do not say this proudly. There is no medal here. I know the mind exaggerates. I know a lonely afternoon can put a magnifying glass over everything. I know a frightened mood can behave like a dishonest accountant, taking one unpaid bill, one awkward memory, and one cloudy afternoon, then producing a full financial report proving that life itself is bankrupt.

Still.

Look outside.

A fragile man can still correctly observe that a building is on fire.

This is the terrible comedy. Inside, I am afraid of the world. Outside, the world keeps making a strong case for being frightening. My room becomes a prison. Then it becomes a bunker. Then a prison again. Then, on a better afternoon after tea, it becomes only a room.

That is relief sometimes.

Not triumph.

Not birdsong.

Just the room becoming a room again.

A plastic chair. A cup. A phone charger. A shirt drying on the back of a chair because there is never enough space. A packet of biscuits going soft in the humidity. The distant honk of traffic. The pressure cooker next door giving three whistles like a small election result. Calcutta continues: damp, loud, hungry, impossible.

And I continue inside it.

This is not a motivational sentence. Please do not put it on a wellness poster. I continue because what else is there? Rice must be bought. Rent must be handled. The electricity bill does not respect interior weather. The body asks for food even when the mind is busy filing appeals against existence.

Middle age is rude that way. Youth allowed one to be dramatic with some style. At twenty-five, despair had hair. At fifty-one, it has acidity, reading glasses, and a suspicious knee.

The thing I fear most now is not hardship alone. It is the bad, unfair, unfamiliar situation.

Bad means painful. Unfair means reason will not help. Unfamiliar means you do not know where the exits are. Put those three together and the mind begins to behave like a trapped thing.

So I shrink the world.

This is dangerous. A life can shrink so politely that you do not notice the theft. First one invitation. Then one meeting. Then one phone call. Then one opportunity. Then the road outside becomes a country. Then the country becomes a planet. Then the room becomes the last safe geography.

But even a shrunken life has windows.

Books are windows. Writing is a window. Tea is a window. A line of absurd humor is a window. The memory of walking under a large sky in San Antonio is a window. The knowledge that I have repaired systems, understood systems, survived systems, and occasionally outwitted systems is a window.

The room lies.

The news lies.

The mind lies.

But not all the time.

That is the crack through which air enters.

There are moments when I can say: this is a thought, not a verdict. This is fear, not prophecy. This is the old machinery grinding, not the universe announcing a final decision.

And there are moments when I cannot say any of that. Then I do smaller things. I wash a cup. I open the window. I make tea. I read one paragraph. I write three lines. I let the bad weather pass through the nervous system like a power cut through a summer evening in Calcutta. It is miserable. It feels longer than it is.

Then the fan starts again.

Not always. Not immediately.

Often enough.

That "often enough" is not decoration. It is engineering done with cheap tools. Tighten one screw. Replace one fuse. Drink water. Do not believe the worst sentence produced after midnight. Remember that the mind, like the city, has load-shedding.

I do not know whether my room is a prison or a shelter.

Maybe it is both.

Maybe the trick is not to smash the door open in one theatrical scene. Maybe the trick is to open it a little, let the air come in, look out, close it if needed, and open it again tomorrow. Stand near the threshold like a suspicious old Bengali man inspecting rain before deciding whether to buy eggs.

For now I remain here under the bright light, with a small income, cracked courage, ridiculous worries, old education, Calcutta humidity, and one useful knowledge: a bad thought can be loud without being true.

The room is not the whole world.

The world, mad as it is, has not yet managed to cancel tea.
