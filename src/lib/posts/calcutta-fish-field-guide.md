The fish stall in Calcutta does not arrange itself like a biology textbook. It arranges itself by memory, price, bones, fat, river nostalgia, household skill, and the private question of whether dinner will be eaten peacefully or with everyone extracting needles from their mouths like amateur surgeons.

Fish names are stable in the market until they are not. A Bengali name can cover more than one close species. A seller can be exact, careless, or optimistic. So "good fish" cannot mean only "tasty fish." It may mean good for regular eating, good for children, good for a quick lunch, good for micronutrients, good for a special day, or good only if the wallet has recently become overconfident.

A useful regular fish in Kolkata is usually one of four things: protein-rich with manageable bones; small enough to be eaten whole; fatty in a nutritionally useful way; or lower in the food chain, where chemical accumulation is usually less worrying than in large predators. A poor frequent choice is not wicked. It is simply a worse bargain: too large, too predatory, too variable, too dependent on frying, too bony for the benefit, or too easily substituted in the market.

Start with the carp republic. Rui or Rohu is *Labeo rohita*. Katla is *Catla catla*, with older names sometimes listing *Labeo catla*. Mrigal is *Cirrhinus cirrhosus*. Bata is *Labeo bata*. These are not glamorous fish, but they are structurally sensible. Rui is the best all-rounder: moderate fat, familiar bones, reliable flesh, good in light jhol or richer preparations. Katla is richer and head-heavy, beloved by those who like the oily collar and head, but heavier as a regular fish. Mrigal is leaner and less celebrated. Bata is one of the better modest decisions in the market.

Then comes Ilish, Hilsa, *Tenualosa ilisha*, the fish before which many Bengalis expect reason to remove its shoes. Hilsa is genuinely excellent. It has a distinctive fat and flavor few fish can imitate. But it is not an everyday rational fish. It is expensive, seasonal, full of bones, and easy to romanticize beyond sense. For a careful adult who knows how to eat it, good. For rushed lunches and small children, less practical. Hilsa is a high-return, high-maintenance asset.

Bhetki is *Lates calcarifer*, Asian seabass or barramundi. This is the urban polite fish: fewer bones, neat fillets, comfortable in fry, paturi, and restaurant ambition. It is useful for people who want protein without combat. But it sits higher in the food chain than small pond fish. Large specimens should not become a blind daily habit, especially for children or pregnancy. Small to moderate portions are wiser.

Pabda is usually *Ompok pabda*, though markets blur silky catfish cousins when convenient. Tangra often belongs to the *Mystus* group, but the market name can cover several small catfish. These are good Bengali fish because they taste distinct, cook quickly, and are not giant predators. Pabda gives tenderness without the prestige tax of Bhetki. Tangra works beautifully in sharper gravies.

Koi is *Anabas testudineus*. Magur is commonly *Clarias batrachus* or related *Clarias* market forms. Shingi is *Heteropneustes fossilis*. These air-breathing fish carry an old recovery-food aura in Bengali households. Some of the reputation is folklore, but the practical basis is real: they are protein-dense, strongly flavored, and often sold live or very fresh because they survive handling. They are good when freshness and source are trustworthy. They are less good when the supply chain is murky, because hardy fish can survive bad handling and still reach the stall looking respectable.

Now the small fish. Mourala is *Amblypharyngodon mola*. Puti is often *Puntius sophore* or close relatives. These fish are too easily dismissed by urban status habits. Eaten whole, they can deliver calcium and micronutrients that fillet culture throws away. The head, bones, and tiny structures are not waste; much of the value sits there. Fresh small fish cooked with restraint are among the better regular foods in the Bengali universe.

Topse is *Polynemus paradiseus*, the paradise threadfin. Parshe is usually *Planiliza parsia*, the goldspot mullet. Pomfret or Rupchanda is commonly *Pampus argenteus*, though trade names can be loose. These estuary and coastal fish can be excellent, but source matters. Estuarine life carries a whole watershed inside it. Urban runoff, industrial waste, and microplastic contamination are not culinary rumors. The fish is not the villain. The water remembers.

Boal, *Wallago attu*, is a large predatory catfish. Here biology begins whispering what appetite may not want to hear. Large predators accumulate what smaller prey collect. They may be delicious, but they should be occasional rather than automatic. The same caution applies to oversized Bhetki, very large catfish, and any large fish from doubtful water.

Plainly put, good frequent choices include Rui, Bata, Pabda, moderate Tangra, and small fish such as Mourala and Puti. They offer protein, reasonable everyday practicality, and in the case of small whole fish, better micronutrient value than their size suggests.

Hilsa is best treated as rich and occasional. Bhetki is excellent for low-bone eating, but moderation is sensible for large specimens. Parshe, Pomfret, Topse, Koi, Magur, and Shingi can be good but depend more heavily on source, size, handling, and market honesty.

The worst frequent bets are very large predatory fish, oversized specimens from uncertain waters, and any fish sold through a chain you do not trust. The badness is chemical and logistical, not moral. Mustard paste cannot purify a polluted watershed.

The older Bengali habit of rotation was wiser than it looked. Carp one day, small fish another, Hilsa when the season and wallet permitted, lighter jhol in heat, richer cooking when the weather allowed. That was not quaintness. It was systems design under constraint.

Buy fish like an engineer inspecting a bridge. Ask what it really is. Ask where it came from: pond, river, estuary, sea. Prefer smaller or medium specimens over heroic monsters. Favor whole fish you can inspect over immaculate anonymous fillets. For children, pregnancy, and frequent fish eating, lean toward smaller, lower-on-the-food-chain, nonpredatory fish. Do not confuse price with purity.

Bengali fish culture survives not because it worships Hilsa alone, but because it knows the entire aquatic hierarchy. The best fish in Calcutta is often not the most expensive fish. It is the one whose ecology, size, freshness, and cooking method match the actual meal in front of you.
