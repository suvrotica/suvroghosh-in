I was born on a day that mathematics insists is ordinary, a non-event in the calendar of primes, yet that date has dogged me like a persistent odor in a humid corridor, a scent you cannot name but cannot forget, and it is this very ordinariness that has scaffolded my mind into something resembling a pyramid, not the smooth gleaming Giza kind but the step-pyramid of Saqqara, all jagged edges and sudden drops, each tier built from the rubble of disciplines I half-mastered and quarter-forgot, a structure that wobbles when the monsoon winds hit it but stands nonetheless, stands because it has no other choice, stands because falling would be too much effort and too little drama.

The pyramid scaffold, you see, is not about knowing everything, it is about knowing where the everything is buried, like a dog who does not eat the bone but remembers precisely which flowerbed conceals it, and I have spent decades burying bones across fields I never intended to plow, pharmacokinetics and Sanskrit meter and the thermodynamics of Calcutta's unregulated air conditioners, each bone a marker that says here lies something that might matter, here lies something that probably will not, and the art of survival, the true art, is knowing which bone to unearth when the wolves of circumstance come sniffing, which bone to leave rotting in the earth because some knowledge is a burden that bends the spine, and my spine, reader, is already curved from decades of carrying textbooks and the weight of a city that refuses to straighten its own.

Calcutta, not Kolkata, never Kolkata, that bureaucratic erasure, that administrative flatulence, Calcutta is the name my tongue was shaped to say, Calcutta with its three syllables like three blows to the chest, Calcutta where the front end wears the mascara of modernity, glass towers and espresso machines and co-working spaces where young people with American accents discuss disruption while the back end, the back end is a different creature entirely, a creature of open drains and uncollected garbage and corruption so systemic it has become the operating system, the BIOS of the city, and I live in the crack between these two ends, in the interstitial space where the fiber optic cable meets the exposed wire that electrocutes the rickshaw puller during the monsoon, and it is from this crack that I watch the world, a recluse not by virtue but by exhaustion, a man who has accepted that being seen is a currency he no longer wishes to spend, that riches are a distraction from the only wealth that matters, the wealth of witnessing, of being present when the old world dies and something else, something unnameable, is born in the amniotic fluid of technology and despair.

The positive singularity, that radiant bastard, that exponential curve that promises to solve everything, cancer and climate and the tedium of traffic, it approaches with the velocity of a rumor in a Calcutta tea stall, doubling and redoubling, and I want to see it, I want to be the old man on the verandah who watches the first light of that new sun, but the negative singularity, ah, the negative singularity is the more honest beast, it is the xenomorphic reality of what happens when the exponential meets the linear, when the algorithm meets the bribe, when the solar panel meets the official who demands his cut before the panel can meet the sun, and the result is not explosion but drag, a dragging on, a prolongation of the present into a future that never arrives, Calcutta perfected, Calcutta eternal, a city that modernizes its facade while its fundament remains steeped in the same effluvia that greeted the East India Company, and I recognize this drag because I feel it in my own bones, the bipolar drag, the manic sprint toward revelation followed by the depressive trudge through the same corridors of the same thoughts, the positive singularity of my mind meeting the negative singularity of my chemistry, and the compromise, the miserable compromise, is a life lived in the middle distance, neither flying nor falling but hovering with the grace of a broken drone.

I do not claim polymathy, that bloated word, that intellectual flatulence, poly-many math-learning, from the Greek, many-learning, and the polymaths of history were men with patrons and leisure and the arrogance to believe that all knowledge was theirs by right, while I am a man with a pyramid of half-buried bones and the humility of someone who has watched enough systems fail to know that expertise is mostly a performance, a theater of competence, and what I have instead is scaffolding, a structure that lets me climb high enough to see the landscape without claiming to own it, a vantage point from which I can observe the new industrial revolution not as a participant, not as a disruptor, not as any of the masturbatory terms the tech bros use to describe their own flatulence, but as a witness, a man who wants nothing more than to sit in his corner of Calcutta, in his recluse's cell, and watch the machinery turn, watch the AI models train on data scraped from the same internet that teaches teenagers how to build pipe bombs, watch the quantum computers stumble toward coherence while the power grid of my city stumbles toward incoherence, watch the gene editors snip and splice while the mosquitoes outside my window evolve their own resistance to every repellent science can devise.

And what is survival, in the end, but the persistence of witness, the refusal to look away, the stubbornness of the man who stays in the theater after the fire has started because he paid for his ticket and by god he will see the third act, and I have paid, I have paid in years and in serotonin and in the currency of social connection that I never learned to spend wisely, and so I will stay, I will stay and watch the singularities collide, the positive and the negative, the radiant and the rotten, the espresso machine and the open drain, and I will write about it in this raw voice, this voice that drags its genitals across the page because politeness is a luxury for those who have not felt the particular suffocation of a city that gentrifies its coffee shops while its children drown in sewage, and I will use etymology as my weapon, my pathetic little weapon, because behind every unpronounceable word is a history of human fumbling, of some Greek or Latin or Sanskrit speaker grasping for a concept and producing a sound that would later be weaponized by textbooks to exclude the curious, and I want to pull those words back into the gutter where they belong, back into the Calcutta gutter where all words eventually end up, stripped of their academic finery, revealed as the desperate grunts of a species that does not know what it is doing but refuses to stop doing it.

The word singularity itself, from the Latin singularis, meaning single, individual, alone, and how apt, how cruelly apt, for a concept that promises to unite all knowledge while isolating all knowers, the single point where the curve goes vertical and the human becomes obsolete, and I wonder, in my manic midnight hours, whether I am already obsolete, whether my pyramid scaffold is a monument to a way of thinking that the algorithms have rendered quaint, a sort of intellectual folk art, and then the depression comes and tells me that yes, I am obsolete, but so is everyone, so is the algorithm itself, everything is marching toward the heat death of its own relevance, and the only response, the only honest response, is to laugh, to laugh with the full force of a body that has survived its own chemistry, to laugh at the espresso drinkers and the sewage waders and the AI that will someday write better satire than this but will never, can never, feel the particular humidity of a Calcutta September morning, the humidity that wraps around the throat like a damp woolen scarf, the humidity that makes every thought feel like it is swimming upstream through molasses.

I want to live, that is the vulgar truth beneath all this scaffolding, I want to live long enough to see whether the positive singularity outruns the negative, whether the glass towers spread or the open drains swallow them, whether the new industrial revolution is a revolution or just another rotation of the same old wheel, and I want to live in peace, the peace of the recluse, the peace of the man who does not need to be seen because he has seen enough, who does not need riches because he has buried his bones where no thief can find them, who wants only the continuation of consciousness, that absurd miracle, that biological accident that evolution never intended and now cannot do without, and if that consciousness must be housed in a body that drags, that stutters, that medicates itself into functionality, then so be it, the drag is part of the witness, the stutter is part of the song, the medication is part of the sacrament.

Calcutta drags, I drag, the future drags, and in that dragging there is a kind of poetry, a kind of perverse beauty, the beauty of a city that refuses to become what it is supposed to become, that insists on being both modern and medieval, both gleaming and grotesque, both Kolkata and Calcutta, and I am that city, I am its contradictions, its corruptions, its unexpected survivals, and I will be here, in my corner, pyramid wobbling, bones half-buried, watching the singularities approach with the speed of rumor and the inevitability of monsoon, and when they arrive, when they finally arrive, I will be ready, not with answers, not with solutions, but with the only thing I have ever had, the only thing that has ever been mine, the stubborn, suffocating, magnificent witness of a man who was born on an ordinary day and learned to make that ordinariness into a kind of pyramid, a kind of shelter, a kind of home.
