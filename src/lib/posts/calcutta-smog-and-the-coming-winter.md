The windowsill collects a fine gray powder before the city has even admitted winter is coming. You wipe it once. It returns. You wipe it again. It returns with the patience of a department that has misplaced your application but not your inconvenience.

Calcutta winter does not arrive only as weather. It arrives as documentation. The cooler air lowers the ceiling of the city and makes visible what traffic, construction, garbage smoke, road dust, small industry, old engines, and public laziness have been writing all year. Summer scatters some sins upward. Rain pushes some into drains. Winter gathers the minutes of the meeting and asks everyone to breathe them.

This is the season when the Air Quality Index, or AQI, becomes a household number again. AQI is a public-health scale that converts pollutant levels into a single risk category. It is useful because ordinary people need a simple signal. It is also a polite compression of an ugly reality. The air is not one number. It is a mixture of particles, gases, smoke, dust, fumes, and whatever the city has decided to burn, grind, idle, or ignore before breakfast.

The number helps. The throat had already filed the complaint.

There is always a civic dance around this subject. Advisories appear. Sensitive groups are told to avoid outdoor exertion, which is excellent advice for people whose lives do not require outdoor exertion. Offices discuss remote work for those whose work can be made remote. Children may be told to stay indoors. Meanwhile drivers, guards, vendors, delivery workers, sweepers, construction workers, and the great unofficial labor force of the city continue serving as mobile air monitors with lungs attached.

A city reveals its class structure by who is allowed to avoid the air.

The winter trick is partly meteorological. On calm cold mornings, dirty air can linger near the ground instead of dispersing upward. The technical explanations are useful, but at street level the translation is simpler: the city's residue cannot leave. The air squats. Buildings blur. Light becomes tired. The morning walk becomes a small act of optimism performed inside a public warning.

Then comes the language fraud.

"Foggy today," someone says.

Fog is innocent. Fog sounds like poetry. Smog sounds like policy has entered the lungs. So the city chooses fog whenever possible. Fog lets everyone admire the softness. Smog asks who lit the fire, who left the truck idling, who allowed the dust, who failed to collect the waste, who built without covering, who measured badly, who enforced for three days and then rested.

Responsibility breaks into convenient pieces. Transport says it is not industry. Industry says it is not construction. Construction says it is not road dust. Road dust says it is not garbage burning. Garbage burning says it is poverty. Poverty says it is planning. Planning says it is politics. Politics says it is public behavior. Public behavior says it is helpless.

This is how a system becomes permanent.

None of the actual remedies is glamorous. Cleaner buses. Better waste management. Dust control that treats dust as real. Less open burning. Proper monitoring. Honest public reporting. Enforcement that survives beyond the first press photograph. Safer work conditions for people outdoors. Cleaner small-industry practices. Street design that does not turn every edge into powder. Maintenance, coordination, budgets, inspection, and the dreadful art of telling someone no.

No one wants to inaugurate maintenance.

Calcutta cannot clean its air by one magnificent gesture. The city is too dense, too old, too improvised, too economically tangled, and too dependent on small combustions that keep someone fed. This does not excuse the pollution. It explains why slogans fail. A clean solution would require the city to become less casual about the thousand small permissions by which it injures itself.

By winter, if the old pattern holds, Calcutta will again produce mornings in which the city looks half-erased. The Hooghly will wear a gray lid. The Maidan will soften at the edges. The lights will grow halos that look almost tender until the throat explains the matter more plainly.

People will continue. That is the Bengali talent and the Bengali trap. We endure and then mistake endurance for policy.

The windowsill will need wiping again tomorrow. The cloth will come away gray. The city will call it weather for as long as it can.
