The smell of chalk dust can return faster than the name of the teacher who produced it.

That is how childhood comes back: not as a clean archive, but as a badly lit theater where the old film keeps playing and the brain keeps changing the dialogue. Every few years the lighting improves, the boring parts are cut, the humiliations become jokes if they can, and background music appears without permission.

This is why an ordinary lane from childhood can look magical at fifty-one.

At ten it was only a lane. It had drains, heat, dust, small shops, irritated adults, and the usual municipal uncertainty. At fifty-one, the same lane becomes literature. The drain becomes monsoon memory. The heat becomes atmosphere. The irritated adult becomes a character. Even the bad smell acquires, with enough distance, an argument for cultural importance.

Time is a shameless interior decorator.

The brain helps. It does not store childhood like a careful school clerk storing mark sheets. It stores pieces. A slap of summer heat on the neck. A metal tiffin box. Chalk dust. Coconut oil. A torn ball. A voice from another room. A tram bell somewhere far away. Then, years later, when you ask what childhood was like, the brain does not give you the truth.

It gives you a production.

The terrible part is that the production is often better than the truth.

Nostalgia is homesickness with decorative lighting. The polite version says it is the pain of not being able to return home. The ordinary version is simpler: the mind takes an old, sweaty afternoon and turns it into a framed painting.

You remember the old house. But which one? The actual house had damp walls, cracked plaster, a calendar hiding a stain, and a fan that turned with the moral energy of a retired clerk. The remembered house has golden light. The actual school had boredom, unfairness, arithmetic, fear, and children capable of astonishing cruelty before lunch. The remembered school has bells, rain, friends, and the noble smell of exercise books.

The actual childhood had stomach upsets, scoldings, loneliness, mosquito coils, small humiliations, and the horror of being sent to buy something and forgetting the item halfway down the lane.

The remembered childhood has magic.

We are fraud victims.

We are also the fraudsters.

Every time you remember something, you slightly remake it. This is not necessarily dishonesty. Memory is not a locked cupboard. It is soft clay. You touch it and it changes. You remember the past while standing in the present, and the present has dirty fingers.

If today feels hard, the past looks warmer. If today feels shameful, the past looks cleaner. If money is tight, the cheap joys of childhood begin to look like pure wisdom. A small ice candy becomes a lost civilization. A shared bottle of orange soda becomes evidence that society once functioned properly. A roadside snack becomes philosophical, though at the time you only wanted the larger piece.

The clearer the memory feels, the more suspicious it may be.

A memory repeated for thirty or forty years is not only a memory. It is a rehearsed performance. You have told it to friends, edited it for relatives, softened it for yourself, sharpened it when irritated, made it funnier when the listener looked bored, and removed the parts where you looked foolish. After enough retelling, the memory stands up, clears its throat, and pretends to be evidence.

It is not evidence.

It is family theater without a reliable script.

I know this because I have my own ghosts. They arrive without invitation, usually on a difficult day. I may be sitting in the southern fringe of Calcutta, trying to stretch consulting money over bills, food, internet, electricity, and the small expenses that breed in corners. The ceiling fan makes its tired helicopter sound. Someone outside is arguing about coriander as if democracy depends on it. My mind is not exactly a garden.

Then childhood walks in.

Not grandly. Not like a hero.

It comes with dusty knees, an old lane, a smell of rain on hot concrete, and the vanished confidence of running without calculating consequences. It brings the old belief that adults knew what they were doing. That was childhood's greatest fairy tale. Adults did not know. They had taller bodies, louder voices, and better access to keys.

Still, we believed them.

We believed everything directly. Not accurately. Directly.

The world had not yet been fully explained, which made it larger. Wonder often enters through the door marked missing information. Then life explains things, and explanation is not always an improvement. You learn rent. You learn interest. You learn that work is proof, shame, negotiation, and survival, not merely work. You learn that sleep can be interrupted by thoughts with no manners.

So childhood begins to shine behind you.

Not because it was perfect.

Because it was before.

Before invoices. Before the arithmetic of middle age. Before public coping became a daily performance. Before every decision carried ten invisible consequences tied to its ankle.

But the old days were not innocent in the way we claim. They were simply less documented. If someone had filmed every childhood afternoon exactly as it happened, the result would look less like poetry and more like a municipal report with occasional sweetness. We were bored often. Frightened often. We misunderstood nearly everything. We cried over things we cannot now name. We were selfish, tender, silly, loyal, brave for three minutes, cautious for three hours, and deeply concerned with snacks.

That is not an insult.

That is childhood.

The Bengali mind knows how to do this polishing. Give an ugly wall enough moss and somebody will call it atmosphere. A cracked courtyard becomes heritage. A leaking roof becomes memory. A cramped room becomes those days. Even hardship, once it stops actively biting, may be invited back as character.

Nostalgia is moss. It softens stone. It also hides cracks.

That is the danger. A little nostalgia is seasoning. Too much becomes a full meal of sweetened fog. It makes us say foolish things like people were better then, when often they were merely less searchable. It makes old poverty look simple, old fear look dramatic, old confusion look pure.

The brain is not wicked. It is practical. It cannot carry every childhood afternoon in raw form: every dull conversation, every failed sum, every minute of waiting for lunch. So it compresses. Like a JPEG, it throws away detail and keeps the shape. Emotional compression has its own mischief. It preserves what glows, what hurts, what explains us, what excuses us, and what accuses us.

This is why childhood can feel like both shelter and courtroom.

You go there to rest, and an old shame is sitting in the corner, tapping its foot. You go there for sweetness, and old loneliness opens one eye. You go there for innocence and find smallness, dependence, confusion, and the terrible powerlessness of being young in a world run by people who were themselves improvising.

Still, I will not scold nostalgia too much. Some evenings, when the city is tired and the sky looks like old aluminum, I want the past to sit beside me for ten minutes. I want the child I was to explain how he managed to wake up every day without thinking of bills, heat, algorithms, politics, and the quiet erosion of ordinary confidence.

He would not understand the question.

That would be his gift.

The child is gone, of course. Not in a tragic way. Gone in the ordinary way all earlier selves are gone. They became us and disappeared into the mixture. The ten-year-old was folded into the fifteen-year-old, who was folded into the thirty-year-old, who was folded into this middle-aged man under a fan, trying to write something honest without turning it into a monument.

We are layered creatures.

Like an old Calcutta wall with five colors of paint, two political slogans, one lost poster, and damp coming through from behind. Scratch the surface and some earlier version appears. Scratch too hard and plaster falls.

So remember childhood, but do not worship it. Smile at it, but do not obey it. Let it visit, but do not give it the house keys.

The past is allowed to be beautiful without being accurate. That does not make it useless. It makes it human.

You cannot go back to childhood.

You can only meet its ghost.

If you are lucky, the ghost will not lecture you. It will arrive quietly, smelling of dust, rain, chalk, coconut oil, and cheap orange ice. It will show you one small scene, not because the scene was perfectly true, but because some part of you still needs it.

Then it will turn the corner.

Naturally, you will follow.

Naturally, you will be too late.
