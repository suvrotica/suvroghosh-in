Three seventeen in the morning and the ceiling fan has developed a personality, a wobble, a whirr, a kind of asthmatic devotion to its own rotation, and I am lying here in Calcutta with my eyes open like two boiled eggs that refuse to be eaten, staring at the plaster peeling in patterns that resemble, depending on my level of sleep-deprived delusion, either the map of Bengal or the face of every venture capitalist who has ever said the word "disruption" without immediately choking on his own tongue. I cannot sleep. There is so much going on, and by "going on" I mean the slow-motion mugging of the global economy by a handful of men in hoodies who have discovered that if you say "artificial intelligence" three times while spinning around, a forty-trillion-dollar market materializes like a genie with a termination letter in its hand.

I keep reading these pronouncements, these blog posts from the oracles of Silicon Valley, and what strikes me, what genuinely keeps me awake like a mosquito made of pure anxiety, is that the people building this stuff do not seem to see the contradictions that are visible from here, from my middle-class mattress in a middle-class flat in a city that the future has apparently forgotten to invoice. They speak of abundance. They speak of the singularity, the rapture of the nerds, the moment when the machines become smarter than us and suddenly nobody has to work anymore and we all live in a post-scarcity paradise where the only remaining human occupation is, presumably, writing thoughtful LinkedIn posts about how grateful we are to have been made obsolete. And I think: wonderful. Splendid. But who, precisely, owns the machines? Because from where I am lying, and I am lying very still, very awake, very much not in possession of any equity in this hypothetical 1.4-trillion-dollar IPO, it looks less like a singularity and more like a singularity of bank accounts, a gravitational collapse where all the money falls into a few pockets and the rest of us are left to orbit the event horizon, stretching into spaghetti.

The math is simple enough to do at three in the morning, which is fortunate, because what else am I doing? If the AI industry captures even a fraction of the value it promises, we are talking about the largest transfer of wealth in human history, a kind of vertical integration of human purpose itself. The pitch goes like this: first, we automate the truck drivers, then the coders, then the lawyers, then the doctors, then the artists, then the people who write the apologies when the chatbot says something racist, and then, presumably, the people who write the apologies for the apologies. And the money saved, the productivity gained, the forty trillion dollars of it, flows upward, always upward, like heat in a universe running toward entropy, until it pools in the accounts of men who have already purchased their apocalypse bunkers in New Zealand and are now purchasing the apocalypse itself, retail, no discounts. The singularity, if there is a financial one, is positive for them and negative for us, the plebeian mass, first in the developed nations where the jobs actually are, and then in the connected developing nations like mine, where much of this labor is outsourced, where the call centers and the back offices and the medical transcriptionists will discover that their jobs have been vaporized not by a robot walking through the door, but by a subscription service.

And they offer us, in exchange for our livelihoods, the most beautiful three-letter acronym since LOL: UBI. Universal Basic Income. The idea that the government, or the tech companies, or some consortium of the enlightened, will simply pay everyone enough to live on while the robots do the work. It sounds lovely. It sounds like the welfare state finally grew a spine and a wallet. But I am from Calcutta, a city that has been promised things by powerful men since the East India Company discovered that promises are cheaper than ships, and I know the smell of a politician's promise. It is an oxymoron without the oxygen, a beautiful corpse dressed for a party. No one, not even in America, knows what UBI would actually mean. How much? Paid by whom? What happens to housing prices when everyone has the same basic income and the landlords, those great readers of economic tea leaves, adjust accordingly? What happens to the dignity of work, that strange Protestant ghost that haunts even us Bengali atheists now, when your job is not taken from you but simply rendered economically absurd, like a lighthouse keeper in an age of GPS? The questions multiply like bacteria in the Calcutta heat, and the answers, when they come at all, come in the form of TED talks and white papers written by people who will never have to live on the amount they are proposing.

I am not a Luddite. Let me be clear, because the fan is spinning and my thoughts are spinning and I want to be precise even in my bitterness. I love the wonderment of it. I love that we have taught sand to think, that we have carved transistors into silicon with wavelengths of light, that a machine can now translate Bengali poetry into English while losing only most of the soul. There is genuine magic here, the kind that would make Faraday weep into his notebook, the kind that makes me, even now, even at this ungodly hour, feel that old childhood thrill of science, the sense that the universe is stranger and more generous than the textbooks admit. But the history of technology is not a history of automatic justice. The spinning jenny did not consult the weavers. The container ship did not ask the longshoremen. And the large language model does not inquire whether the content moderator in Manila or the paralegal in Leeds or the junior programmer in Bangalore has any particular attachment to eating regularly.

The contradiction, the thing that glows in the dark of my insomnia like a radioactive tritium dial, is that the people building this future speak constantly of "benefiting humanity" while constructing business models that require, as their fundamental fuel, the reduction of human participation in the economy. They say they are democratizing intelligence, and perhaps they are, in the same way that the printing press democratized literacy, which is to say, after a few centuries of bloodshed and adjustment and the invention of entirely new forms of stupidity. But the printing press did not have a business model dependent on the printing press being the only literate entity in the room. The AI companies do. Their valuation, their forty-trillion-dollar ambition, rests on the premise that human labor becomes a legacy cost, like maintaining a vintage steam engine for the tourists.

So I lie here, and I worry, and the worry is not abstract. It is specific. It is the worry of a man who has outsourced his own anxiety to the cheapest bidder and now finds the bidder has been automated. I worry about the developed nations first, yes, where the rift between the very rich and everyone else is already a canyon, and where this technology will pour acid into the canyon until it becomes a trench, a grave, a geological feature. But I worry more, in a way, about India, about the developing world, because we are not ready. We are not automated. We are not efficient. We are a nation of magnificent inefficiency, of jugaad and chai stalls and three people doing the job of one with great argument and greater love, and this, this mess, is actually our employment policy. The easy utopia that AI can engineer, the one where robots pick the vegetables and drive the trucks and the humans are freed for higher pursuits, requires a baseline of development that we do not have. When the call center jobs go, when the coding jobs go, when the content moderation and the data entry and the back-office accounting all evaporate into the cloud, what remains? What is the Indian equivalent of the post-work society? A billion people contemplating the infinite with empty stomachs?

The optimists say new jobs will emerge, as they always have. And perhaps they will. But the last time technology destroyed work this fast, we had a century of labor movements, two world wars, the invention of the welfare state, and a Great Depression to sort out the transition. We are proposing to do it in a decade, globally, with less social safety net than a tightrope walker over the Hooghly. The historians will note, if there are historians and not just content summarizers, that we automated the economy before we automated the empathy, that we built the machines before we built the meaning, that we raced toward the singularity with our eyes on the prize and our hearts in our throats.

It is four in the morning now. The fan continues its rotation, faithful, stupid, employed. I envy it. I envy its single task, its lack of imagination, its complete indifference to the forty-trillion-dollar future being built by men who cannot sleep either, I suspect, though for different reasons, though perhaps for the same. We are all awake in the dark, the billionaires in their bunkers and me in my bed, and somewhere in between, the machines are learning to dream for us, and I am terrified that when we finally let them, we will find that dreaming was the only job we had left.
