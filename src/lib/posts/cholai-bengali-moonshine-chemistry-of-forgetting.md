The first warning is not the bottle. It is the smell.

It rises from rice, molasses, palm sap, yeast, a hidden vessel, and time: sour-sweet, damp, slightly secretive. No label. No cork. No polished word about notes of fruit. This is local spirit, made close to poverty, impatience, and risk.

Cholai is not merely alcohol.

It is a small illegal promise of distance from the day.

That sentence should not be made pretty. Cheap liquor can ruin households, injure bodies, swallow wages, sharpen violence, and leave families paying for one person's escape. There is no folk charm in poisoning. But if we stop at scolding the glass, we miss the life standing around it.

A poor person does not always drink because he is foolish. Sometimes he drinks because the day has been too long, the wage too small, the room too crowded, the future too tight, and the only affordable pause comes in a small burning mouthful. A reasonable society would ask why forgetting has a market. We often ask why the poor lack discipline.

The chemistry is simple enough to be frightening. Fermentation happens when yeast consumes sugar and produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. A warm place, a sugary base, and enough time can turn household ingredients into alcohol. Distillation concentrates the alcohol by heating the liquid, collecting vapor, and cooling it back into stronger liquid.

The danger is that alcohol is not one tidy substance. Ethanol is the intended drink. Methanol, when present through contamination, poor technique, bad ingredients, or adulteration, can cause severe poisoning. It can damage vision, acidify the blood, and kill. The difference between one kind of alcohol and another is not a moral lecture. It is chemistry entering the bloodstream.

Every hooch tragedy follows a familiar public rhythm. People die. Officials announce raids. Politicians speak gravely. The cameras arrive. Then the cameras leave. The same poverty remains. The same thirst remains. The same economy of risk resumes.

The state is not innocent either. Legal liquor brings revenue. Illegal liquor brings condemnation. Public health appears after disaster, not always before it. Between tax, morality, police action, and local protection, the poor drinker often becomes the easiest visible offender in a much larger arrangement.

The larger arrangement includes low wages, weak healthcare, shame, boredom, hard labor, local patronage, masculine pride, and a society that often treats the poor as if they should suffer quietly for everyone else's comfort. The drink is the visible danger. The life around it is the slow ferment.

There is also the old domestic knowledge behind the process. The kitchen has always known transformation: batter rising, pickles souring, rice changing character, milk turning, spices blooming in heat. Fermentation is not foreign to Bengal. What changes is control, concentration, need, and risk.

A society can ferment too.

Leave humiliation in a closed room and it changes smell. Leave young people with promises and no work, families with bills and no margin, workers with pain and no rest, and something sharp develops. Some of it becomes anger. Some becomes politics. Some becomes cheap liquid in a plastic pouch.

The question is not whether the liquid is dangerous. It is.

The question is why danger remains cheaper than relief.

Evening comes. A kettle boils somewhere. A small glass changes hands somewhere else. Steam rises, vapor escapes, condenses, returns. Physics has a cruel sense of humor. The body wants relief. The world offers chemistry.

Not every escape is freedom.

Some are only another room in the same locked house.
