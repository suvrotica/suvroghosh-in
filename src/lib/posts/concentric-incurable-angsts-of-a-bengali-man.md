The lower middle-class Bengali man does not have problems. He has arrangements.

Problems come one by one, like visitors who remove their shoes at the door. Arrangements arrive carrying relatives. Rent, teeth, electricity, work, shame, sleep, the price of fish, the neighbor's drilling machine, the laptop that looks at you with the moral disappointment of a schoolteacher, and the old question sitting in the corner: what exactly happened to your life?

At first you think money is the center.

Money is not the center. Money is the first circle, the one everyone can see. It is the ring road of humiliation. It determines whether the tooth is fixed now or postponed. Whether the fan is repaired or tolerated. Whether the phone call is answered cheerfully or with the small fraud of a busy voice. Money is the grammar of modern dignity. Without it, even silence becomes badly phrased.

But the second circle is time.

Time changes texture after a certain age. At twenty, delay feels like strategy. At thirty, delay feels like difficulty. At forty, it begins to look like pattern. At fifty-one, delay starts arriving with documents. The body has its own filing system. The market has already moved to new software. The younger men have acronyms you understand too well and energy you do not trust. The past does not become past. It becomes inventory.

Then comes the circle of social inquiry.

"Ki korchho ekhon?" What are you doing now?

Such a small question. Four words. Harmless as a biscuit. But inside it lives a police station, a bank audit, a job interview, and a committee of invisible neighbors. Nobody asks it cleanly. Even when they mean well, the sentence arrives wearing shoes. It steps on the tender place between survival and explanation.

The Bengali genius for concern should be studied by military engineers. It can enter a room without breaking the door. It can appear as advice, curiosity, pity, comparison, nostalgia, or a sigh placed carefully after your name. It does not need to accuse. It only has to ask for an update.

The third circle is the body.

The body is not dramatic at first. It is bureaucratic. It sends reminders. A tooth. A back. A stomach that has become a local politician, loud and unhelpful. Sleep that arrives late and leaves early. The body does not care about your reading list. It does not respect your theories. It looks at your ambitions and asks whether you have paid the maintenance bill.

The fourth circle is respectability.

This is the most expensive one because it cannot be purchased only with money. A man can be broke and still respectable if his failure has a socially approved shape. Retired. Temporarily between assignments. Caring for something. Building something. Recovering from something. But to be simply stalled is vulgar. Stalling has no ceremonial category. It makes people nervous because it reveals how much of adult life is motion used as costume.

So the bankrupt Bengali man becomes a small republic of explanations. He explains why work changed. Why the market changed. Why the city changed. Why he came back. Why he did not leave again. Why he reads. Why he writes. Why he is not exactly finished. The explanations are not all false. That is the trouble. A clean lie would be easier to discard. These are half-true, and half-truths breed in damp corners.

At the center, after all the circles, sits a very small creature called fear.

Fear that the arrangement is permanent. Fear that the afternoon is the real shape of the rest of life. Fear that talent was only an advance payment from youth and the bank has now called the loan. Fear that the world is not hostile enough to blame and not kind enough to forgive.

What does one do with such circles?

Not solve them. That word is for equations and marketing slides. One learns to walk inside them without pretending they are not there. Pay one bill. Fix one line. Answer one question with less self-hatred than yesterday. Refuse to let concern become the official narrator. Eat carefully. Laugh where possible, especially at the absurdity of being a full-grown man defeated by a ceiling fan, a tooth, and a question asked over tea.

Writing does not cure the concentric angsts. It only draws them clearly enough that they stop pretending to be infinite.

Sometimes it makes the afternoon smaller.

In Calcutta, a smaller afternoon is not a small thing.
