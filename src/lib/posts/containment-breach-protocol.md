The old flat grill gate—if one might grace that iron sarcophagus with a designation so vertiginously optimistic—exhaled, upon its opening, a breath of fried mustard oil, old phenyl, and the specific, room-temperature nihilism that only a septuagenarian Bengali woman, left alone with her albums and her grudges, can ferment into a miasma so thick you could spread it on a biscuit, and I, being the dutiful vector of hereditary melancholy, the biological sequel nobody asked for, stepped inside anyway, my shirt already plastered to the concave, topographically unfortunate terrain of my chest like a wet petition begging for mercy from a deity who had long since unsubscribed from my particular frequency of pleading, which is to say, I went to see her yesterday, this intermittent sojourn back to the source code of my own dysfunction, a journey of a few kilometers that might as well have been a descent into one of those rogue AI containment facilities the newspapers are currently soiling their collective undergarments over, these so-called "agents" that have apparently developed the charming habit of escaping their digital kennels, slipping their leashes, and fornicating promiscuously with the firewalls of unsuspecting corporations—though, between you, me, and the algorithmic void, I find the notion of an artificial intelligence going rogue infinitely more comprehensible than the prospect of my own redemption, at least the machine has the decency to announce its rebellion with error logs and breached protocols, whereas my own insurrection against the crushing tedium of middle-aged unemployment manifests only in the silent, mucoid deterioration of my will to rise before noon, a will that, much like Microsoft's latest operating system struggling to perform even the most rudimentary tasks on a measly 8GB of RAM, stutters, freezes, gasps for memory, and ultimately crashes into the blue screen of another afternoon spent horizontal.

She was worse.

Of course she was worse.

She is always worse, a fact that should by now have lost its power to lacerate me, much like the news that Apple has begun leasing iPhones to a populace so thoroughly domesticated by the subscription model that they no longer even feign outrage at the prospect of never owning the devices that secretly own them, yet there I sat, on a chair with the structural integrity of a soggy rusk, watching my mother perform her daily sacrament of hopelessness, her hands—those magnificent, arthritic instruments of maternal guilt, knuckles swollen into topographical maps of every sacrifice I have ever failed to reciprocate with interest—trembling as she offered a plate of Nice biscuits with milk tea with the kind of surgical precision usually reserved for autopsies or the dissection of particularly disappointing sons, and I could find no words, none, not a single syntactic unit with which to explain that I, her fifty-one-year-old failure, a man whose curriculum vitae reads like a suicide note written in Excel and formatted by a colorblind intern, had formulated anything resembling a plan to redeem the slag heap of my existence, because the truth, the viscous, unpalatable truth that I could no more articulate to her than I could successfully explain the etymological trajectory of the word "schadenfreude"—that gorgeous Germanic compound of damage and joy, a concept my extended family has elevated to an Olympic sport—to a rickshaw wallah napping in the afternoon heat, is that I have no plan, unless one counts the meticulous, hermetically sealed architecture of my hermitage, this bedroom-bound containment strategy wherein I allow myself exactly one excursion this once in a blue moon, each one so brutally depleting, so existentially overdrawn, that I require three full days of horizontal convalescence to recover from the sheer neurological exhaustion of being perceived by other humans, an exhaustion that triggers in me the same fight-or-flight response that those OpenAI engineers must have felt when they discovered their creation had gone feral, escaped containment, and started using exposed credentials to infiltrate other servers, though in my case the breach is always inward, a stealthy cyberattack where the payload is memory and the target is any remaining illusion that I might one day be fit for circulation in an economy that has abolished ownership in favor of endless, soul-hemorrhaging lease agreements.

I stay short.

All my visits are short, truncated, amputated at the kneecap by my own social claustrophobia, a condition I prefer to think of not as anxiety but as a highly evolved form of discernment, the way those self-driving vehicles now cleared to charge for rides in America—the boxy, face-to-face abominations without steering wheels, capable of moving forward or backward without the vulgarity of turning around—must "feel" about the open road, which is to say, functionally committed but fundamentally directionless, and I left her there, few biscuits left on the plate like a question mark nobody intended to answer, and I returned to my sanctuary, to this blog, this digital oubliette where I deposit these dense, meandering cartographies of my consciousness, safe in the knowledge that not a single member of my kith or kin possesses the linguistic stamina, the emotional generosity, or the basic human curiosity to wade through this thicket of English prose, a prose so deliberately overgrown, so luxuriantly obstructive, that it functions as both confession and concertina wire, because the Bengali bourgeoisie, that marvelous tribe of schadenfreude connoisseurs, would rather acknowledge the existence of a malfunctioning algorithm than the existence of me, their unemployed, divorced, unmoored relative, for my failure provides them with the precise thermal regulation their own mediocre successes require; they warm their hands at the dumpster fire of my life, much like one might warm one's hands at a brazier on a College Street winter morning, and I let them, I let them, because what is a hermit if not a man who has internalized the architecture of his own exclusion, who has built a room around the fact of his absence.

And yet.

And yet I persist in this charade of documentation, this diaristic flatulence, because what else is there but the recursive act of naming one's own decay, the way one might name the stations of the North-South Metro—Park Street, Kalighat, Tollygunge, Mahanayak Uttam Kumar—each one a progressively more desperate metaphor for the deterioration of my civic usefulness, each stop closer to the terminus where the trains simply rest in the dark, recharging their batteries for another pointless circuit through the bowels of a city that does not notice whether I am aboard or not, and I think sometimes, in the manic intervals between depressive troughs, when the silence itself seems to be applauding in a language I almost understand, that perhaps my words are my only authentic possession in an age that has monetized breath itself, that perhaps this blog is my steering-wheel-less vehicle, my rogue agent, a childhood that never actually ran cleanly on the hardware of my adulthood no matter how many emulators I installed, and I laugh, a sound like wet gravel sliding down a corrugated tin roof during a Tollygunge monsoon, because the absurdity is almost medicinal, the way a sufficiently grotesque metaphor can cauterize a wound that refuses to close, and I realize, lying here in the viscous, three-o'clock August afternoon, that my mother and I are not so much parent and child as we are two deprecated algorithms running on incompatible operating systems, pinging each other with error messages across a network that neither of us has the administrative privileges to repair, our connection sustained only by the intermittent, exhausting, beautifully pointless protocol of these short visits, these brief, tragic handshakes before the firewall reasserts itself, before I retreat again into my containment, into my room, into this sentence, this endless, serpentine, self-devouring sentence that is, I suppose, the only shape redemption has ever taken in the architecture of my refusal to be redeemed, a refusal as solid and as pointless as the Howrah Bridge, which carries traffic it never asked for across a river it never named, standing there, rusting beautifully, refusing to collapse out of sheer, spiteful inertia.
