The phone glows in a damp South Calcutta room, and the ceiling fan turns with the tired neutrality of a government office at 3 p.m.

Somebody has not replied.

That is all. No disaster. No official notice. No public disgrace. Just one message seen, or perhaps not seen, or perhaps seen and set aside by a person whose entire day may have nothing to do with me. Yet the body behaves as if the neighborhood has assembled and voted on my social fitness.

This is the comic cruelty of approval hunger. It rarely arrives as a grand philosophical crisis. It arrives as a blue tick, a missing reply, a smaller-than-expected reaction, a post that sinks quietly, a joke that nobody acknowledges, a silence with a timestamp.

Ordinary silence can be peaceful. Digital silence has a watch.

The ancient part of the nervous system does not understand the difference. It knows that being cast out once meant danger. No food, no fire, no protection, no one to keep watch while night did what night has always done. The group turning away was not a minor social inconvenience. It was survival risk.

Now the same old alarm system sits inside a person holding a smartphone in 2026. It cannot properly distinguish between exile from a camp and a lukewarm response on WhatsApp. The software is ancient. The interface is modern. The result is ridiculous and still painful.

This is where Alfred Adler becomes useful.

Adlerian psychology asks a question that feels rude because it removes some of the furniture we hide behind: what are you doing with this feeling now? Not where did every wound begin, not which past event explains the entire weather system of the self, but what purpose does the current behavior serve?

That does not mean fear is imaginary. It does not mean a person chooses every tightening of the chest, every hesitation, every little collapse of nerve. But feelings can have functions even when they hurt. Fear can keep a person from applying. Shame can keep a person silent. The need for approval can keep a life small while pretending to keep it safe.

Not trying is wonderfully protective. If I do not write, nobody can dismiss the sentence. If I do not call, nobody can refuse. If I do not begin, the failure remains theoretical. A theoretical failure is much more comfortable than a real one. It can sit in the mind wearing clean clothes.

Approval is the cheapest painkiller society sells. A nod, a like, a "well said," a little praise, a little reassurance that one is still socially edible. Social media did not invent this hunger. It industrialized it. The old village had a few judges. The modern phone carries a tiny examination hall in the pocket.

Every thought can be submitted for grading. Every meal, opinion, irritation, face, joke, book, and grievance can be placed in front of the public counter. Then the same person wonders why the mind feels measured all day.

Adler's separation of tasks is useful because it is simple without being easy.

My task is to live according to my values.

Their task is to react.

That is the whole blade. Not a soft idea. A clean one.

My task may be to speak honestly, work decently, write without begging, help without becoming furniture, refuse without staging a drama, and contribute without dissolving. Other people may like, dislike, misunderstand, gossip, scroll past, envy, applaud, ignore, diagnose casually, or file me under "difficult." That is their interior weather. I cannot manage it without turning my life into unpaid public relations.

The attempt to control perception is exhausting because it has no finish line. One keeps adjusting, explaining, polishing, softening, cutting away the inconvenient edges, fattening the agreeable parts, until the actual person has been reduced to a small pellet of compliance.

Middle-class Bengali life knows this disease well. We call it adjustment. We call it respect. We call it keeping relations. Sometimes those words name real decency. Sometimes they name fear wearing a clean shirt.

Be liked. Be decent. Do not speak too directly. Do not fail publicly. Do not say no too often. Do not begin again too late. Do not make others uncomfortable by being too clearly yourself.

The courage to be disliked does not mean becoming rude, cold, theatrical, or pleased with one's own abrasiveness. Authenticity is not a permit to behave badly. A person can be kind without being owned. Cooperative without being domesticated. Available without being absorbable.

There is a small biological metaphor here. A living cell needs a membrane. Too much wall and nothing useful enters. Too little wall and everything leaks in. Health is controlled permeability.

A person is like that. Not a prison. Not a puddle. A membrane.

The humiliating discovery, after years of worrying about public judgment, is that most people are not thinking about us that much. This should be comforting. It is also slightly insulting. One imagines a grand tribunal of critics somewhere, all discussing one's failures under a chandelier. In reality, most people are thinking about money, food, children, status, their own bodies, their own resentments, and whether the latest photo made them look tired.

Even criticism is often autobiography in disguise. A person attacks your risk because they abandoned theirs. They mock your independence because it reminds them of their obedience. They call you arrogant because your refusal exposes their performance of politeness.

Not always.

Often enough.

So when hesitation arrives - write or do not write, apply or do not apply, call or do not call, begin again with bad teeth, a nervous stomach, and an unimpressive bank balance - the better question is not whether they will approve.

Of course they may not.

The better question is: whose task is this?

If it is mine, I must carry it. If it is theirs, I must leave it with them. This does not repair loneliness, cool the room, refill the account, answer the message, or fix the weather. It gives back only one stolen object: the steering wheel.

Not the road. Not the traffic. Not the weather. Just the steering wheel, sticky with sweat, held badly perhaps, held late perhaps, but held.

Tonight, if someone dislikes me, ignores me, misunderstands me, rolls their eyes, or silently classifies me as troublesome, I will try to remember the small clean rule.

My task is mine. Their reaction is theirs.

Then I will probably check the phone again, because autonomy takes practice and the old alarm system is not retired so easily.
