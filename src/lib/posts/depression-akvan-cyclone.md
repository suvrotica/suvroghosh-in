They have named it Akvan. Of course they have. The meteorological bureaucrats in their air-conditioned rooms, the ones with the satellite feeds and the anemometers and the calm, measured voices, have looked at this swirling vortex of warm ocean spite and decided it deserves a name, an identity, a passport to destruction. Akvan. I repeat it to myself because the naming of things matters, because to name a catastrophe is to pretend we have some dominion over it, the way we name diseases and demons and the various ways the body and the sky can betray us.

And here is the joke, the cosmic pun that no one else seems to appreciate. Depression Akvan. The cyclone is a depression. I am a depression. We are both low-pressure systems, me and this meteorological event currently squatting over the Bay of Bengal like a fat, grey toad. The India Meteorological Department has categories for it, neat little boxes: Depression, Deep Depression, Cyclonic Storm. They have wind speeds in kilometers per hour, pressure readings in hectopascals, coordinates that pin it to the earth like a butterfly in a collection. I have none of these. My depression has no anemometer, no satellite imagery, no warning system broadcasting to the fishing villages of my consciousness. It simply arrives, unscheduled, unnamed, and sits.

The screen shows me Akvan's vitals. On the 19th of November, at 11:30 in the morning, it was a Cyclonic Storm with winds of 65 kilometers per hour and a pressure of 1004 hectopascals. By the 20th, it is forecast to weaken, to become a mere depression again, then a remnant, then nothing. A lifecycle. A beginning, a middle, an end. I study this data with the envy of a man studying the instruction manual for a machine he cannot operate. The cyclone will dissipate. It will spin itself out over land, dump its rain, exhaust its fury, and die a quiet meteorological death, remembered only in the annual reports and the insurance claims. My depression does not follow this curve. It intensifies without warm ocean water. It stalls without landfall. It has been spinning for weeks, months, years, a perpetual motion machine of grey.

I cannot read. This is the first casualty of my own private Akvan. The words sit on the page like dead insects, and I move my eyes across them the way one moves a broom across a floor that will never be clean. Pick up the book, put down the book. The book is too heavy. Not physically, though my arms have become thin, suspicious things, but ontologically. The book exists in a world where meaning accumulates, where sentence follows sentence like train cars, and I cannot board. I stand on the platform watching the narrative depart without me.

The non-stick pan. I keep returning to the non-stick pan. It is the perfect object for this condition. Nothing adheres. You pour the egg of your attention into the skillet and it slides around, intact, uncooked, useless. You tilt the pan and the entire contents slop over the edge onto the burner, where they sizzle and die. That is my brain now. Teflon where there should be cast iron. I watch a documentary about the history of concrete and twenty minutes in I realize I have been staring at the wall behind the television, that the narrator has been speaking to a man who left the room long ago.

Netflix asks if I am still watching. The question feels accusatory. Am I still watching? I am not even still existing. I am buffering. I am the spinning wheel of death made flesh. The series continues without me, characters moving through their plotted lives while I sit in the dark, unable to commit to even the passive act of observation. The remote control becomes a stone in my hand. To press a button requires a certainty of desire that I no longer possess. What do I want to watch? Nothing. What do I want? Nothing. The wanting itself has want-ed away.

The memories come then, because the defenses are down. When the mind cannot hold the present, the past rushes in to fill the vacuum, and my past is not a pleasant place to visit. It is a slum of old embarrassments, a museum of failures with the lights left on. I remember the thing I said in 1997. I remember the letter I did not send. I remember the face I made when I was eleven and someone took a photograph. These memories do not arrive as memories should, softened by time, rounded at the edges. They arrive with the freshness of wounds, still bleeding, still absurd. Akvan gathers moisture from the warm sea; my mind gathers grievance from the warm swamp of what has already happened and cannot, despite my most fervent prayers, be unhappened.

I would like a blank slate. This is the secret wish, the one I do not say at dinner parties, the one I do not even say to the ceiling at 3 AM because saying it gives it too much solidity. I want the etch-a-sketch shaken. I want the hard drive magnetized. I want to wake up with no name, no history, no archive of nights spent staring at the wall wondering if this is all there is. The Buddhists call this state *nirvana*, the extinguishing of the flame. I call it Tuesday, if Tuesday would only come.

But the blank slate requires the ultimate deletion. The great formatting of the disk. And here is where the joke curdles, where the satire puts down its drink and looks at you with bloodshot eyes. To be blank, truly blank, one must be dead. The memories are not stored in a cloud that can be wiped while the hardware remains. They are the hardware. The brain that cannot read is the brain that remembers, and to silence the remembering is to silence the whole orchestra, the violins and the tubas and the little triangle that plays when something is almost funny.

I know this. I know this the way one knows the sun is a star, abstractly, uselessly, a fact that does not warm the skin. And yet the wish persists, a cockroach in the kitchen of my intentions, surviving every spray of reason. *If only*, says the cockroach. *If only you could be empty. If only Akvan could come not for Orissa but for you, a direct hit, a cleansing wind.*

The wind rises outside. The trees begin their anxious dance, the leaves showing their pale undersides, the ancient signal of approaching storm. I watch them from the window. I cannot read, cannot watch, cannot think in any direction except downward, but I can watch the trees, and this is something. The trees do not have memories. The trees do not remember last year's cyclone, or the one before, or the one that took the roof off the neighbor's house and deposited it in the canal. The trees simply bend. They do not read philosophy. They do not wish for blank slates. They are already blank, already enlightened, already the thing I am trying to become through all this futile wanting.

A drop of rain hits the glass. Then another. The sky is the color of old dishwater, the color of my thoughts, and for once there is no dissonance between inside and outside. The world has become my mood. Akvan has made landfall somewhere south of here, near Bhubaneswar, near Padmapur, somewhere that is not my skin but might as well be. The meteorologists speak of landfall and wind speed and storm surge, and I envy them their measurements. They have numbers for their catastrophe. They have categories. My depression has no landfall, no dissipation, no forecast track plotted in colored dots. It simply spins, and spins, and spins.

I should be afraid. Akvan will kill people. It will flood fields and drown livestock and turn the streets of coastal towns into rivers. I know this. I have seen the photographs from previous storms, the bloated cattle, the children on rooftops, the mud where houses used to be. And yet my sympathy is as non-stick as my attention. It slides off the surface of the world and pools in my own lap, a warm puddle of self-pity that I cannot mop up because the mop is also broken. This is the shame beneath the sadness, the second layer of hell: to know that others suffer more, and to be unable to care.

The rain begins in earnest now. The sound fills the room, fills the skull, a white noise that almost, almost drowns the memory of the thing I said in 1997. Almost. I sit in the grey light, in the grey mood, under the grey sky, and I wait for the eye of the storm to pass over, knowing that the eye brings not peace but the briefest pause before the wind reverses direction and tears everything down from the other side.

Akvan will weaken. The forecast says so. By the 20th of November, it will be a remnant low, a ghost of circulation, a memory of wind. I will still be here, in this chair, in this skull, waiting for my own forecast to trend downward, for my own pressure to rise, for the winds in my mind to drop below gale force. The meteorologists will archive Akvan. They will retire the name if the damage is sufficient, retire it the way we retire old gods, by forgetting them. No one will retire my depression. It is not a name but a condition, not a storm but a climate.

The rain continues. The non-stick pan sits in the kitchen, empty, clean, useless for cooking anything that requires sticking. I think of the cyclone's eye, that perfect circle of calm at the center of the spinning chaos, and I wonder if there is such a place in me, a still point where the memories do not reach, where the attention rests, where the blank slate exists without the necessity of death. Probably not. Probably the eye is just another illusion, another trick of perspective, another way the storm convinces you it has a center when it is all just wind.

I watch the trees bend. I watch the rain trace its sad mathematics down the glass. I do not read. I do not watch. I do not think, except in circles, which is not thinking at all but the simulation of it, a dog chasing its tail and calling it philosophy. Akvan spins toward the coast. I spin toward nothing. We are both depressions, both low-pressure systems, both gathering our strength from the warmth of things that will eventually cool and kill us. The difference is that Akvan will be remembered. I will simply be forgotten, one grey day among others, a man who could not read while the sky fell.
