The room looks the same, but the color has been quietly removed.

The chair remains. The cup remains. The window grill remains. The same Calcutta traffic continues outside, making its usual case against silence. Yet something has altered. Not dramatically. Not like a storm tearing branches from trees. More like a clerk has gone through the day with a grey stamp and marked every object: less.

Food becomes less. Music becomes less. News becomes less. Conversation becomes a task wearing shoes. The phone lights up and the body reacts as if a file has arrived from a department that never closes.

This is why I think of it as weather.

Weather does not ask permission. It enters the room through pressure, humidity, light, and skin. You can argue with it, but argument does not reduce the heat. You can call it irrational, but the air still presses down. Private weather behaves similarly. It is not a conclusion. It is a condition.

The danger is that the mind turns weather into law.

It says: because today is grey, life is grey. Because the morning is heavy, the future is heavy. Because desire has gone quiet, desire was always a fraud. The reasoning is bad, but it arrives with bodily force. Bad logic spoken by the body is harder to dismiss than bad logic spoken by a fool.

The city helps and does not help.

Calcutta is never neutral. It offers noise, heat, old buildings, stalls, buses, damp corners, and human presence at all hours. This can be comforting. It can also become too much. When the inner weather is low, even ordinary life feels overpopulated. You want the world nearby but not touching. You want evidence that life continues without needing to participate in every proof.

I have learned to distrust grand decisions made under that sky.

Do not decide your worth before breakfast. Do not rewrite your whole biography because the morning arrived without mercy. Do not treat a blank feeling as a philosophical discovery. Sometimes the mind is not revealing truth. Sometimes it is reporting bad internal weather with the arrogance of a national broadcast.

The practical work is embarrassingly small.

Wash the cup. Open the window. Eat something ordinary. Step outside for ten minutes if the body permits. Answer only the message that must be answered. Leave the rest of life unjudged for a while. A day can be lived without being interpreted to death.

None of this is heroic.

Heroism is too noisy for these mornings. The better word is maintenance. The same way a household survives by paying bills, clearing plates, checking switches, and repairing leaks before the ceiling gives up, a person sometimes survives by refusing to let one bad sky become the entire climate.

The color may not return at once.

But the chair is still a chair. The cup is still a cup. The street outside still goes on with its indifferent traffic. Somewhere a shop shutter rises. Somewhere water boils. Somewhere a fan turns because the switch, at least, has accepted its purpose.

The weather remains inside the room.

The room remains too.
