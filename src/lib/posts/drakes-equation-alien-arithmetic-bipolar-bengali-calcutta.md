The night sky above Calcutta is often too bright, too hazy, and too interrupted by cable wires to feel like a clean window into eternity.

Still, it does its work. It reminds a person that the city is not the whole room. Somewhere beyond the humidity, the traffic, the building lights, and the thin argument of the evening news, there are stars. Around many of those stars, planets. Around some of those planets, perhaps chemistry restless enough to become life.

Perhaps.

That word is the honest beginning.

The Drake equation is a dignified way of arranging ignorance. Frank Drake proposed it in 1961 to estimate the number of detectable technological civilizations in the Milky Way. It is not an alien-finding machine. It is a shelf system for uncertainty.

The equation is usually written:

$$N=R_* \times f_p \times n_e \times f_l \times f_i \times f_c \times L$$

$N$ is the number of civilizations we might detect. $R_*$ is the rate of star formation. $f_p$ is the fraction of stars with planets. $n_e$ is the number of potentially habitable planets per system. $f_l$ is the fraction where life begins. $f_i$ is the fraction where intelligence appears. $f_c$ is the fraction that develops detectable technology. $L$ is how long such a civilization remains detectable.

The first few pieces have become less mysterious. We now know planets are common. NASA's exoplanet resources list more than 6,000 confirmed worlds beyond our solar system. That is a profound change from Drake's time, when the existence of planets around other stars was still largely inference and hope.

But planets are only the first door.

How often does life begin? We have one confirmed example: Earth. One example is precious. It is also a poor statistical foundation for confidence. Human beings love building certainty from one case. Science keeps tugging the sleeve and saying, gently, no, not yet.

How often does life become intelligent? We do not know.

How often does intelligence build technology? We do not know.

How long does detectable technology last? That last question is where astronomy becomes a mirror.

A civilization might broadcast signals for a short time, then become quieter. It might hide. It might communicate in ways we do not recognize. It might never build the instruments we expect. Or it might fail to endure long enough for another civilization to notice.

This is where the Fermi paradox enters with its cold little question: where is everybody?

If the galaxy is old, large, and full of planets, why do we not see obvious evidence of other technological civilizations? No confirmed signals. No visible galactic engineering. No visitor asking for directions near Howrah. Nothing.

There are many possible answers. Life may be rare. Complex life may be rare. Intelligence may be rare. Technology may be rare. Distance may be too large. Timing may be cruel. Civilizations may be quiet. We may be looking in the wrong way. The universe may be biologically rich and conversationally poor.

The Great Filter makes the question darker. It asks whether there is a nearly impossible step between dead matter and long-lived technological civilization. If the hardest step is behind us, we are lucky. If it is ahead, the silence may be a warning.

That is not a reason for theatrical despair. It is a reason for humility.

The Rare Earth idea adds another caution. Simple life may be common while complex life requires a difficult sequence: stable star, right kind of planet, liquid water, protective conditions, enough time, and a chain of accidents that do not ruin the experiment. Ingredients are not enough. Timing matters. Sequence matters. Stability matters.

Sara Seager's biosignature framing shifts the target from civilizations to signs of life in atmospheres. Life may not send radio messages. It may only alter air. Oxygen, methane, or other gases in certain combinations can become clues, though chemistry is an unreliable witness and must be questioned carefully.

That is the adult pleasure of this whole subject.

It does not give a simple answer.

It improves the question.

In ordinary public life, certainty is cheap. People declare, forward, denounce, predict, and explain from three scraps of evidence and one mood. Drake's equation is useful because it slows the hand. Which unknown are you claiming to know? Planet formation? Life's origin? Intelligence? Technology? Survival?

Each variable is a locked door.

The sky remains silent so far. But silence is not automatically emptiness. We have searched a tiny part of a vast possibility space over a tiny slice of cosmic time. A person who dips a cup into the sea and finds no fish has not disproved fish.

So the honest position is restrained. Extraterrestrial life is plausible. Simple life may be more common than intelligence. Detectable civilizations remain deeply uncertain. The universe may contain many living worlds and still be almost impossible to converse with.

That thought is lonely and beautiful at once.

From this side of Calcutta's glow, I do not know whether anyone is out there.

I only know the question is too large for shouting.
