The first good hour arrives suspiciously, like a refund from an office that usually loses forms.

I do not trust it immediately. The room is the same. The money is not suddenly better. The future has not become obedient. Calcutta has not repaired itself overnight. The fan still turns with its old resignation. Yet something inside the day has loosened. Tea tastes like tea. A paragraph moves. The body is not a locked door. The mind, that unreliable municipality, has cleared one lane for traffic.

This is happiness after quicksand.

Not the grand happiness advertised by films, festivals, wellness gurus, or people with suspiciously clean kitchens. This is smaller. It is the happiness of not sinking for a while. Of standing on ordinary ground and noticing that ordinary ground is not a minor thing.

Quicksand is a useful image because panic makes it worse. The more violently one fights, the more the body is claimed. Many difficult periods in life have that texture. Bills, uncertainty, failed plans, public noise, private shame, the slow reduction of options: one struggles, then struggles against the struggle, and soon the whole person is flailing in mud.

A good hour does not rescue the life. It only interrupts the sinking.

But interruption has value. It proves the story was not complete. Yesterday's verdict may have sounded final, but today the cup is warm in the hand and a small appetite has returned. The evidence has changed, not dramatically, but enough.

I have learned not to make speeches during such hours. Happiness is easily frightened by management. If you ask it where it came from, how long it will stay, what its five-year plan is, and whether it can be converted into productivity, it may leave by the back door.

Better to let it sit.

Do one useful thing. Wash a plate. Answer one message. Read three pages. Move a file. Step onto the balcony. Resist the old habit of converting relief into ambition before the floor has dried.

There is a kind of gratitude that does not require theology. It is simply attention paid to reprieve. The room remains poor, the future remains unclear, but for an hour the internal ground holds.

Outside, the city sweats and bargains.

Inside, nothing glorious happens. That is the mercy.
