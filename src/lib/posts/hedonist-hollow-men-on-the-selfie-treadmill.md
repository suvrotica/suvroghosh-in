The phone rises before the moment has happened. Food arrives, and the camera arrives with it. A street, a shop, a visit, a kindness, a festival, an opinion, even tiredness itself: everything waits to be staged before it is lived.

The selfie is not the disease. It is the rash.

The fever underneath says: be seen before you become. Display before you understand. Consume before you can afford. Belong before you examine what you are joining. Be outraged before breakfast and certain by lunch.

Modern Indian public life has turned the self into a small roadside shrine. It gathers light, dust, captions, borrowed words, and nervous offerings from strangers who are also waiting for offerings. The result is not individuality. It is performance under pressure.

The new citizen is not merely living. He is producing evidence that he is living correctly. Food must prove taste. Clothes must prove status. A holiday must prove arrival. A child must prove parental worth. Politics must prove courage. Religion must prove purity. Anger must prove loyalty. Even kindness now often arrives with camera support.

The treadmill runs upward, but the incline is a pyramid. Not everyone can stand at the top. A status society needs people below, watching. A consumer dream needs those who can see the goods but not reach them. A social-media ladder needs invisible attention feeding visible lives.

The poor are told to aspire. The middle class are told to perform. The rich are told they deserve everything. Then everyone is told failure is personal.

That is a neat little cruelty.

Markets know how to mine insecurity. Tell people they are not attractive enough, pure enough, successful enough, modern enough, traditional enough, patriotic enough, spiritual enough, masculine enough, feminine enough. Then sell a repair kit: a cream, a course, a phone, a guru, a slogan, a party, a style, a tribe.

The age can turn emptiness into a subscription plan.

There is pleasure in visibility. Let us not pretend otherwise. Humans like being admired, and only a very dishonest person claims indifference to praise. But pleasure becomes danger when it becomes anesthesia. A posted dinner is fine. A life lived mainly as proof becomes thin.

Digital culture makes the old impulses faster. Earlier a foolish thought might die quietly between the skull and the tea cup. Now it can be typed, decorated, forwarded, defended, monetized, and turned into identity before evening. Earlier, public cruelty required effort. Now the screen supplies speed, audience, and distance.

Fatigue makes people easier to recruit. After traffic, bills, family pressure, heat, office drama, and the ordinary Indian obstacle course of getting one thing done, a person is not always looking for truth. He wants relief. Give him a villain and he may accept the discount.

This is not stupidity. It is exhaustion with a loudspeaker.

The repair will not be grand. It begins in less photogenic places: a meal not posted, a rumor not forwarded, a disagreement not turned into treason, a child not turned into a trophy, a poor person not treated as scenery, a national flag not used to cover laziness and cruelty.

Tiny refusals make civilization more than a performance.

The phone glows in the hand. It waits to turn the next moment into evidence. Sometimes the sane act is to leave the moment unproved.
