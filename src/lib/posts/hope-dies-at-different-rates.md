The old envelope stays on the table after the bill has been paid.

It is empty now, but the hand does not throw it away immediately. Some objects retain the weight of worry after the worry has moved on. Hope is like that too. It does not disappear all at once. It fades unevenly, by department.

One hope dies in money first. Another in work. Another in public life. Another in the private belief that effort will be recognized if only it remains sincere long enough. Some hopes are stubborn and vulgar, like weeds in a cracked wall. Some are delicate and perish at the first harsh invoice.

The difficulty is not only losing hope. It is not knowing which hope is dead and which is merely resting.

Calcutta trains a person in this ambiguity. A road repair may happen tomorrow, next year, or after a procession has made the pothole politically visible. A payment may come. A promise may be honored. The rain may cool the evening. The electricity may hold. The old machine may start. The mind becomes skilled at partial expectation.

Too much hope makes a fool. Too little makes a stone.

The art, if it is an art, is to keep hope small enough to carry. Not "everything will change." That is a festival banner. Rather: this paragraph may be written. This call may be answered. This week may not collapse. This one useful thing may still be done without applause.

Large hopes are expensive. They require a future capable of supporting them. Small hopes are cheaper and often more honest. They do not claim to redeem the life. They simply keep the next hour from closing.

I distrust inspirational talk because it treats hope as a moral duty. It is not. Sometimes hope is unavailable, like a shop shuttered in the afternoon. A person should not be insulted for finding the counter closed.

But if a small opening appears, one may use it.

The envelope remains on the table. I fold it once, then again, and put it with the waste paper.

Something in me continues, though not loudly.
