"How are you?" is not a question. It is a tiny social trap, polished with kindness and carried around by perfectly decent people who have no idea what they are asking.

It arrives with a smile. Three harmless words. A soft little knock on the door of the self. And immediately a committee assembles inside the skull.

How honest may we be?

How much damage can this room tolerate?

How quickly can we produce a socially edible answer?

Nobody asks "How are you?" because they want a full report. They ask because conversation has customs, and one of the customs is that before we exchange information we must first exchange counterfeit evidence of normalcy. Produce wellness. Produce manageability. Produce the face that says the inner plumbing is functioning, the lights are on, the ledgers are balanced, and no private roof has collapsed since lunch.

Honesty is therefore difficult.

What am I supposed to say? That I am operating within acceptable external limits while the internal accounts department is still reconciling several decades of disputed entries? That the structure is standing, though the walls have begun holding private meetings about collapse? That I am ambulatory, caffeinated, underfunded, and not yet a public inconvenience?

This would be more accurate than "fine."

It would also be unforgivable.

The truth, introduced too quickly into small talk, behaves like a leaking pipe in a drawing room. People admire honesty in principle. In practice, they look for a towel and an exit. Friends remember appointments. Relatives look wounded, as if one's actual condition has shown poor manners. Office people retreat toward spreadsheets. Nobody wants the interior audit. They want the weather bulletin.

Mild. Manageable. Carry on.

So I say fine.

Fine is a damp little rag of a word. A universal bandage applied to wounds nobody intends to inspect. Fine means the building has not fallen in public. Fine means the rot remains private. Fine means the afternoon may proceed without witnesses.

Even lying requires craftsmanship, and this is where the problem becomes technical. Say fine too flatly and people smell smoke. Say it too brightly and they suspect a hostage situation. Say it with irony and someone may ask a follow-up question, which is how civilizations weaken. The correct fine must be tuned carefully: not cheerful, not bitter, not hollow, not theatrical. A slight lift, a small exhalation, one polite flicker of the eyes. Enough to satisfy the customs officer of conversation.

Class enters here with its old bag of instructions.

The lower-middle-class Bengali condition is a mezzanine existence. Too educated to be innocent, too insecure to be free, too proud to beg, too short of money to be eccentric. One grows up among books, exam results, moral lectures, borrowed furniture, and the suggestion that with discipline, English, and correct posture, life will improve.

Then life, with excellent timing, becomes more complicated.

The lower-middle-class person knows many words and still worries about cooking oil. He can discuss probability, empire, cricket, Tagore, public systems, technology, and the collapse of institutions, but cannot answer "How are you?" without feeling he has been asked to justify his entire presence on earth.

Middle age adds the audit.

Youthful ambition, once a charming fever, is reclassified as evidence of poor forecasting. Dreams become documents. Hope becomes a suspicious transaction. The future, once waving from a distance, now sits across the table with reading glasses and asks what exactly has been achieved.

Not enough, apparently.

And still the world asks, "How are you?" as if the answer lives in tone rather than archaeology.

What is really being asked is: can you confirm that your private weather will not inconvenience this interaction? The expected answer is yes. Yes, my disappointment is packed safely. Yes, my biography will not leak onto your shoes. Yes, I understand the terms of participation and will now produce the agreed sound.

Fine.

The strange thing is that the question is not evil. The people asking it are not necessarily cruel. Most are obeying choreography. They too have unsaid answers behind their teeth. They too have mornings when the ceiling looks accusatory. They too have learned that honesty must be rationed.

Knowing this does not make the question less irritating.

One can answer "surviving," but that has too much dignity and invites interpretation. "Could be better" smells of complaint. "Same old" is acceptable but walks too close to truth. The safest technique is the full exchange of mutual evasion:

"How are you?"

"Good, good. You?"

There it is. Two damaged mammals in decent clothing, exchanging harmless noises so the afternoon can continue.

Maybe this is not hypocrisy. Maybe it is crowd management. If everyone answered fully, buses would stop, offices would empty, family lunches would require committees, and casual encounters near the pharmacy would become tribunals. Small talk is a drainage system. It prevents the whole interior city from flooding the street.

But drainage systems also smell.

So when someone asks how I am, I stand for one second before the little gate of etiquette. A reckless member of the inner committee proposes honesty and is removed. Another suggests cheerfulness and is distrusted. Silence is considered and rejected for poor employability. The old bureaucratic compromise wins.

Fine.

Out it comes. Small. Bland. Safe.

The world, satisfied, moves on.

Meanwhile I remain what I was before the question arrived: middle-aged, underlit, over-examined, Bengali, tired, comic against my will, standing in the corridor of ordinary life with one weak bulb and no receipt.

How am I?

Unanswerable, obviously.

But thank you for asking.
