The new ambition often arrives overdressed. A title before there is craft. A brand before there is work. A public declaration before the first private discipline has survived a dull Tuesday.

What gets called ambition in modern India is often status anxiety under theatrical lighting.

Not excellence. Not devotion to a difficult object. Not the long unglamorous labor by which a person becomes good at something. Something narrower: a public struggle against invisibility.

The fear is not mediocrity in the honest human sense. Most lives are ordinary, mixed, useful in patches, foolish in patches, and not destined for a stadium. The feared condition is social mediocrity: the humiliation of not being visibly ahead, not being nameable as impressive, not carrying a badge that travels well through relatives, classmates, neighbors, and digital feeds.

So work becomes costume.

The person may not want to write as much as to be known as a writer. Not build as much as inhabit the founder costume. Not study a subject deeply as much as possess the credential. Not serve a real need as much as become socially protected from the accusation of being ordinary.

This is not uniquely Indian, but India adds pressure: scale, exam culture, class fear, family comparison, English hierarchy, migration dreams, and a crowded social world where obscurity can feel like a verdict. Families often do this from fear, not cruelty. They want children to be safe. Unfortunately, the language of safety becomes ranking.

Legibility matters. Quiet competence has a public-relations problem. It does not always compress into a headline, a wedding introduction, a profile update, or a parental boast. Spectacle travels better. Drama travels better. Prestige objects travel better.

That is how the thing itself disappears.

The work should be the center. The code must run. The bridge must hold. The diagnosis must be sound. The prose must clarify. The research must survive scrutiny. The classroom must teach. The process must endure contact with reality.

But in costume ambition, the hidden question becomes: what identity does this work let me wear?

That question produces performers, not practitioners.

Social media pours fuel on it because it collapses the distance between self and audience. Every act gains a second purpose: how will this look? The result is a culture full of aspirants but not always apprentices. Everyone wishes to arrive. Fewer people wish to submit themselves to the humiliating repetitive process by which arrival becomes deserved.

The sadness is that much of this is fear. Fear of financial descent. Fear of disappointing family. Fear of becoming invisible in a market that sorts people without mercy. Fear that if one is not impressive, one is nothing.

That fear deserves compassion. It does not deserve obedience.

Recognition is useful, but it cannot become sovereign. Public validation is a rental property with harsh terms. It must be renewed constantly. Someone younger, louder, richer, better connected, or simply more shameless is always arriving. If self-respect depends on applause, success does not calm the person. It sensitizes him.

A healthier ambition is object-centered. It begins with the work itself and accepts obscurity as part of incubation. Many worthwhile things are done for long stretches by people nobody is clapping for: engineers keeping systems alive, careful teachers, exacting researchers, disciplined workers, quiet craftspeople.

That is not mediocrity.

It is the condition under which real competence grows.

The work waits on the table, plain and resistant. It does not care what costume we wear. That is why it can still save us from performance.
