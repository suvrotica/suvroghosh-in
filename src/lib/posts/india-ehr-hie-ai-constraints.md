The file is still paper because paper has no onboarding meeting.

That is one reason EHRs struggle in India. Paper is weak, lossy, fragile, and often illegible. It is also cheap, portable, socially understood, and compatible with chaos. A patient can carry it from a neighborhood clinic to a diagnostic center to a specialist to a hospital desk without anyone agreeing on software, workflow, contracts, or data standards.

An Electronic Health Record asks for much more.

It asks doctors to document in structured ways. It asks hospitals to invest before the benefit is obvious. It asks staff to change habits. It asks vendors to support local complexity. It asks institutions to care about data reuse beyond immediate billing and survival. It asks a fragmented system to behave as if continuity is a shared obligation.

That is a large request in India.

Health Information Exchange asks for even more. HIE requires identity matching, consent, governance, standard terminologies, facility registries, security, audit trails, and institutional trust. It requires organizations to share information with other organizations that may be competitors, strangers, or bureaucratic adversaries. It requires the state to define rules clearly and enforce them fairly. It requires data to mean something after it crosses a boundary.

The technical problem is hard. The organizational problem is harder.

India's healthcare landscape is uneven: corporate hospitals, small nursing homes, solo practices, public hospitals, diagnostic chains, informal care, cash payments, insurance islands, and a patient population moving through all of it without one stable digital corridor. A polished architecture diagram can make this look like a missing app. It is not. It is a missing settlement among institutions.

This is why AI diagnostics may spread differently.

Some AI tools do not require full EHR maturity to offer value. An image model can read a scan. A screening tool can triage a narrow problem. A voice or symptom tool can assist front-line routing. A decision-support module can work inside one workflow without waiting for national interoperability. These tools can attach to islands.

That does not make them safe by default.

An AI diagnostic tool can still fail through biased training data, poor validation, bad workflow fit, overtrust, weak audit, unclear liability, language gaps, and commercial pressure. But its adoption path may be narrower than the path required for full EHR and HIE infrastructure. It can enter through devices, labs, telehealth, radiology centers, insurance programs, government pilots, or specialty workflows.

This creates a strange possibility: India may adopt AI pieces before it builds the data spine those pieces ideally need.

That can produce useful shortcuts. It can also produce a dangerous patchwork. A model may classify an image well but have no longitudinal context. A triage system may suggest urgency without seeing prior care. A screening app may generate referrals into systems that cannot absorb them. A tool may create new data without a trustworthy place to put it.

AI can route around missing infrastructure for a while. It cannot abolish the need for infrastructure.

In Calcutta, a narrow lane can carry traffic that planners did not imagine. People adjust, reverse, shout, wait, improvise, and somehow the handcart passes the scooter. That does not mean the lane is a highway. It means humans are very good at surviving bad design until the cost becomes normal.

India's AI path in healthcare may look like that.

The sensible view is neither pessimism nor worship. EHR and HIE struggle because they require continuity, governance, incentives, and meaning across institutions. AI diagnostics may spread faster because they can begin as focused tools inside narrower workflows. But if they are not eventually connected to better records, audit, provenance, and follow-up, they will become yet another clever layer above a weak floor.

The file may remain paper for longer than the slogan admits.

The model may arrive anyway.
