The television is too loud for the room, but nobody lowers it, because in India volume has begun to pass for seriousness.

There is a man on the screen with a fixed expression of national emergency. There is another man waiting to interrupt him. There is a panel, a ticker, a soundtrack, a flag somewhere in the visual arrangement, and a subject that was once a public problem but has now been converted into heat. The room in Calcutta grows smaller under the noise. The tea cools. The actual question disappears.

This is the machine.

It does not rule only by telling lies. That would be too easy to defeat. It rules by exhausting judgment. It keeps the citizen angry, proud, frightened, entertained, accused, vindicated, suspicious, and busy. It gives him a fresh enemy before he has finished thinking about the old bill. It turns unemployment into attitude, price rise into sacrifice, corruption into whataboutery, and cruelty into cultural confidence.

The small man under it does not need a perfect theory of propaganda. He feels it in his body.

He wanted a road repaired. He received a debate on civilization.

He wanted a job. He received a lecture on national greatness.

He wanted the police to behave fairly. He received a panel discussion about enemies within.

Noise is useful because it prevents scale. A person who can compare the size of a problem with the size of the response becomes dangerous. He may ask why a bridge failed, why a reporter is afraid, why a public contract smells arranged, why a minority neighbor has lowered his voice, why a student's question is treated like rebellion, why a political donor is treated like a public-spirited cloud.

Noise rushes in before these questions can stand up.

It says: look there.

Then there.

Then there.

Never here.

India was never a spotless republic. It has always carried corruption, caste arrogance, religious injury, patronage, bureaucratic delay, dynastic privilege, police excess, and the ancient administrative art of making a simple matter require three counters and a person who is never available. The difference now is not that hypocrisy exists. The difference is that shamelessness has learned stagecraft.

It wears better clothes.

It hires better sound.

It makes wrongdoing look like confidence.

Much of the media ecosystem helps because outrage is cheaper than reporting. Real reporting needs documents, time, legal risk, fieldwork, and the stubborn habit of asking a question after the spokesperson has performed injury. Outrage needs a camera, a prompt, and a willingness to treat the public as a captive audience.

Reporters Without Borders ranked India 151 out of 180 countries in the 2025 World Press Freedom Index and 157 out of 180 in 2026. Rankings can be debated. Methodologies should be read carefully. But when a country keeps appearing in a worrying part of the table, defensiveness is not enough. The question is not whether every foreign index is sacred. The question is whether Indian journalists can investigate power without expecting lawsuits, threats, raids, economic pressure, or organized abuse.

The answer, too often, is complicated in the worst way.

A democracy needs a press that irritates rulers. It needs facts that do not wait for party permission. It needs editors who still know the difference between caution and surrender. When the press becomes a noise amplifier, the citizen loses one of his last public instruments for seeing through fog.

Then majoritarian politics steps in and offers emotional wages.

This phrase is ugly because the bargain is ugly. When real wages are weak, when services fail, when the young wait too long, when the small trader feels squeezed, when the household lives one emergency away from panic, pride can be offered as substitute income. You may not get a secure job, but you can be told you belong to a victorious community. You may not get a working hospital, but you can be told your civilization has risen. You may not get justice, but you can get the warm little poison of superiority.

It is a terrible bargain.

It is also politically efficient.

Ordinary faith is not the problem. The quiet ritual before breakfast, the festival, the household offering, the memory of ancestors, the small gestures by which people arrange meaning around uncertainty - these are part of life. The problem begins when faith is turned into a badge of dominance and citizenship becomes graded by cultural obedience. The problem begins when a neighbor is asked to prove belonging while the loudest men claim ownership of the country.

That kind of politics does not strengthen a nation. It makes the nation narrow enough to be managed by slogans.

The small man is told to clap.

He may clap. Not always because he believes. Sometimes because silence is safer. Sometimes because his workplace is listening. Sometimes because his family WhatsApp group has become an unpaid surveillance committee of public emotion. Sometimes because fatigue is stronger than argument.

But a part of him keeps count.

He notices which questions are never asked on television. He notices which scandals vanish quickly. He notices which arrests are celebrated and which are explained. He notices which leader's relative becomes talented at the correct moment. He notices which rich defaulter receives elegant vocabulary and which poor defaulter receives notice. He notices which community is expected to be patient with insult.

Judgment does not always speak immediately.

Sometimes it stores.

The corruption underneath the noise is not only cash. Cash is merely the old visible form. Modern corruption also moves through permission, policy, selective enforcement, public contracts, advertising pressure, data access, investigation timing, institutional silence, and the soft conversion of independent bodies into decorative architecture. A country can have elections, courts, media, commissions, and rules, and still change in texture if every institution learns the correct direction in which to bend.

This is how democratic life becomes thin while democratic ritual remains thick.

The signboard stays.

The queue stays.

The inked finger stays.

The citizen's power after voting day becomes negotiable.

At street level, the noise machine produces a particular loneliness. The citizen sees something wrong, but the screen tells him the wrong thing is actually glory. He watches a public lie become consensus through repetition. He hears cruelty described as strength. He sees fear described as discipline. After a while, he may doubt his own eyes.

That is the most serious damage.

Not misinformation alone. Not propaganda alone. The real injury is the weakening of the ordinary person's confidence in his own perception. He sees the drain. The screen says the city is transformed. He sees the jobless graduate. The speech says youth are empowered. He sees a reporter being targeted. The panel says national interest. He sees hatred rewarded. The slogan says unity.

A citizen who cannot trust his eyes becomes easier to rule.

So the first civic act is modest: keep the eyes open.

This does not mean becoming heroic. Most people cannot live like full-time dissidents. They have rent, health expenses, school fees, work pressure, family obligations, a phone bill, a gas cylinder, and a body that grows tired. But they can refuse the cheapest confusion. They can refuse to forward poison. They can ask for evidence. They can distinguish faith from bullying, patriotism from obedience, journalism from performance, development from publicity, and leadership from volume.

Small acts, yes.

But public reason is built out of small acts repeated by ordinary people who are not famous enough to be protected.

In Calcutta at night, when the television finally quiets and the lane returns to its usual sounds, the country looks less like a grand argument and more like a tired household. A fan turns. Someone counts money. Someone studies. Someone checks medicine prices. Someone wonders whether the next form will be accepted. The noise machine has gone to sleep for a few hours.

The small man is still awake.

He has not solved anything.

But he has not fully handed over his judgment either.
