The chip in the phone was invisible, which is how power often travels. A finger touched glass in Calcutta, a message opened, a payment went through, a map turned, and somewhere inside the device a tiny industrial civilization did its work without asking for applause.

That invisibility is why semiconductor talk so easily becomes slogan.

India should make chips. India should become self-reliant. India should not depend on foreign supply chains for every crucial component in phones, cars, defense systems, telecom equipment, satellites, medical devices, factories, power infrastructure, and ordinary machines that now contain more computation than yesterday's offices.

All of that is true. It is also the beginning of the problem, not the solution.

A semiconductor ecosystem is not one factory. It is a nervous system of design, electronic design automation tools, intellectual property blocks, fabrication, gases, chemicals, wafers, photomasks, deposition, etching, inspection, metrology, packaging, testing, substrate supply, logistics, water, power, talent, capital, customers, standards, and yield learning.

Yield is the humbling word. It means how many chips come out usable after all that precision. A fab can exist and still lose money if yield is poor. A press release can be perfect while the line is still learning how not to waste silicon.

This is why semiconductor sufficiency cannot be built by patriotic mood alone. The mood may help. The real work is dull, layered, expensive, and repetitive. The machine must be tuned. The workers must be trained. The suppliers must arrive. The power must stay stable. The water must be clean. The customer must trust delivery. The same error must be removed a thousand times without turning the factory into a speech.

India does have real advantages. It has engineers. It has a large electronics market. It has software depth. It has design talent already working in global chip companies. It has strategic urgency, because the world has learned that the chip supply chain is not merely business. It is geopolitics, manufacturing, energy, trade, and national security in one narrow slice of silicon.

But design strength is not fabrication strength. They touch each other, but they are not the same muscle.

The most sensible route is not to chase glamour first. Not every useful chip is at the bleeding edge. Cars, appliances, power electronics, industrial control systems, telecom equipment, sensors, and many embedded devices need reliable mature nodes. A country can become stronger by producing boring chips well.

Boring is underrated in national development.

Advanced packaging also matters more than public conversation admits. Packaging is not merely wrapping. It connects chips to the outside world, manages heat, protects the die, enables performance, and increasingly allows multiple chiplets to behave like a larger system. As the easy geometry gains slow down, packaging becomes part of performance itself.

Assembly, testing, marking, and packaging, often abbreviated as ATMP, is the final industrial half that turns delicate silicon into a component a customer can actually use. That does not sound glamorous until one remembers that untested brilliance is not a product.

The danger is lip service from every side. Government may enjoy announcements. Companies may enjoy incentives. Commentators may enjoy maps with arrows. Citizens may enjoy the phrase self-reliance because it feels sturdy in the mouth.

None of this will etch a wafer.

India needs the humility to build a stack. A mature-node fab that runs reliably. Packaging facilities that improve every year. Design houses with customers beyond demonstration slides. University programs that understand fabrication instead of treating it as imported magic. Supplier networks that learn quality. Clean rooms that remain clean. Engineers who stay long enough to accumulate factory memory.

And yes, public money. There is no serious semiconductor race without state support. The question is not whether the state should help. The question is whether help becomes disciplined industrial capacity or a subsidy parade.

From Calcutta, the semiconductor question looks less like a moonshot and more like plumbing. The pipe must connect. The pressure must hold. The joints must not leak. The household does not care how beautiful the plan was if water never arrives.

India should make chips. It should say so plainly. Then it should stop confusing the sentence with the factory.
