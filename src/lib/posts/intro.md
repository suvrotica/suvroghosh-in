Acronyms used: AI means Artificial Intelligence. COBOL means Common Business-Oriented Language. CSS means Cascading Style Sheets. D3 means Data-Driven Documents. FORTRAN means Formula Translation. HTML5 means HyperText Markup Language 5. IBM means International Business Machines. IT means Information Technology. OPNET means Optimized Network Engineering Tools. SAS means Statistical Analysis System. SQL means Structured Query Language. SPSS means Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. UML means Unified Modeling Language. VBA means Visual Basic for Applications. Y2K means Year 2000.

There is an old kind of programming notebook that looks more serious than the machine it was meant to command. Ruled pages, block letters, arrows, tables, crossings-out. In Calcutta, before software became a lifestyle word, code often arrived like that: on paper first, in the mind next, and only later in front of a blinking cursor.

My technical life began in that older air.

At Jadavpur University in the mid-1990s, I learned Pascal, FORTRAN, COBOL, C, and C++. That list now looks like a small museum shelf, but it was not nostalgia then. It was training in translation. Each language forced a different shape of thought. Pascal disciplined structure. C exposed memory and pointers. FORTRAN carried the smell of numerical work. COBOL showed how business logic can become a civilization of records. C++ added ambition and danger in equal measure.

Digital logic and relational databases mattered just as much. SQL, tables, keys, joins, and normalization taught me that data is never merely stored. It is argued into shape. A bad schema is not just an implementation detail. It becomes a long-term tax on understanding.

In 2000, at the University of Texas at San Antonio, that habit widened. My master's work pulled me toward C, C++, Proto-C, OPNET, and the simulation of ad hoc wireless protocols. Distributed systems were not a fashionable metaphor to me. They were technical problems with packets, delays, routes, assumptions, and failure modes. My thesis work around SQL Server, distributed databases, and parallel computing deepened the same lesson: the interesting question is rarely where one object sits. It is how many imperfect parts coordinate without lying too much.

Y2K-era Austin brought another education. At Surgient Networks, I worked around video caching behavior and Zipf's law, the strange pattern by which a small number of items attract a large amount of demand while the long tail continues breathing in the background. That idea has followed me everywhere. Popularity, workload, attention, clinical events, support tickets, traffic, illness, complaints: the world is rarely evenly distributed.

In 2002, at UTHSCSA, healthcare research changed the scale of the problem again. Clinical data is not tidy because human life is not tidy. Computational genetics, tree and string manipulation, machine learning before the present wave of hype, data cleaning, provenance, cohorts, trial records, reporting, and research support all taught me that data work is partly engineering and partly moral patience.

I worked as a DBA and systems analyst in clinical research environments where the database was not an abstract backend. It carried studies, patients, labs, forms, queries, exports, and deadlines. SQL Server administration, ETL, Crystal Reports, Tableau, Excel, Python, SPSS, SAS, and related tools were not badges. They were different instruments for the same work: make the messy thing usable without pretending it was born clean.

Later, in Auckland, after teaching at the University of Fiji, I worked as a SQL Server administrator and Crystal Reports programmer at Taxicharge. That shift may look odd from outside: healthcare research to transport and payments. To me, the connection was obvious. Systems still had to move records, reconcile events, report accurately, and survive daily use by people who had no patience for conceptual elegance if the report was wrong.

Back in India, web development for hospitals and clinical trial operations brought the work closer to the screen. HTML5, CSS, JavaScript, APIs, UML, testing, privacy concerns, and operational workflows mattered because healthcare software sits between pressure and consequence. A poorly designed interface does not merely annoy someone. It can slow work that already happens under strain.

The telemedicine architecture work pushed that thinking further. A cloud virtual browser model, using a headless browser in AWS, first with Gecko and then WebKit, made me think about how clinical interaction could be delivered through constrained infrastructure. It was never only about code. It was about access, latency, security, interface control, and what happens when a tool has to function under real-world Indian conditions.

Consulting work added D3, Orange, Python, analytics, dashboards, and the usual awkward business of turning vague requests into something testable. Ride-hailing projects followed, including work with Chennai in 2017 and Kolkata in 2020 using Onde from Belarus and Taxi Butler from the Netherlands. Again, the domain changed but the system questions remained: dispatch, demand, location, incentives, payments, support, failure, and the gap between what the software believes and what the street does.

If there is a theme in all this, it is translation.

Translation between clinicians and databases. Between managers and developers. Between legacy systems and new interfaces. Between academic models and operational reality. Between what a client says and what the system must actually do. Management, in this sense, is not bossing people around. It is semantic damage reduction. It is noticing where words have begun to drift apart before the project collapses under polite misunderstanding.

Now the AI stack has made that translation problem larger and stranger: Python for machine learning and data engineering, SQL and vector platforms, cloud systems, APIs, RAG, embeddings, evaluation, orchestration, and agentic workflows. The language has changed, but the discipline has not. A model still needs context. A workflow still needs ownership. A result still needs evaluation. A system still fails at the boundary where assumptions meet use.

So this blog is not a formal resume. It is the working notebook after many machines, cities, databases, reports, clinics, taxis, and half-successful attempts to explain one system to another. The line from Pascal in Jadavpur to AI systems in Calcutta is not straight. But it is real.
