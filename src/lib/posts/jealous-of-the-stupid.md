The confidently ignorant man at the tea stall often has a shine on his face that the educated man cannot purchase.

It is not intelligence. It is not even happiness exactly. It is the glow of a world that still speaks in simple sentences. A dream means warning. A twitching eye means money. A forwarded video means research. A planet somewhere has taken personal interest in his business prospects.

And I am jealous.

That is the embarrassing part.

Knowledge is sold to children as liberation. In many ways it is. Understanding germs, statistics, history, physics, incentives, and evidence removes many traps. But it also removes the sweetness of easy mystery. Once you know how explanations are made, wonder becomes more demanding. The unknown is no longer a sacred fog. It becomes a room with a locked door, and somewhere there is probably a paper, a dataset, a diagram, or a tired specialist who can explain it.

Even the stars become paperwork if you stare long enough.

As a child, the world had drawers and adults seemed to hold keys. Electricity felt magical. The moon seemed to follow. Trains carried whole stories. Rain on dust was not chemistry but arrival. The unknown was not yet a syllabus.

Then knowledge entered with tools.

It showed mechanisms. It showed interest, power, money, bacteria, class, bad incentives, and historical dirt under cultural poetry. It gave eyesight, then removed the chair. A flower could still be beautiful, but now evolution stood behind it. A political speech could still move a crowd, but now one could smell the contract under the slogan.

This is not always pleasant.

In India, knowing is useful only when it can be monetized, laminated, worshipped, or used to help someone's application. Real knowing asks irritating questions. What is the evidence? Who benefits? What is the denominator? What mechanism is being hidden under the phrase "tradition"? These questions are not welcome at family gatherings, offices, or many public platforms. They make people feel inspected.

Money changes the reception. A wealthy fool is eccentric. A poor careful thinker is negative. A rich person's vague idea becomes strategy. A poor person's precise objection becomes attitude.

This is why ignorance can look warm from a distance.

The ignorant person may have a world full of signs, omens, blessings, invisible assistance, cosmic messages, and neat causes. The skeptical person has mechanisms, probabilities, structural failure, and the knowledge that many things go wrong for no narratively satisfying reason. Mechanism is useful. It is not always comforting.

Still, public ignorance is dangerous. It burns trust, spreads fake cures, elects frauds, worships noise, fears evidence, and demands science only after superstition has failed. At scale, ignorance is not charming. It enters the water supply of public life.

But there is a smaller private unknowing I miss.

One cupboard kingdom.

One moon that follows without explanation.

One evening where a sound in the wall does not become construction defect, rodent, pipe pressure, trapped air, or landlord negligence.

The mind, once spoiled by explanation, rarely becomes whole again. It keeps sweeping the room. It keeps asking. It keeps detecting the damp under the fresh paint. That is useful, and sometimes it is lonely.

Below the window, someone laughs into a phone, untroubled by history, economics, class, decay, or the secret machinery of his own certainty. For a second, I envy the ease.

Then the old mind returns with its little broom.

It starts sweeping again.
