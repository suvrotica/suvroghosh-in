The screwdriver wrapped in old tape is not the problem.

A poor household repairing a fan with wire, tape, and patience older than the republic is not the villain of the story. A nurse finding a humane workaround when a printer fails, a mechanic coaxing motion out of an elderly scooter, a clerk helping a person trapped in a database error: these are often acts of intelligence under scarcity. Real life is not a standards manual. Poverty has always forced people to become engineers of inconvenience.

In Bengali, jogar has that old, practical smell of making do. In Hindi, jugaad carries a similar warmth when it means improvisation under constraint.

The rot begins when improvisation escapes necessity and becomes privilege.

Then jugaad is no longer ingenuity. It becomes greed wearing the mask of cleverness. Do not learn the system, game it. Do not improve the process, bypass it. Do not build competence, cultivate access. Do not produce value, capture clearance. Do not ask whether the bridge will stand; ask whether the file has moved. Do not ask whether the patient is safe; ask whether the form has been signed.

The nation becomes a backstage pass, except the backstage is also on fire.

This is why the word is slippery. Jugaad can mean creativity, resilience, corruption, emergency repair, bribery, networking, manipulation, or cheerful sabotage, depending on who is speaking and who is paying. The poor person's jugaad is survival. The powerful person's jugaad is extraction. Confusing the two is one of India's most profitable moral mistakes.

The Indian version is exhausting because corruption has fused with improvisational pride. We do not merely cut corners; we celebrate the cutter as a genius. We do not merely evade rules; we produce folk philosophy about why rules are foolish. We do not merely tolerate shoddy work; we admire the speed with which shoddiness can be delivered, photographed, and defended as vision.

There is a small fatal difference between flexibility and lawlessness.

Flexibility understands the purpose of a rule and adjusts when conditions change. Lawlessness treats every rule as an obstacle placed by fools for the benefit of cowards. In a healthy system, exceptions are documented, reviewed, and learned from. In a jugaad system, exceptions become the business model.

The deepest damage is epistemic. Jugaad without understanding destroys the relationship between action and knowledge. A competent engineer knows why a structure holds. A competent clinician knows why a dose is dangerous. A competent architect knows why an interface breaks when meaning is smuggled through a field designed only for transport. But the greedy improviser knows whom to call, whom to flatter, whom to threaten, whom to pay, and when to disappear.

Knowledge becomes secondary to maneuver.

Once that happens, error stops teaching. In a serious system, failure produces evidence. Something cracks, someone investigates, a design assumption is revised, a control is added, an owner is named. In a jugaad system, failure produces fog. Contractor blames subcontractor, officer blames predecessor, report becomes technical, public attention moves on, another tender appears.

Accountability is not always defeated by one grand conspiracy. It is nibbled to death by practical men.

This is why many Indian data failures are not merely data-quality failures. The record says the road was completed, the bridge inspected, the beneficiary served, the school functional, the grievance resolved. On paper, reality has behaved nicely. But representation is not reality. It is often a bureaucratic portrait painted under pressure by people who know what the dashboard wants.

The lie may be structurally valid.

Healthcare teaches this with brutal clarity. A hospital system may record discharge, appointment, consent, or follow-up. The message moves. The field is populated. The report looks complete. But did the person understand the instruction? Could the household act on it? Did the next clinician receive meaning or merely a code? Transport is not meaning. A message can move flawlessly and still misrepresent the world.

Indian civic systems suffer from the same disease at national scale.

The middle class complains about corruption while asking for a contact. It curses nepotism while forwarding resumes through influence. It mocks the queue while searching for a side door. It demands rule of law until the rule touches its own building extension, school admission, tax notice, passport verification, or parking violation.

This is not theatrical hypocrisy. It is adaptation. People imitate the system that punishes innocence and rewards navigation.

The tragedy is that local adaptation can be collectively ruinous. If everyone else is bribing, delaying, faking, flattering, and gaming, the honest person becomes exposed. So the decent citizen learns to be practical. Practical becomes one of the most dangerous words in the country. It often means: surrender the principle before the principle inconveniences someone stronger.

Bad governance then enters ordinary life. A weak inspection regime becomes a cracked wall. A politicized police station becomes a household strategy. A broken municipal office becomes a broker economy. A corrupt procurement chain becomes a hospital without reliable supplies. A collapsed education system becomes coaching-center feudalism. A captured regulator becomes a poisoned river.

The public does not merely experience bad governance.

It internalizes the architecture of bad governance as common sense.

There is also a class hierarchy hidden in the language. When the poor improvise, they are called informal. When the middle class improvises, it is called networking. When the rich improvise, it is called strategy. When the state improvises, it is called reform. The moral vocabulary changes with air-conditioning.

Jugaad politics does not need citizens. It needs clients. It offers shortcuts instead of rights, favors instead of institutions, spectacle instead of repair. A functioning right can be claimed by anyone. A favor must be begged for, remembered, and repaid. That is why the favor is politically superior. It keeps the citizen small and the patron large.

The corporate world is not innocent. Too much managerial culture confuses urgency with intelligence and firefighting with leadership. A team works nights to fix a crisis caused by bad planning, and the crisis manager becomes a hero. Documentation is treated as decoration. Testing is compressed. Security is patched later. Governance is resented until the audit arrives. The same organization that cannot maintain a clean source of truth will announce AI ambitions with ceremonial confidence.

That is not resilience.

It is chronic technical debt with a patriotic soundtrack.

True resilience is designed before the shock. It has redundancy, maintenance budgets, trained staff, boring checklists, escalation paths, spare parts, and people empowered to stop the line. Jugaad resilience often means discovering that nothing was maintained and then praising the person who found rope.

India does not need less improvisation. It needs disciplined improvisation bounded by ethics, competence, and traceability. Emergency adaptation should be allowed, but it must leave a trail. Exceptions should expire. Workarounds should be reviewed. Public systems should distinguish between temporary relief and permanent theft.

To preach legality without repairing the legal path is only comfort-class aerobics. Many rules are stupid. Many offices are predatory. Many citizens use shortcuts because the formal route behaves like punishment.

But the impossibility of a clean solution is not an excuse for dirt.

Reduce unnecessary permissions. Publish process data. Protect whistleblowers. Fund inspection capacity. Punish professional negligence, not only petty bribery. Separate service delivery from political patronage. Make maintenance more honorable than inauguration. Treat documentation as infrastructure. Make dashboards auditable by people outside the department that produces them.

Above all, stop praising the bypass as genius.

A better India would still have jogar in the old humane sense. The household fix would remain. The roadside repair would remain. The exhausted worker's small act of rescue would remain. But the bridge would not be jugaad. The exam would not be jugaad. The oxygen supply would not be jugaad. The land record, lab result, police complaint, procurement file, and discharge summary would not depend on someone knowing someone.

The test of a civilization is not whether clever people can survive broken systems. Clever people usually can. The test is whether an ordinary person can live without becoming a broker, supplicant, liar, amateur lawyer, unofficial project manager, and part-time cynic.

India has too often mistaken forced adaptability for national strength.

It is not strength when people become ingenious because the floor keeps collapsing.

It is a warning.
