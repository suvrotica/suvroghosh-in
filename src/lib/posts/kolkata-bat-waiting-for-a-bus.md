The bus arrives already full, which in Kolkata means the negotiation has only begun.

Nobody believes the laws of space. People adjust, compress, fold, angle, plead, accuse, and enter. One hand finds a rod. One foot finds a ledge. A bag becomes public property. A shoulder becomes infrastructure. The conductor moves through the impossible geometry with the professional calm of a man who has seen Euclid fail before breakfast.

This is not merely transport. It is political theory with sweat.

Every crowded bus teaches the same lesson: a bad system can continue functioning long after dignity has left. The route exists. The fare is collected. People reach work. Therefore, on paper, the system works. The paper is pleased with itself. The passenger hanging sideways from the door may have a different view.

India has a genius for such partial functioning. A sticker warns about safety after everyone has already ignored the condition that would make safety possible. A form demands accuracy from a process built on evasion. A salary is delayed while the employer speaks of cash flow in the tone of a man discussing monsoon patterns. A statistic is published, adjusted, softened, lost, recovered, and finally quoted in a meeting where nobody has ridden the bus in years.

When I was younger, I mistook paperwork for order. A signature looked like accountability. A rule looked like intention. A company signboard looked like legitimacy. Age has corrected this optimism with admirable cruelty. A rule may exist only to decorate the wall. A ledger may conceal more than it records. A process may be designed not to solve a problem but to distribute blame thinly enough that no one can be held.

The crowded bus becomes a small moving diagram of the larger arrangement. Too many bodies. Too little space. Too much confidence from those not being crushed. Everyone learns the technique of survival without ownership. Shift a little. Bear it. Shout if necessary. Do not expect the vehicle to change shape for you.

The same logic enters offices, accounts, hospitals, schools, housing, contracts, and politics. A person is told there is a system. Then he is asked, quietly, to survive its failure as a private matter.

Perhaps this is why I have grown suspicious of any speech that praises resilience too easily. Resilience is admirable in a human being. In public policy it can become an excuse to keep the bus overcrowded.

The conductor rings the bell. The bus moves. Somewhere inside the press of elbows and tired shirts, a passenger protects his bag and his remaining patience.

That is the republic in motion.
