Acronyms used: IPM means Integrated Pest Management. Aedes, Anopheles, and Culex are mosquito genera. CDC means Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Lift a damp doormat in Kolkata after rain and the city becomes less human for a second. Small legs move. Antennae test the air. Something flattened and brown withdraws into a crack. A web trembles under the stair. The building has been shared all along.

Urban Calcutta is excellent habitat because it is warm, wet, old, crowded, layered, and generous with food scraps. Drains, kitchens, terraces, courtyards, potted plants, roof tanks, broken plaster, paper stacks, old wood, and monsoon seepage create a city beneath the city. We call many of its residents pests because they cross the line into our rooms, our food, our sleep, or our fear.

But the first useful distinction is simple. Insects have six legs, three main body regions, and often wings as adults. Arachnids, such as spiders, ticks, mites, and scorpions, have eight legs and no antennae. Myriapods, including centipedes and millipedes, are another group altogether. The city does not care about our categories, but good categories make fear less stupid.

Cockroaches are the obvious ambassadors. The large American cockroach turns up in drains, bathrooms, basements, kitchens, and the kind of corridor where the tube light has already accepted defeat. The smaller German cockroach is more closely tied to kitchens and food storage. Their real importance is not theatrical disgust. It is hygiene, allergen load, and contamination risk. They thrive where moisture, warmth, hiding places, and food residues come together.

Termites are quieter but often more expensive. They are not interested in frightening anyone. They are interested in cellulose: wood, paper, cardboard, and damp structural corners. In old buildings, especially where water has entered walls or timber, termites can work invisibly for a long time. The first sign may be mud tubes, hollow wood, powder, or a door frame that has become less solid than memory promised.

Ants are less a single problem than a city of strategies. Some follow sugar. Some harvest crumbs. Some nest in soil, wall gaps, or electrical spaces. Most are more nuisance than danger, but they are excellent indicators. A line of ants is a small audit report: food is accessible, a path exists, and the house has issued an invitation.

Mosquitoes are where annoyance becomes public health. Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are associated with dengue and chikungunya risk and often breed in clean standing water: buckets, plant saucers, discarded cups, rooftop containers, and anything that holds rain. Anopheles stephensi matters for malaria in urban settings. Culex mosquitoes are common around polluted water and drains. The practical lesson is boring because it is true: remove standing water, cover storage, clean containers, repair drainage, and do not let small reservoirs become nurseries.

Flies are another sanitation report. Houseflies move between waste, food, and surfaces with no respect for boundaries. Drain flies signal organic buildup in wet pipes and traps. Fruit flies appear where fermenting fruit, damp waste, or residues have been allowed to linger. Killing adults helps briefly. Removing breeding material helps more.

Bees and wasps bring a different problem: people panic before identifying the situation. Bees are pollinators and usually not interested in conflict unless the nest is disturbed. Wasps can be more defensive around nests. The sensible response is not amateur heroics. A nest in a risky location needs careful removal by people who know what they are doing.

Dragonflies and damselflies are signs of water nearby and are among the more elegant hunters in the urban air. Butterflies and moths arrive with gardens, host plants, lights, and seasons. Beetles vary wildly: stored-grain pests, wood borers, flower visitors, scavengers, and occasional wanderers who entered by mistake. Mantises, crickets, grasshoppers, katydids, and cicadas add to the seasonal orchestra, though they are rarely household enemies.

Silverfish and booklice belong to the damp-paper world. They like humidity, starches, glue, old books, cardboard, and quiet shelves. Bed bugs are different. They are not a sign of dirt. They are transportable, persistent, and deeply inconvenient because they hide in seams, cracks, furniture joints, and luggage. Their management requires patience, heat, inspection, and repeated work rather than shame.

Spiders are usually misread. Most house spiders in Kolkata are doing pest control without salary. Huntsman spiders look dramatic because they are large and fast, but they are not hunting people. Jumping spiders are small, alert visual hunters. Orb-weavers build visible circular webs. Cellar spiders take corners and ceiling spaces. Wolf, crab, and lynx spiders may appear near plants, walls, or open areas. The usual rule is simple: leave them alone unless they are in a place where contact is likely.

Scorpions, ticks, and mites require more caution. Scorpions are not everyday indoor companions for most city households, but they can appear in debris, gardens, stored material, and disturbed ground. Ticks and mites are small enough to be underestimated, and some can affect skin or transmit disease depending on species and exposure. Identification matters. So does not turning every small moving dot into a horror story.

Integrated Pest Management is the grown-up answer because it begins before the spray can. Remove food access. Store grain and snacks properly. Seal cracks. Fix leaks. Clear clutter. Clean drains. Empty standing water. Use screens where possible. Inspect luggage and bedding when needed. Reserve chemicals for targeted situations and use them safely. The aim is not to sterilize the city, which is impossible. The aim is to reduce contact, breeding, and risk.

Calcutta teaches natural history badly because it teaches it too close. The lesson is not in a park with labels. It is in the bathroom corner, the rice tin, the wet wall, the terrace bucket, the book shelf, and the bright tube light after rain. We live inside an ecology and then act offended when ecology enters the room.

Some of these creatures matter medically. Some damage property. Some are merely fellow citizens of damp masonry. Fear is allowed, but accuracy is better. The city is not only human infrastructure. It is also antennae, wings, silk, eggs, larvae, and patient little systems making use of every crack we forgot to seal.
