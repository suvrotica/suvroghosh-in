The spreadsheet does not hate you. It does not know you exist. It is a grid of cells, a rectilinear meadow of numbers, and yet when the formula in cell H47 divides the annual payroll by the quarterly revenue, the quotient emerges with a certain intestinal inevitability, a digestive finality, as if the numbers themselves have undergone a long, greasy meal and are now ready to excrete the excess. Forty-five becomes an accounting adjustment. Not a tragedy. Not a mass extinction. Just a rounding error with health insurance.

In a previous life, when I wore the shackles of junior staff engineer in Austin back in 2002, I watched a vice president or whoever with the emotional range of a filing cabinet announce that we would be doing more with less, which is corporate Sanskrit for doing everything with nothing, and the nothing in question was us. That was back then, not much has changed really!

This new thing, this AI did not arrive in a chrome skeleton humming with malevolent sentience. It arrived as a subscription. A monthly fee. A line item smaller than the coffee budget. And yet it digested the work of ten analysts and passed them like so much undigested fiber. Consider the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the only part that does not lie. A company employs one hundred souls. It introduces a system, a clever concatenation of transformers and tokens, that doubles the output per worker. Now, in the kingdom of the enlightened, the management might look upon this surplus capacity and see opportunity. New products. New markets. Perhaps, in a fit of hallucinatory generosity, new hires. But we do not live in the kingdom of the enlightened. We live in the kingdom of the quarterly earnings call. And in that damp cellar, management sees that the same revenue can be extracted from fifty-five bodies. The remaining forty-five are not fired, mind you. They are optimized. They are rightsized. They are invited to explore new opportunities, which is a euphemism for being shown the door so fast the door handle leaves a bruise.

This is the first heresy that must be spoken: the machines do not need to be intelligent to replace you. They need only be cheap. They need only be adequate. A system that completes eighty-five percent of a task and leaves the remaining fifteen percent for a human overseer is not a marvel of engineering. It is a cost center with a kill ratio. If one human can supervise ten such systems, you do not need ten humans. You need one human with the haunted eyes of a lighthouse keeper watching ten mechanical waves crash against the rocks of commerce.

And here is where the Bengali in me, the part that grew up watching my father calculate the price of rice to the paisa, begins to chuckle with a mouth full of mustard oil and despair. We have seen this digestion before. First, the looms. Then the assembly lines. Then the call centers of Bangalore and the back offices of Salt Lake Sector V, where I have watched rows of graduates typing away their twenties into databases that did not know their names. Every generation believes its labor is too cerebral, too nuanced, too intimately human to be automated. Every generation discovers that the spreadsheet has a different opinion.

The economists speak of productivity as if it were a blessing from the gods, a cornucopia spilling forth abundance for all. But productivity is merely a ratio. Output divided by input. It says nothing about who keeps the surplus. It says nothing about the forty-five who become the denominator. When the company that employed two hundred analysts to process a million transactions discovers it can process the same million with sixty, the productivity has soared, the shareholders have smiled, the customers have received their statements three days earlier, and one hundred forty analysts have discovered that their professional skills are now negotiating with the void.

The void, incidentally, does not negotiate.

But wait. The enthusiasts tell me I am being hysterical. They point to the World Economic Forum, that Davos circus of the perpetually optimistic, which promises one hundred seventy million new jobs by 2030. A net gain, they cry. A harvest of employment. What they do not mention is that these new jobs do not appear in the same place as the old ones. They do not appear at the same time. They do not appear for the same people. The software developer in Salt Lake does not automatically become a wind turbine technician in Rajasthan. The accountant whose spreadsheet has learned to think does not instantly transmute into a cybersecurity oracle. The jobs are created, yes. But they are created elsewhere, in some other pocket of the economy, while the displaced worker is left holding his obsolete skills like a handful of rupees in a demonetized economy.

This is the merism of modern labor: creation and destruction, the making and the unmaking, happening simultaneously in different rooms of the same house, and the house is on fire.

The cruelest trick, however, is not the firing. It is the not-hiring. The Stanford study tells us that employment among twenty-two to twenty-five year olds in AI-exposed occupations has dropped nineteen percent. Not because they were thrown out. Because they were never let in. The door was simply painted on the wall. The entry-level position now requires five years of experience, fluency in three programming languages, and the ability to architect cloud infrastructure while juggling flaming chainsaws. One wonders where this mythical creature is supposed to have acquired its five years. In the womb? In a previous incarnation?

We are witnessing the disappearance of the apprenticeship, the death of the junior role. For generations, the young entered the temple of the profession to sweep the floors, to copy the manuscripts, to make the coffee and the mistakes that teach. Now the AI sweeps the floors. The AI copies the manuscripts. The young stand outside the temple, clutching their degrees, while the priests inside wonder why no new acolytes are arriving to learn the sacred rites.

The sacred rites, of course, now consist of prompting a machine and praying the output does not hallucinate.

I have used these tools. I will not pretend otherwise. I have watched them generate in seconds the code that once took me hours of documentation-diving, error-log-divining, caffeine-fueled cursing. There is a genuine wonder here, a synaesthesia of silicon and syntax where the machine seems to taste the shape of a solution. But I have also watched these same confident machines produce code that would collapse in production at three in the morning, that would confuse patient identities in a clinical system, that would treat an authorization boundary like a polite suggestion. The AI can write the function. It cannot be held accountable when the function kills someone.

And accountability, that heavy, inconvenient, thoroughly human burden, remains the last employment of the employed.

Yet we must not console ourselves too quickly with the thought that the machines are fallible. Fallibility has never been a barrier to replacement. We are all fallible. We are tired, we are distracted, we forget where we saved the spreadsheet, we die inconveniently in the middle of fiscal year. The question is not whether the machine is perfect. The question is whether the machine is perfect enough, cheap enough, and obedient enough to make the spreadsheet sing a sweeter song. And the song it sings is always the same: do more with less.

Do more with less. The mantra of the modern age. The anadiplosis of our economic liturgy. Less leads to more, more leads to less, and around we go until the head spins and the stomach turns.

The companies compete, you see. Even if one benevolent employer decides to keep all one hundred workers, to share the surplus, to be the good uncle who remembers your mortgage, the competitor down the street will not be so sentimental. The competitor will adopt the machines, cut the costs, lower the prices, capture the market. And the benevolent employer will be forced to choose between benevolence and bankruptcy. The spreadsheet does not permit virtue for long.

So the adoption spreads, not through malice but through mathematics, a contagion of efficiency. The executives do not even need to understand the technology. They need only understand that the other fellow is using it, and that his quarterly results look better than theirs. Fear of falling behind does the work that understanding cannot.

And beneath all this, the infrastructure grows. The data centers rise like cathedrals of computation, drinking rivers of electricity, consuming silicon and capital and the dreams of venture capitalists who believe they are building the future when they are merely building a more efficient guillotine. Is it a bubble? Perhaps. The railway barons thought they were building empires; many built bankruptcies. The dot-com prophets thought they were inventing a new economy; they invented a very expensive way to deliver pet food. The technology may survive the financial collapse. The investors may not. And the workers displaced in the meantime will not be comforted by the thought that history will vindicate the machines.

For in India, in my Calcutta, the stakes are different. Our modern middle class was built on the outsourcing of drudgery. We became the back office of the world. We processed their claims, answered their calls, tested their software, documented their failures. Our economic proposition was simple: we will do your boring work for less money than you would pay yourself to do it. But if the machine will do the boring work for even less, if the machine does not require health insurance or visa sponsorship or a Diwali bonus, then what is our role in this global digestion?

The answer, of course, is that we must become the engineers of the digestion itself. We must move up the value chain, or so the consultants tell us, in their PowerPoint slides generated by the very AI that is digesting their own junior analysts. But not everyone can be a prompt engineer. Not everyone should be. We still need nurses, electricians, plumbers, people who can fix the physical world when it breaks. The AI cannot unclog your drain. Not yet. Not until someone invents a robot with strong arms and a weak sense of smell.

But for the white-collar worker, the analyst, the coder, the writer of reports and the reconciler of accounts, the future is a narrowing corridor. The tasks that can be described can be automated. The tasks that can be documented can be delegated to the machine. What remains is judgment, accountability, and the ability to stand in a room full of angry stakeholders and absorb their wrath when the AI has produced a confident, plausible, catastrophically wrong answer.

This is not a large employment category.

So what is to be done? The individual cannot stop the tide. The individual can only learn to swim in it, or drown with style. Learn the tools, yes, but more importantly, learn what the tools cannot do. Learn to verify. Learn to doubt. Learn to understand the business, the customer, the consequence of error. Do not be the person who knows how to use the software. Be the person who knows why the software is wrong.

And keep your savings liquid. Keep your skills portable. Do not confuse loyalty with security. The company is not your family. The company is an economic arrangement, and when the arrangement becomes inconvenient, you will be rearranged.

The machines do not need to take over the world. They only need to make the spreadsheet look better. And the spreadsheet, that grid of innocent cells, will do the rest. It will divide, it will subtract, it will optimize. It will not weep. It will not remember your name. It will simply show, in cell H47, that forty-five is a number that can be reduced to zero, and the world will continue spinning, indifferent and efficient, while the forty-five adjust their resumes and wonder where the years went.

The years went into the training data. The machine learned from your labor, and now it labors without you. This is not science fiction. This is arithmetic.

And the arithmetic, like a bad stomach, always wins.
