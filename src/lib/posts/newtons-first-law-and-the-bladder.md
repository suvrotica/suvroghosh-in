Acronyms used: USB means Universal Serial Bus, the ordinary cable-and-port standard by which half the world charges its gadgets and misplaces its patience. AI means Artificial Intelligence. HIE means Healthcare Information Exchange, the sharing of clinical information between hospitals, clinics, labs, and other care systems.

The bedsheet is damp at the edge, the fan is turning with the tired authority of an old office clerk, and the floor is only three feet away. Three feet should not be a serious distance. It is shorter than a tea-shop bench, shorter than a bus doorway, shorter than the gap between intention and embarrassment in most Bengali households.

And yet, on certain mornings, three feet is a continent.

This is where Newton becomes useful.

A body at rest remains at rest unless acted upon by an external force. In school, the sentence arrived with neat diagrams: a ball, a cart, a smooth surface, an arrow. Everything looked polite. Nobody mentioned the adult version, where a person lies in bed in Calcutta, fully aware that the day has begun, and still cannot convert awareness into motion.

That failure is often misnamed laziness.

Laziness is a comfortable word for spectators. It makes the observer feel wise, strict, and slightly taller. But executive dysfunction is not the same as deciding to do nothing. It is the unpleasant gap between knowing the next action and being unable to initiate it. The command exists. The body does not obey. The mind begins a meeting. The meeting adjourns without action.

Get up.

Take a bath.

Open the laptop.

Make tea.

Reply to the message.

Each task looks small from outside because the outside view counts only visible movement. From inside, the first movement has to overcome static friction. That is the cruel part. Once a body is moving, continuation may become easier. The impossible step is often the first one: elbow into mattress, foot to floor, spine upright, eyes accepting the room.

Static friction is a good metaphor because it does not insult the problem. A cupboard pushed across a floor may resist fiercely at first and then slide with less effort. A life can behave like that too. The difficulty is not always the total length of the day. It is crossing the threshold from zero to one.

The bladder, unfortunately or mercifully, understands thresholds.

It does not care about philosophy. It does not ask whether the morning has meaning. It does not request an inspirational quote. It applies pressure in the most literal possible way. The body that was negotiating with despair receives a practical instruction: move.

This is not noble. It is not cinematic. There is no music. There is a sideways roll, a hand on the bed frame, a small complaint from the lower back, and the first contact of feet with a floor that feels cooler than expected. But it is motion.

That matters.

Once upright, the universe changes slightly. The bathroom happens. Water touches the face. The mirror performs its usual audit and is ignored as much as possible. A kettle may be filled. Tea may appear. The laptop lid may open an inch, then another. None of this is victory in the public sense. It is mechanics. A force entered the system and the system crossed a threshold.

The same principle applies elsewhere. Heat can move a person from bed to chair because the bed becomes unbearable. Hunger can move a person toward the kitchen. A deadline can move the hand toward the keyboard. A mosquito can perform more motivational work than a whole shelf of self-improvement books. These forces are crude, local, and sometimes humiliating, but they are forces.

I have become suspicious of advice that treats motion as a purely moral event.

"Just start."

Yes. Correct. Also incomplete.

The first start is the expensive one. It requires energy from the very system that is short of energy. It is like being asked to pay an entrance fee in order to enter the building where money is distributed. The advice is not false. It is merely too clean for the room in which it is being applied.

In a better vocabulary, one might speak of activation energy. A chemical reaction may be possible, but it still needs enough energy to begin. A person may be capable of work, speech, bathing, cooking, or writing, but still fail to cross the first barrier in the morning. Capability is not initiation. Potential is not motion.

This is where small external structures help. Not because they cure life. Because they reduce the first barrier.

Keep water near the bed. Put the towel where it can be seen. Leave the laptop charged. Make the first task absurdly small. Let tea become a lever. Let light enter the room. Use the body's ordinary demands without adding a sermon. If the first action is too large, shrink it until it becomes almost ridiculous. Sit up. Stand. Wash face. Boil water. Open one document. Write one sentence. Stop treating the first step as proof of character.

Calcutta mornings are good at supplying external force anyway. A drill begins in the next building. A pressure cooker whistles. A vendor calls from the lane. A bus horn drags itself through the air. The city does not wait for private readiness. It starts, badly and loudly, and sometimes that is enough to pull one reluctant body into the day.

There is no neat lesson here. Some mornings remain heavy. Some bodies stay at rest longer than dignity would prefer. Physics does not become kindness simply because one borrows its language.

But Newton's first law is still less cruel than moral judgment. It says rest continues unless force changes it. It does not call the body wicked. It does not ask for a motivational speech. It describes a condition and a requirement.

On certain mornings, that description is enough. The first force may be undignified. The first movement may be clumsy. The day may not become grand. But the body has moved from zero to one, and in the arithmetic of a hard morning, one is not a small number.
