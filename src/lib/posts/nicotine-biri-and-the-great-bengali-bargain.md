A loose biri lies on a tea-stall counter with the modest confidence of something cheap enough to escape moral inspection.

That is one of nicotine's best disguises. It becomes small. It becomes ordinary. It becomes folded into tea, boredom, work breaks, paan shops, after-lunch rituals, street-corner talk, and the long Calcutta habit of treating personal harm as a private account to be settled later. The cigarette has glamour. The biri has class history. Khaini has secrecy. Zarda has flavor. Gutkha has packaging. Vaping has battery-powered confidence. Nicotine pouches have discretion. Patch and gum have the respectability of the pharmacy when used properly to quit tobacco.

Different costumes. Same hook.

The first distinction is combustion. Burning tobacco is a major disaster because smoke carries tar, carbon monoxide, fine particles, and many toxic chemicals into the lungs and blood vessels. A biri is not made safe by looking rustic. A cigarette is not made safer by looking polished. Smoke is smoke. The lung does not award cultural marks for presentation.

Smokeless tobacco changes the route, not the bargain. Khaini tucked under the lip, zarda in paan, gutkha in a sachet, and other oral products spare the room from smoke but not the body from risk. The mouth, gums, and throat become the local receiving office for repeated chemical insult. Because the harm is quieter, the user can pretend it is smaller. Public health has seen this trick before. Quiet harm is still harm.

Vaping then arrives with the grammar of modernity. It looks clean because it has lights and liquid and a charging port. It calls smoke by another name. But aerosol is not air, and nicotine is still nicotine. A person who never smoked and begins vaping has not performed a harm-reduction maneuver. He has installed a dependency with better packaging.

Nicotine pouches are the newest polite entry in the same argument. They can be small, flavored, tobacco-free in leaf content, and easy to hide. WHO warned in May 2026 that nicotine pouch brands were using youth-friendly marketing: sleek packets, flavors, influencers, social media, discreet-use messaging, and lifestyle branding. The public-health worry is obvious. When a dependence-forming product becomes easier to conceal and easier to aestheticize, it does not become innocent. It becomes easier to recruit.

None of this means every product has identical risk. That would be lazy arithmetic.

For a smoker, moving away from combustible tobacco is a serious step. Nicotine replacement therapy, used as a bridge to quitting, can help reduce withdrawal while avoiding smoke. That is not the same as declaring nicotine harmless. A bridge is useful because it helps you cross. It becomes a problem when you move furniture onto it and call it home.

Calcutta understands the ritual better than the risk chart does. Tea and biri. Bus stop and cigarette. After rice and zarda paan. Work stress and khaini. Phone scrolling and vape. Evening irritation and one small pouch. The habit attaches itself to the hinges of the day until the day feels poorly assembled without it.

That is why quitting is not only chemistry. It is redesign.

The hand wants something. The mouth wants something. The pause wants something. The insult swallowed at noon wants a small ceremony. The tired walk home wants punctuation. Addiction does not live only inside the body. It lives in timing, place, gesture, shopkeeper familiarity, and the little rewards by which a difficult day is made tolerable for five minutes.

This is also why public-health scolding often fails. It tells people the thing is bad. Many already know. The packet tells them. The cough tells them. The mouth ulcer tells them. The price tells them. The mirror tells them. Knowledge alone does not rebuild the hour after tea.

A more honest sentence is this: if you do not use nicotine, do not start; if you smoke, move first away from smoke; if you use smokeless tobacco, do not comfort yourself with the absence of ash; if you vape without having been a smoker, ask whether you have merely bought a cleaner-looking chain; if you use patch or gum to quit, use it as a planned exit rather than a permanent side business.

WHO keeps repeating the larger arithmetic because the arithmetic remains brutal. Tobacco use is linked to cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, and many cancers, and it remains one of the world's leading preventable causes of death. The exact delivery system changes the risk profile, but dependence has a way of defending itself through language. "Only one." "After tea." "This is not as bad as that." "At least I do not smoke." "At least I only smoke biri." "At least it is tobacco-free." "At least it is mint."

At least is one of nicotine's favorite phrases.

The great Bengali bargain is not that people do not understand risk. It is that risk is asked to wait outside while fatigue, habit, price, stress, and social ritual hold the meeting inside. A man buys one loose cigarette and feels disciplined because he did not buy a packet. Then he repeats the discipline ten times. A pouch user feels modern because there is no ash. A khaini user feels private because the room does not complain.

The body keeps its own ledger.

It is not necessary to hate oneself to stop. Shame is a poor long-term strategy. But flattery is worse. Nicotine is not a personality, not a cultural inheritance, not proof of toughness, not an intellectual aid, not a reward for surviving another ordinary day in a difficult city.

It is a hook.

The wrapper changes. The hook keeps its profession.
