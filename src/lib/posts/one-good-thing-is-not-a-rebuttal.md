The headache lifted at 4:47 PM, not because the universe finally noticed my suffering and decided to issue a refund, but because the paracetamol dissolved into my bloodstream with the quiet efficiency of a bribed municipal official. I sat there, in the blue light of a monitor that has seen more of my face than any human being should ever have to witness, and realized that the vice around my temples had loosened its grip. Not gone. Just loosened. Like a landlord who stops hammering on the door but leaves the eviction notice taped to the frame.

I am told by people with framed certificates and therapeutic couches that I should practice gratitude, which is a wonderful concept if you happen to be a golden retriever or a man who has never had to calculate whether he can afford both dinner and the electricity bill. Gratitude, in the mouth of the privileged, sounds suspiciously like a demand for the poor to be thankful for the crumbs that fell from the table they were never invited to sit at. But here is the thing, the tiny, infuriating, chemically-induced thing: for eleven minutes, the pain stopped being the only fact of my existence. The future remained fucked, of course. The future is a collapsed bridge in West Bengal, a promise made by contractors who pocketed the cement and left the rebar to rust in the monsoon. My career prospects still resemble the Hooghly during idol immersion season, clogged with debris and the rotting remains of good intentions. The bipolar disorder still lurks in the wiring of my skull like a corrupt policeman waiting for his cut. All of that remained true. But the headache lifted, and in that lifting, there was a space where I could breathe without feeling like someone was driving a railway spike through my left eye.

I did not feel happy, exactly. Happiness is a big, bloated word, a wedding banquet word, a word that requires catering and flowers and the kind of money that buys silence from awkward relatives. What I felt was more like the absence of a specific variety of torture. It was the removal of a stone from the shoe after you have walked three miles on a broken pavement in Tollygunge, the pavement that the municipality keeps promising to repair every election year and never does. You do not dance with joy when the stone comes out. You stand there, sweating, furious at the road, furious at your own foot for being soft, furious at the concept of gravity, and yet, there is this undeniable relief, this small mercy that you did not earn and do not trust.

The ceiling fan rotated with the lazy indifference of a government clerk who knows his pension is secure. Outside, a bus groaned past with its engine knocking like a dying man's heart, carrying passengers packed tighter than electrons in a degenerate gas, each one holding onto the dream of reaching home before the next breakdown. I watched the fan blades cut the air, slice after slice, a metronome marking time that I will never get back. And I thought, with the kind of clarity that only comes when the pain stops shouting, that perhaps this is all we get. Not joy, not triumph, not the Instagram-filtered nonsense of wellness influencers telling us to manifest our destinies while they sell us overpriced coffee. Just this: the temporary cessation of one specific agony. The headache lifts. The code compiles. The rain arrives thirty minutes after the weather app promised it would, and the heat breaks like a fever. The cat eats without vomiting on the carpet. A message arrives from someone who is not asking for money or favors or emotional labor.

We are taught to think in narratives, in arcs, in the Aristotelian bullshit of beginning, middle, and end, where the hero learns something and the clouds part and the music swells. But life in this city, life under the weight of late capitalism and the specific Bengali talent for turning nostalgia into a chronic illness, does not work that way. Here, the story does not resolve. The story continues, often badly. The good ten minutes do not cancel the bad fourteen hours. They coexist, they interpenetrate, they form a solution more complex than any chemistry I learned in school. The headache returns eventually, as it always does, because the body is a traitor that keeps excellent accounts. But for those eleven minutes, I was not a failure, not a diagnosis, not a statistic in the mental health crisis that this country refuses to acknowledge because it is too busy building statues. I was just a man sitting in a chair, watching a fan spin, feeling the absence of pain.

And if that is not happiness, it is at least a truce. I will take the truce. I will take it with both hands and a mouth full of unbrushed teeth, because tomorrow the hammering starts again, and I will need the memory of this silence to survive the noise.
