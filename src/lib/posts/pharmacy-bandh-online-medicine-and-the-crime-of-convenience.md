Medicine is not shampoo with a fever.

That is the small fact our clever age keeps forgetting. Shampoo may be fake and you feel cheated. A fake antibiotic is a different moral category. A counterfeit cancer drug is not a bad shopping experience. An expired strip re-entering circulation is not a logistics bug. Healthcare commerce carries bodily consequences.

The May 2026 pharmacy bandh in India was presented, predictably, as a shopkeeper-versus-app story. Traditional chemists angry at e-pharmacies. Online platforms offering discounts. Small retailers fearing corporate scale. Customers wanting convenience. That story is real, but too small.

The darker issue is trust architecture.

According to Indian Express reporting, chemist associations called a nationwide shutdown for May 20, 2026, with AIOCD involved and emergency services expected to continue. Reports described objections to online pharmacy platforms, business pressure, and regulatory gaps. State-level reporting also described government efforts to keep essential supply available during the strike. The dispute sat against older regulatory material, including the 2018 draft e-pharmacy rules and the pandemic-era doorstep-delivery framework later debated by regulators.

The public question is not whether apps are convenient. Of course they are.

The public question is who owns the risk when drugs move at internet speed.

A retail chemist can be careless, corrupt, or excellent. An online platform can be careless, corrupt, or excellent. The channel does not purify the act. What matters is prescription verification, pharmacist accountability, batch tracking, expiry control, cold-chain integrity where relevant, recall management, privacy, auditability, returns discipline, and the ability to trace a strip from source to patient.

Convenience becomes dangerous when it outruns verification.

The e-pharmacy problem is not that delivery is inherently wrong. For elderly patients, disabled patients, remote households, and chronic care needs, delivery can be humane. The problem is weak governance around repeat use of prescriptions, aggressive discounts that hide supply-chain incentives, unclear responsibility for validation, and marketplaces that blur the line between care and commodity.

Small chemists are not automatically saints. Large platforms are not automatically villains.

But scale changes the failure mode. A bad local shop can harm locally. A bad platform process can replicate harm across regions before anyone sees the pattern. Conversely, a well-governed digital platform can preserve audit trails, batch details, pharmacist validation, and complaint history better than a paper shop. The question is not local versus digital. The question is governed versus opaque.

Healthcare IT should see the familiar pattern.

Transport is not meaning. Delivery is not safety. A prescription image uploaded to an app is not the same as clinical appropriateness. A discount is not access if it encourages unsafe use. A transaction log is not accountability unless regulators can inspect it and patients can act on it. A platform claim is not a chain of custody unless the chain is visible and enforceable.

India often treats regulation as either a brake or a slogan. It needs to be a working system.

That means clear licensing, pharmacist responsibility, data localization where required, privacy boundaries, transaction audits, controlled product restrictions, adverse-event reporting paths, expiry and batch traceability, marketplace liability, grievance redressal, and penalties that matter. It also means protecting continuity of access during strikes, because the patient should not become the bargaining table.

The pharmacy bandh was therefore not only about apps. It was about whether India can build digital health commerce without dissolving the fragile trust that already holds ordinary care together.

In Calcutta, the neighborhood chemist often knows more about a family's practical care than any app screen can infer. That local knowledge is not a substitute for regulation. It is a reminder that healthcare is not merely distribution. It is responsibility with a receipt.

Who owns the risk when medicine becomes commerce at internet speed?

Not the patient. The patient has carried enough already.
