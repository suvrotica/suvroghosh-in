The chair made a complaint before I did.

Its plastic back bent slightly under me, not enough to break, only enough to remind me that even furniture has limits. The afternoon was hot in the low, sticky, democratic way of Calcutta heat. It did not discriminate. It entered cloth, skin, food, thought, and the patience of anyone foolish enough to remain conscious.

There is a rude local phrase for the condition of being finished from below upward, the kind of collapse that begins in the body and then becomes philosophy. I will not repeat it here. Some phrases work better as smoke than as print.

But I understand the weather it describes.

The day can die in the hips first. Then the back. Then the neck. Then the will to answer a message beginning with "Dada, just one small thing." Soon the whole man is sitting there like a postponed repair.

The danger is letting that condition become the entire biography.

It wants to. Physical discomfort is a gifted propagandist. Heat says there is no future. Hunger says nobody respects you. An unpaid bill says you were foolish to expect decency. A damp shirt says civilization has failed at the level of cloth.

Some of this may even be true.

Still, the day must be handled in pieces. Tea. Rice. One paragraph. One bath after excessive negotiation. One bill opened without fainting theatrically. One small decision not to let the weather write the whole book.

The chair complains again when I stand.

Fair enough.

I complain too, only with more vocabulary.
