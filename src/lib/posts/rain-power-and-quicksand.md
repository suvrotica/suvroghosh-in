The light goes out just as the rain begins to sound serious.

For a second the room becomes a photograph. Fan stopped. Screen dimmed. Street noise softened by water. Then the inverter coughs into duty, and the house resumes in reduced mode. This is India's genius: not collapse, not function, but reduced mode.

Rain reveals infrastructure with an honesty that speeches avoid. The lane fills. The drain remembers its limits. A wire becomes suspect. A wall darkens. The road surface, which yesterday carried motorbikes and confidence, becomes a negotiation.

Quicksand is not always dramatic. Sometimes it is civic. You stand where ground was expected and feel it change underfoot.

Modern India speaks often in the language of arrival. Digital, global, scalable, rising, transformed. Some of that is real. A phone can do astonishing things. Payments move. Information travels. A young person with a cheap device can learn more than an earlier generation could access through an entire neighborhood.

And then the power cuts.

Both facts are true. The mistake is choosing only one because it suits the mood. Optimists edit out the water rising in the lane. Pessimists edit out the phone lighting in the hand. The country is neither miracle nor ruin. It is a machine running unevenly, sometimes brilliant, sometimes absurd, always at risk where maintenance has been treated as less glamorous than announcement.

Rain has no respect for announcement.

It tests slopes, drains, roofs, wires, foundations, and the patience of people who must still cook, work, study, charge phones, move parents, reach clinics, and keep appointments made by offices with dry floors. Weather turns policy into household fact.

The room grows warm without the fan. The rain continues. A message arrives on the phone, bright and confident, promising some new future in a tone that has never waited for an electrician.

I put the phone face down and listen to the water.

The ground is still there. It is only less certain than advertised.
