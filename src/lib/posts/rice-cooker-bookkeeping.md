The rice cooker clicked with the confidence of a small accountant.

It had finished its calculation. Water, heat, grain, time. Result: edible enough. Beside it, my notebook lay open to another calculation, the kind no appliance will finish for you. Rent, food, electricity, data, medicine shop items, repairs postponed, work uncertain, dignity negotiated.

A household budget is not only money.

It is a map of what gets cut first. Taste goes early. Then convenience. Then repair. Then preventive care. Then sleep. Then candor. A man begins saying "fine" because the accurate sentence would take too long and improve nothing.

People with comfortable cushions speak of discipline. They imagine budgeting as a moral exercise performed with colored pens. At the lower end, budgeting is closer to triage. The question is not what should be done. The question is what can be allowed to fail this month without bringing the ceiling down.

Rice is useful because it is honest. It does not pretend to be prosperity. It says: here is bulk, here is heat, here is something to place under dal, here is the minimum architecture of a meal. It gives the stomach a floor.

The rest remains uncertain.

Calcutta teaches arithmetic at street level. A fare increases. A cylinder empties. A repair waits. A bill arrives with official calm. The month, meanwhile, stretches its jaw.

I scrape the bottom of the cooker and collect the stuck grains because waste is not a philosophical category here. It is an accusation.

The rice is done.

The month is not.

Somewhere in the notebook, a small digit sits quietly, waiting to become an event.
