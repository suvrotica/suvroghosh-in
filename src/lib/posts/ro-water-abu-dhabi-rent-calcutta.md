The water tasted wrong even after passing through the filter.

That is the insult. A machine sits there with its little tubes and promises purification in the language of plastic, yet the tongue remains unconvinced. Something metallic, something tired, something from the old underbelly of the building still arrives in the glass.

So I boil it.

Then I make tea.

This is the Calcutta method of problem solving: filter, boil, overboil, add tea dust, call the result manageable.

Meanwhile, rent behaves as if my room has relocated to a richer map. The landlord speaks in numbers that seem to have returned from Abu Dhabi with sunglasses. The walls remain damp. The pipework remains ancestral. The lane remains narrow. But the price has developed international confidence.

Moving up, in theory, means better water, better wiring, better air, a bathroom that does not look like a committee compromise. In practice it means paying too much to be disappointed at a slightly higher elevation.

I browse listings with the seriousness of a man comparing coffins with attached bathrooms.

The body becomes suspicious under these conditions. It doubts the glass, the tap, the kitchen smell, the stain near the drain, the sincerity of all improvements. Poverty is not only shortage. It is the loss of trust in surfaces.

The tea turns dark in the cup. Heat covers many sins. Sugar covers some more.

Outside, the city continues selling aspiration by the square foot.

Inside, I take one careful sip and wait for the water to prove it has no further argument.
