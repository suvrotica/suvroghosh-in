The sentence on the screen sounded clever enough to borrow. That was the first small surrender. Not because it was false exactly, and not because it was brilliant, but because it arrived with a sheen of confidence that made my own hesitation look shabby.

AI has made pretension cheaper.

This is not the usual fear about fake images or wrong answers. Those matter. But there is a quieter damage: a person can use the machine to sound more certain than he is, then slowly begin to believe the sound.

That is self-gaslighting in its modern office form. You do not need another person to distort your sense of reality. You outsource the distortion, edit it slightly, publish it in your own name, and then feel obliged to stand behind the tone.

The machine produces polished language. The language implies mastery. The person reading it may assume competence. The person posting it may start performing competence. Soon everyone in the room is standing near a sentence that nobody truly owns.

This is not always malicious. Often it begins with embarrassment. Nobody wants to look slow. Nobody wants to admit that a topic is only half understood. A tool that can turn confusion into paragraphs feels merciful. It reduces exposure. It gives the uncertain person a suit of language.

The trouble is that language is not only clothing. It shapes thought.

If I write "I am not sure," I remain near the truth. If I ask AI to make it sound stronger, I may receive "The evidence suggests a clear directional shift." That may be useful in the right context. It may also hide the simple fact that I am guessing.

Corporate language has always done this. Academic language too. Bureaucratic language perfected the art long before chatbots arrived. AI did not invent pretension. It industrialized the first draft of it.

The danger is scale. Earlier, to sound grand, one had to learn the costume slowly. Now anyone can generate the costume in seconds. Strategy documents, essays, emails, social posts, policy memos, technical summaries, and personal declarations can all acquire the same smooth confidence. The texture becomes familiar: balanced, caveated, impressive, and strangely airless.

After a while, the human behind it may forget the original size of the thought.

There is a decent use of AI here. It can clarify. It can ask better questions. It can point out contradictions. It can help a timid person write plainly. It can translate messy notes into order without lying about the notes.

But the user must resist the machine's temptation toward finish. AI likes completion. It wants the paragraph to land. It wants the bullet list to look resolved. It wants the answer to occupy the space cleanly. Human thought is often not clean. It is tentative, revised, irritated, partial, and aware of its own limits.

That awareness is precious.

The phrase "I don't know" is not a failure state. It is a navigational tool. It stops the mind from walking into furniture. It tells other people where the floor is uncertain. It prevents a meeting from becoming theatre.

AI makes "I don't know" easier to conceal. That is why it must be said more deliberately.

In Calcutta, one learns quickly that surfaces are unreliable. A wall may be newly painted and damp behind it. A road may look repaired until the first serious shower. A signboard may promise efficiency while the queue explains the older truth. The city is a teacher of hidden conditions.

Language has hidden conditions too.

When an AI-generated sentence sounds too clean, I try to ask a rude internal question: what do I actually mean? Not what can be said. Not what would sound intelligent. Not what might impress someone skimming. What do I know, what do I suspect, and where am I pretending?

That question slows the hand.

Sometimes the answer is humiliating. The thought was smaller. The claim was softer. The confidence was borrowed. Good. Better a smaller honest sentence than a large polished fog.

The cursor blinks. The machine waits, cheerful and ready. I delete the clever line and write the poorer one. It is less impressive. It is closer to the room.
