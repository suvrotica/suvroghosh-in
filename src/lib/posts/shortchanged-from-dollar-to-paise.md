The shirt smelled of heat before I had finished wearing it.

Calcutta does that. It takes cloth as evidence and submits its verdict early. I remembered, with unnecessary cruelty, the imported scent I once used in America, the clean administrative fiction of being presentable under fluorescent lights. Now the body smells of humidity, cheap soap, and the exchange-rate damage of a life that changed currencies badly.

America was not paradise.

The I-35 between San Antonio and Austin could bore a man into abstract thought. Offices had their own lonely weather. Competence did not always mean belonging. Still, boredom there had infrastructure. Roads went somewhere. Paychecks arrived with fewer philosophical complications. A dull life, I later learned, can be a luxury if the dullness is properly funded.

Return converted dollars into paise in more than one sense.

Old school memories from WWA, Salt Lake addresses, UTSA, work, ambition, all came back through a smaller pipe. The same skills that once seemed useful began waiting outside doors with bad nameplates. A man can know how to solve a problem and still lose to the person who knows whom to call.

That is the Indian refinement of insult.

It does not always reject competence. It often praises competence warmly while arranging to use it cheaply.

I do not spare myself. I made choices. Some were brave, some vain, some merely poorly timed. I mistook movement for improvement. Many men do. We call it a chapter change because "miscalculation" has less music.

The shirt clings to my back.

Outside, traffic thickens. Inside, the old American road appears again in memory, flat and overbright and dull enough to feel almost merciful.

At least it went somewhere.
