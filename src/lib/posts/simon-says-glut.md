Herbert Simon, that patient, owlish, Nobel-laden gentleman of the seventies, the one who gave us "satisficing" because "optimizing" sounded too much like a man trying to choose the perfect mango in a market that is on fire, once said something so obvious that we have spent fifty years pretending it is profound: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. He said it in 1971, which means he said it before the personal computer, before the internet, before the smartphone, before the algorithm learned to fondle our dopamine receptors like a pickpocket who has fallen in love with your thigh. He saw the flood coming while the rest of us were still admiring the plumbing. And now, in this year of our lord two thousand and twenty-six, the flood is not coming. The flood is here, it has brought its extended family, it has eaten the couch, and it is asking why we have not installed the app that will help it digest the couch.

I have spoken of Simon before, I know. I am aware. I repeat myself the way a manic depressive repeats the same three songs at four in the morning, not because the songs have changed, but because I have not, and the walls of this Calcutta flat have not, and the ceiling has not, and somebody has to keep singing to the mold. So let me sing again, in a slightly tired, modified, Bengali-acerbic, repetitious tone, the tone of a man who has read too much and slept too little and who suspects that the entire edifice of human progress is a termite colony that has learned to file taxes.

There is going to be a glut. Not a glut of bread, not a glut of milk, not the honest glut of mangoes in a Haldia summer that rots because the trucks are stuck in a political rally. No, a glut of software, of applications, of services, of digital contraptions that purport to solve problems you did not know you had in ways that mostly do not work. The supply of software ideas, software products, software-related services, this supply is expanding like a gas in a vacuum, filling every corner of every possible niche, every crevice of human hesitation, every moment of boredom that used to be sacred because it was empty. And the products, oh, the products! Not rounded, not well-thought, not the polished pebbles of a craftsman who has spent years rubbing the stone against his own errors. No. Janky. Vibe-coded. Thrown against the wall to see what sticks, and what sticks is mostly wet spaghetti, and the wall is your attention, and the spaghetti is code that works on Tuesdays but not when it rains.

The barrier to entry has collapsed. Once, to make software, you had to know things. Difficult things. Things with names like "pointer arithmetic" and "race condition" and "undefined behavior," phrases that sound like the diagnosis of a particularly aggressive form of digital syphilis. You had to suffer. You had to spend nights debugging a missing semicolon while your youth curdled like milk left out in a Calcutta power cut. Now? Now you speak to the machine in plain English, or plain Hindi, or plain Bengali if you are feeling patriotic, and the machine spits out code that mostly works, mostly, the way a drunk man mostly walks in a straight line, which is to say he does until he does not, and then he is in the gutter wondering where his keys went. The machine does not know where the keys went either. The machine does not know what keys are. The machine has simply pattern-matched the shape of key-ness from the corpus of human text, and if the lock is imaginary, well, so is the key, and so, increasingly, is the job of the locksmith.

So the flood comes. Multifarious and many. Huge. Flooding every corner of every possible niche. An app to tell you when to drink water, because your ancestors who crossed deserts and oceans apparently could not manage thirst without a notification. An app to tell you how you feel, because feeling is now a data stream requiring analytics. An app that writes your emails, and an app that summarizes the emails that the first app wrote, and an app that deletes both and claims you never sent them, and an app that apologizes for the deletion, and an app that invoices for the apology. The snake is eating its own tail, and the tail is a subscription service, and the snake is wondering why it is still hungry.

And here is the rub, the friction, the sand in the Vaseline: the problem, as always, first will be the human finiteness. Attention span. That little candle of consciousness that flickers in the skull of a bipedal ape who thinks he is a rational agent but who is, in fact, a moist robot running on glucose and grudges. There are only so many hours in a day, only so many glances at a screen, only so many times a man can swipe left on the infinite before his thumb develops arthritis and his soul develops a callus. The attention economy is called an economy because it obeys the laws of scarcity, and the scarcity here is not of content, not of product, not of the janky and the vibe-coded and the hastily slapped, but of the human eyeball, the human ear, the human capacity to give a damn.

Capacity to consume. We cannot drink the ocean, though we are welcome to try, and many do, scrolling through the feed until the feed scrolls through them, until they are the feed, until the distinction between the man and the medium dissolves like a sugar cube in hot tea. Capacity to entertain. We bore so easily, we monkeys in shoes, we connoisseurs of the novel who tire of novelty before the packaging hits the bin. What delighted us Tuesday is landfill by Friday, and the software glut feeds on this disposability, this planned obsolescence of the human heart. Make it fast, make it cheap, make it now, because the attention span is a mayfly, and mayflies do not read terms and conditions.

Capacity to afford. Ah, there is the knife in the ribs, the twist of the wrist, the moment the satire stops being funny and starts being an autopsy. Because when most won't have a job anymore, when the white-collar work that once filled the middle-class belly has been automated, optimized, satisficed into the ether, who buys the software? Who subscribes to the service? The unemployed do not need project management tools. The redundant do not purchase premium tiers. The man whose job has been eaten by the very machine that promised to augment him, he does not need an app to tell him he is thirsty; he knows he is thirsty, and he knows the water costs money, and he knows the money is gone, and the app that tells him to hydrate is a mockery from the depths of a server farm that does not know his name.

Simon saw the attention problem. He saw that information consumes the attention of its recipients, that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention, and that attention must be rationed. But even Simon, with his Nobel and his bounded rationality, even he did not quite foresee the employment apocalypse that would follow, the way the glut of software would require a glut of buyers, and the buyers would be busy trying to become software themselves, or busy trying to survive the software, or busy driving taxis for algorithms that tell them where to go while the algorithm itself does not know the way to anywhere except the quarterly earnings report.

I send off these essays like little paper boats in an ocean where people have stopped looking for glass bottles with messages, or for that matter soaked surviving paper boats. I fold the paper, I write the words, I launch the vessel from the dirty mattress of my Calcutta existence, and the ocean is full of other boats, millions of them, billions, all carrying messages, all screaming "read me, read me," and the ocean does not care, and the sky does not care, and the fish have evolved to ignore paper. But hey. I am still alive. I am sitting, or lying, on this mattress that has known my contours longer than most relationships have known my name, and these thoughts disturb me, and I want people to know this is what I am worried about.

I am worried that we are building a cathedral of code on a foundation of attention that was never as solid as we pretended. I am worried that the glut is not a bug but a feature, that the machine produces because it can, and we consume because we cannot help ourselves, and the loop tightens, and the job disappears, and the subscription renews, and the water reminder pings in the dark.

Simon says: when a resource is free, it gets wasted. Attention was never free, but we priced it at zero, and now the bill is due, and the currency is time, and the time is running out, and the apps are multiplying like bacteria in a petri dish, like thoughts in a manic brain at three a.m., like paper boats in an ocean that has forgotten how to drown them.

The flood is here. I am still writing. The boat is soggy. The message is smudged. But if you find it, know that I warned you, repetitiously, acerbically, from the boondocks of South Calcutta, while the software sang its siren song and the jobs went down with the ship.

P.S. Herbert A. Simon, "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World," 1971. The man saw the flood coming. We built boats out of the floodwater and wondered why we were sinking.
