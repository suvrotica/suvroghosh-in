The mosquito net is never praised until something starts biting.

It hangs there, plain, practical, unfashionable. No one writes poetry to the mesh. No one calls it visionary. It does not remove the world. It only puts a thin, inspectable barrier between your blood and the appetite circling it.

Skepticism works like that.

It is not cynicism. Cynicism says everything is false and then goes to sleep pleased with itself. Skepticism stays awake enough to check the hole in the mesh. It asks: what is the claim, what is the evidence, who benefits, what would prove this wrong, and why does this comforting story require my obedience so quickly?

In Bengal, this is not always popular. We enjoy intelligence, but not always inspection. We enjoy debate, but often prefer our inherited comforts to survive the debate unchanged. A family can discuss politics for three hours and still become offended if someone asks whether the miracle cure, lucky stone, or ancestral certainty has any evidence behind it.

Evidence has poor manners. It enters rooms without touching the feet of tradition.

That is why a net is useful. Not a wall. A wall becomes fanaticism. A net allows air. It lets beauty through, memory through, affection through, uncertainty through. It only stops the thing trying to feed on you while humming near the ear.

A tired person is vulnerable to easy explanations. So is a frightened society. When money is tight, health uncertain, work unstable, and public systems unreliable, magical thinking arrives as emotional credit. Buy now, pay later. The interest is paid in bad decisions.

Skepticism cannot make the night pleasant. It cannot remove the heat or guarantee sleep. It simply prevents some bites. That is enough reason to keep it repaired.

The net has a small tear near the edge. I notice it before lying down.

That, too, is part of the practice.
