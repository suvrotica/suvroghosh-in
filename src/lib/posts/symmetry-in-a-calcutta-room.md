The mirror above the basin gives back the room with one quiet betrayal: left and right have changed places, but almost everything else has survived.

That survival is symmetry.

Symmetry is not merely prettiness. It is not a decorative border, a matching curtain, or the neatness of a school geometry diagram. Symmetry asks a harder question: what can you do to an object while preserving what matters?

Turn a square by a quarter turn. It looks the same. Turn it by half a turn. Same again. Flip it across a diagonal. Still the same in the sense that its structure has survived. A circle is even more generous. Rotate it by any angle and it refuses to look disturbed.

The object changes position. The structure remains.

That is the heart of the idea.

Mathematicians call the allowed moves transformations. A transformation may rotate, reflect, slide, stretch, permute, or otherwise rearrange something. If the transformation leaves the relevant structure unchanged, it is a symmetry of that object.

This begins in childhood with mirrors, folded paper, rangoli patterns, and the pleasing balance of shapes. Then the same idea grows up and walks into algebra, chemistry, physics, crystallography, quantum mechanics, particle theory, and those blackboards where a small dot can mean an entire system.

Take regular solids. In three dimensions there are exactly five Platonic solids: tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron. Not six. Not "many." Five. Space refuses the sixth application.

Why? Because regular faces cannot be packed around a corner in arbitrary ways and still close into a solid. Triangles allow several possibilities. Squares allow the cube. Pentagons allow the dodecahedron. Beyond that, the angles become too much. Geometry is not endlessly accommodating.

This is one lesson of symmetry: beauty often comes from restriction.

Wallpaper gives another example. A repeating pattern in the plane can be shifted, rotated, reflected, or glide-reflected, which means reflected and then shifted. Every repeating wallpaper pattern belongs to one of exactly seventeen wallpaper groups. Artisans used such patterns long before mathematicians classified them. The hand often knows before the theorem arrives with luggage.

Classification is one of the great human responses to complexity. The world comes as a crowd. We try to form a queue.

In mathematics, the queue becomes a group.

A group is a collection of moves that can be combined, undone, and repeated while staying within the same world. The rotations and reflections of a square form a group. Do one move, then another, and you still have an allowed move. Every move has an inverse. There is also a do-nothing move, which sounds unimpressive until you realize that mathematics insists even stillness must have paperwork.

Groups turn symmetry from visual intuition into algebra.

To make a group act on a space, we often use matrices. A matrix is a table of numbers that tells points where to go. It can rotate, reflect, shear, stretch, compress, or change coordinates. A 90-degree rotation in the plane can be written as a matrix. The numbers are not decoration. They are instructions.

This is where representation enters. A representation gives an abstract symmetry a concrete action. It says: here is how this group moves vectors. Here is how the symmetry appears in a particular space.

Once symmetry has representation, it can do work.

Continuous symmetry then deepens the story. A square has only a finite number of rotations that preserve it. A circle has infinitely many. A sphere has all rotations in three-dimensional space. The group SO(3), the group of rotations preserving length, angles, and orientation in three-dimensional space, appears in physics whenever angular momentum and rotational invariance enter the room.

That phrase, rotational invariance, means the laws do not care how the whole system is rotated. The coordinates may change. The underlying physics does not.

Physics is full of such invariants. Conservation laws often arise from symmetry. If time translation does not change the laws, energy conservation appears. If space translation does not change them, momentum conservation appears. If rotation does not change them, angular momentum conservation appears. The idea is precise in Noether's theorem, but the street-level version is simple: what stays unchanged tells you what is conserved.

This is why symmetry matters beyond visual pleasure.

It is a way of finding the durable part of change.

The periodic table, electron orbitals, and quantum states also carry symmetry. The world at small scales is not a collection of tiny hard balls behaving like common sense in miniature. It is ruled by structures, allowed states, transformations, and constraints. Many of the shapes and patterns in atomic physics are better understood through symmetry than through ordinary pictures.

Then come Dynkin diagrams.

They look almost offensively simple: dots and lines, sometimes with arrows. Yet they encode deep families of symmetry structures called Lie algebras. The families have names such as $A_n$, $B_n$, $C_n$, and $D_n$, along with exceptional cases such as $E_8$, $F_4$, and $G_2$. The names sound administrative. The contents are not.

This is mathematical compression with authority. A few marks can describe an enormous structure. Calcutta also teaches compression, though less formally. Say "summer afternoon" and a whole scene appears: hot walls, slow fan, tired street, damp shirt, metal tumbler, the sense that even time has become sticky. A tiny phrase holds a climate. A Dynkin diagram holds an algebraic climate.

Symmetry also appears where order itself becomes mysterious. The Yang-Baxter equation, central in parts of mathematical physics and knot theory, captures situations where different orders of pairwise interaction can lead to the same final result. In ordinary life, order matters painfully. Pay late, apologize late, cook late, arrive late: the sequence changes everything.

So it is startling when mathematics finds settings where the final structure is invariant under a change in the order of certain interactions.

That is not a small technical curiosity. It says hidden structure may survive beneath apparent sequence.

This is the larger invitation of symmetry. It asks: after transformation, what remains? After rotation, reflection, translation, deformation, collision, classification, or compression, what has not changed?

Modern life sells transformation constantly. Transform your work, your health, your face, your workflow, your nation, your attention span, your old laptop, your public image. Some change is real. Much of it is theater with a payment link.

Symmetry is useful because it does not applaud transformation immediately. It asks for invariants.

What was preserved?

What merely changed costume?

What structure survived the move?

What did the motion reveal?

In a Calcutta room, this question becomes less abstract than it first seems. The furniture shifts. The bills change. The phone changes. The weather changes its method of irritation. A person changes by age, work, exhaustion, reading, error, and small practical defeats. Yet something may remain invariant: a habit of attention, a suspicion of slogans, the need to understand difficult things in plain language, the refusal to call a pattern meaningless before looking closely.

That is not optimism. It is a smaller and sturdier thing.

Symmetry is disciplined change. It is the little accountant inside mathematics asking every disturbance for a receipt.

The mirror above the basin keeps giving the room back incorrectly and faithfully. The left side becomes right. The right side becomes left. The stain on the wall remains itself. The light falls where it fell before.

Something has moved.

Something has not.
