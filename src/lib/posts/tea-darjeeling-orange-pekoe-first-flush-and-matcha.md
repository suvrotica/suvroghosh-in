The tea label looks more educated than the person trying to read it. Orange Pekoe, First Flush, FTGFOP, muscatel, orthodox, green, matcha, white, oolong: the words gather on the packet as if the leaf has passed several competitive exams.

The simplest way in is this: most true tea comes from one plant, Camellia sinensis. The enormous differences in taste come from geography, cultivar, altitude, season, plucking standard, and what is done to the leaf after it is picked.

Tea categories are often different fates, not different plants.

A leaf that is withered, rolled, oxidized, and dried becomes black tea. A leaf heated early to stop oxidation becomes green tea. A leaf allowed partial oxidation becomes oolong. A leaf handled minimally becomes white tea. Matcha is a special case: shade-grown Japanese green tea processed into tencha, stripped of stems and veins, then ground into powder so the drinker consumes the leaf itself rather than only an infusion.

Darjeeling is prized because it resists being standardized into one blunt flavor. Grown in the Himalayan foothills, under altitude, cool air, seasonal variation, and a long history of Chinese-origin bushes and hybrids, Darjeeling can behave almost like wine. One garden, one month, one plucking standard, and one year can produce a cup quite unlike another. That variability is not a defect. It is the point.

Orange Pekoe is the term that confuses many drinkers first.

It does not mean orange-flavored. It does not promise citrus. It does not mean the brewed liquor must be orange. Pekoe is connected to old tea grading language around fine young leaves and buds. Orange is generally linked to prestige in old European trade usage, often associated with the Dutch House of Orange. In practice, Orange Pekoe is a leaf-grade term. It speaks about leaf style and size, not flavor.

This is where labels can mislead. Grade and quality are related but not identical. A high-sounding grade may describe the appearance of the leaf, the presence of tips, or the plucking standard. It cannot by itself tell you whether the garden was careful, whether the season was kind, whether the lot was skillfully manufactured, or whether the tea is fresh.

First flush refers to the earliest spring harvest in Darjeeling, when the bushes wake after winter and put out tender growth. These teas are often light, brisk, floral, greenish, and high-toned. Their liquor may be pale, which confuses people who think serious tea must look like polished wood. First flush is prized for brightness and lift.

Second flush arrives later. These teas are usually fuller, rounder, and more fruit-forward. This is the season most associated with Darjeeling's famous muscatel character. Muscatel refers to an aroma reminiscent of Muscat grapes: fruity, ripe, lightly winey, sometimes honeyed, sometimes floral or woody. A good second flush can feel as if grape skin, warm leaf, flower, and mountain air have all agreed to sit in the same cup.

That muscatel quality is not one magic molecule. It comes from plant genetics, altitude, temperature shifts, soil, rainfall, insect pressure, plucking interval, and oxidation chemistry. Mild feeding by leafhoppers can trigger defensive chemistry in the tea plant that changes aroma in desirable ways. The leaf responds to stress, and the cup becomes more interesting.

Manufacture matters. Orthodox tea preserves more of the leaf's twisted form and is common in fine Darjeeling. It tends to favor aroma and complexity. CTC tea chops leaves into small pellets designed for strength, color, speed, and consistency, especially for milk tea. CTC has its place. It is just solving a different problem from fine orthodox Darjeeling.

Oxidation also needs a clean explanation. Black tea is often called fermented in old tea language, but the main process is enzymatic oxidation, not microbial fermentation like yogurt or beer. Rolling damages leaf cells and allows enzymes to transform compounds that shape color, briskness, body, and aroma. Green tea stops that process early through heat. Japanese green teas are often steamed; many Chinese green teas are pan-fired.

Matcha goes further. The bushes are shaded before harvest, altering leaf chemistry, increasing chlorophyll, and favoring amino acids such as L-theanine. Since matcha is powdered leaf suspended in water, not a steeped leaf discarded afterward, the texture, caffeine delivery, flavor concentration, and body are different. Good matcha can be sweet-bitter, grassy, creamy, marine, and savory at once.

White tea is minimally processed, usually withered and dried with limited handling, often using buds and young leaves. It can be subtle, with notes of hay, melon, wildflower, cucumber skin, honey, or dried fruit. Oolong occupies the broad middle between green and black, from lightly oxidized and floral to darker, roasted, nutty, or fruit-heavy styles.

Herbal infusions deserve a small border check. Chamomile, peppermint, rooibos, hibiscus, and similar drinks may be lovely, but they are tisanes unless they come from Camellia sinensis. Kitchen language is forgiving. Classification is less sentimental.

The practical buying lesson is simple. A label may tell you origin, process, season, leaf grade, cultivar, or style. It may also tell you only what the seller wants you to imagine. Ask what the word is doing. Is Darjeeling naming place? Is first flush naming season? Is Orange Pekoe naming leaf grade? Is matcha naming a process and preparation? Once the categories separate, the shelf becomes less theatrical.

Tea is agricultural chemistry made drinkable. It is weather, leaf handling, trade history, and human appetite in a cup. Bengal's affection for Darjeeling carries empire, railways, plantations, class, taste, and memory, but the cup survives the baggage because the leaf itself can be extraordinary.

Once the words become legible, the tea becomes more interesting. The packet stops being a row of aristocratic titles and becomes a compact record of origin, timing, and handling. Then the cup can do what it was always meant to do: sit quietly in the hand and make the room a little more precise.
