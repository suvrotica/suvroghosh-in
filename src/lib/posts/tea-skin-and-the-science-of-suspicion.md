The tea had grown a skin again.

That thin beige film on milk tea floated on top with the confidence of a small office that has learned nobody will inspect it. I stood in the kitchen, half awake, looking at the cup and thinking: yes, even the tea arrives with a cover story.

In India, almost everything arrives wrapped in a claim.

Fresh. Original. No tension. Fixed tomorrow. Genuine. Approved. Best quality. Limited offer. Three minutes away. Minor problem. Small adjustment. Don't worry.

Suspicion is not cynicism here. Cynicism is a drawing-room luxury. Suspicion is practical hygiene. It is Dettol for the mind. It says: before you pay, sign, trust, forward, vote, believe, or hand over a document, ask what is being hidden under the clean shirt.

This does not mean everyone is evil. Most trouble is smaller and stickier than evil. It is the extra charge, the missing receipt, the inflated quote, the duplicate part, the service fee, the cousin who knows someone, the expert who cannot explain his expertise, the spiritual man with unusually concrete land interests.

The body notices before the mind arranges a theory. Something tightens when a stranger is too friendly. A small alarm rings when someone says no problem too quickly. The nervous system may be crude, but in a low-trust society crudeness becomes useful.

"No problem" is often the first footprint of the problem.

Proper suspicion is not paranoia. Paranoia soils everything. Useful suspicion is disciplined. Ask for details. Ask for it in writing. Check the spelling. Look at the date. Verify the number. Compare the claim with the incentive. Ask who benefits. Ask what would happen if the promise failed.

This is not negativity.

This is field science.

Every day becomes peer review conducted in heat: the bazaar, the clinic, the municipal office, the property desk, the app cab, the school counter, the bank branch, the water-filter repair call. You learn to read faces, phrases, hesitation, overconfidence, and too much English.

The scientific method, stripped of its lab coat, is suspicion with manners.

Show me.

Measure it.

Repeat it.

What are you not telling me?

That is why plain evidence feels rude in public life. It interrupts comfort. It makes the family success story look like a balance sheet. It makes the political speech show its wiring. It asks why the room smells odd after everyone has agreed to call it sandalwood.

Suspicion keeps you alive, but it does not make you popular. People prefer the easy nodder. The person who asks questions delays the meal, the deal, the ritual, the purchase, the vote, the speech. He is called difficult. Often he is only awake.

Still, one cannot live by distrust alone. A person must buy vegetables, call repairmen, take buses, answer unknown numbers sometimes, and accept that not every cup contains a conspiracy. Suspicion should be a handrail, not a cage.

The tea skin floated. I lifted it with a spoon and dropped it into the sink.

It landed with a wet little sound, like a promise that had lost its balance.
