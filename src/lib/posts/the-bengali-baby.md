The winter sun in Calcutta arrives through dust with the manners of an old visitor who knows the furniture is cracked but sits down anyway.

By four in the afternoon the city changes color. Rooftop tanks become silhouettes. Wires turn into handwriting. Broken walls gather a little dignity. Even the narrow lanes seem, for a few minutes, to be forgiven by the light. This is one of the city's cheaper miracles. We may not receive orderly pavements, clean drains, or public common sense, but the sun occasionally lends the place a borrowed nobility.

I tend to watch it too long.

Perhaps this is a Bengali weakness: to mistake light for argument, memory for evidence, melancholy for depth. Bengal has produced so much intelligence that it sometimes forgets to grow up. We love conversation, refinement, poetry, old grievances, cleverness, and the delicate art of explaining why action is vulgar until someone else has taken it. We can turn a staircase landing into a parliament and a cup of tea into a theory of civilization.

There is beauty in that. There is also delay.

The title of this essay is not an insult from outside. It is an accusation from inside the house. The Bengali baby is the part of us that wants the world to remain impressed by our sensitivity while someone else repairs the roof. It wants applause for intention. It wants credit for inheritance. It wants to be told that because the past contained books, songs, arguments, and luminous minds, the present is absolved from maintenance.

No society can live like that forever.

Nor can a person. A life also has its private Bengal: a shelf of old brightness, a talent half-used, a story about what might have happened if circumstances had been less damp, less expensive, less absurd. At some point the afternoon light becomes dangerous because it makes even delay look tender.

I have been guilty of this. I have stood at windows and mistaken perception for work. I have thought deeply and done slowly. I have understood traps without escaping them. That is a very Bengali form of failure: intelligent paralysis, annotated nicely.

Still, the light matters.

Without it, the world becomes only damage. With it, one can at least see the damage clearly. A cracked wall seen clearly is not repaired, but it has stopped pretending to be smooth. That is a beginning, if not a victory.

The sun drops. The room darkens. Somewhere a switch clicks, a pressure cooker calls out, and the city returns to its unfinished business.

The baby must grow, or at least stand up for a while without demanding praise.
