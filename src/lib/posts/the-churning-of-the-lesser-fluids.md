Rice starch had dried at the bottom of the cooker, a pale film clinging to aluminum like a small domestic accusation.

I stared at it longer than necessary. Some mornings, the mind prefers a stuck layer of starch to the larger tasks waiting on the laptop. The small mess is honest. It does not ask for a deck, a revised slide, a polite reply, or proof that the day has a purpose. It only asks to be scrubbed.

Naturally, I thought of the Samudra Manthan.

In the old story, gods and anti-gods churn the cosmic ocean using Mount Mandara and the serpent Vasuki. Before nectar appears, poison rises. Treasures emerge. Desire, labor, deception, exhaustion, and divine strategy all enter the same churn. The polished version makes it sound grand. But the detail that stays with me is simpler: the churning produces poison before nectar.

That feels accurate.

Many ordinary lives have the same order. First come the lesser fluids: sweat, tea, dishwater, cooking oil, rain leaking into a bucket, starch drying on metal, the strange thin liquid at the bottom of a bad day. The nectar is always promised later. After the next project. After the next payment. After the next improvement. After the next correction. After the next year.

One keeps churning.

Work often resembles this more than noble public speech admits. A person makes a slide, revises a paragraph, answers a message, prepares a file, cooks rice, cleans the pan, waits for feedback, redoes the same task in a slightly different color. Nothing mythic. Nothing heroic. Yet the motion continues: back and forth, back and forth, rope against mountain, hand against sponge, thought against a stubborn sentence.

Entropy is the science word hovering nearby. In ordinary language, systems tend to spread out, disorder, cool, separate, decay, and become less available for useful work unless energy is spent to maintain them. A room gathers dust. Tea cools. Rice starch hardens. A life without care turns into layers.

Understanding entropy does not save you from it.

It only gives the process a name.

That is both useful and rude.

The myth has a god who swallows poison to save the world. The kitchen has no such arrangement. There is only the person in front of the cooker, deciding whether to scrub now or let the crust become future archaeology. Most days, the blue-throated rescue does not arrive. One must become a smaller, less dramatic creature: someone who rinses, scrubs, rewrites, replies, boils water, and begins again.

The nectar may be a story.

The churning is real.

This is not despair. Despair would be too grand for a rice cooker. It is only proportion. The day is not redeemed because the basin becomes clean. The world does not change because a chart is rebuilt or a cup of tea is poured. But some small resistance to decay occurs. The metal shows through. The task moves one inch. The sink empties.

In Calcutta, morning enters with traffic, heat, voices, and the familiar disorder of a city that has never promised neatness. The laptop waits. The tea goes dark in the cup. The cooker sits in the sink, less accusatory now.

I pick up the sponge.

There is no nectar in it.

Only work.
