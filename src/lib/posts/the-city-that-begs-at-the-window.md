The palm hits the taxi window softly, then again, with more insistence. The traffic light is red. The driver looks ahead. The person outside waits. For forty seconds, the whole unfinished city leans against the glass.

Calcutta's street begging is not a plague of people. It is the visible interface of public failure.

A plague sounds as if something arrived from outside. What we see at traffic lights, railway platforms, hospital gates, temple entrances, markets, and under flyovers is not invasion. It is output. The city produces it, tolerates it, performs discomfort around it, and then asks ordinary citizens to make tiny moral payments so the scene can move forward.

The easy reactions are wrong.

Sentimental pity is too soft. It cannot see coercion, organized begging, child exploitation, intimidation, addiction, or the way visible misery can become a business model. Middle-class contempt is too stupid. It cannot see abandonment, disability, displacement, caste, gender exclusion, old age, migration, and the arithmetic of not having a room.

The honest reaction is harder: compassion and alarm at the same time.

The transgender person clapping at the window carries a whole social history into the crossing. In India, many hijra and transgender communities have survived at the edge of ritual, family, stigma, informal economy, and public performance. Some people bless. Some pressure. Some are themselves exploited. Some exploit the moment. A red light cannot untangle this. It only holds your vehicle still while poverty, gender exile, public fear, and exclusion become immediate.

Calling it culture is too easy. Culture is often the word we use when a survival mechanism has become old enough to look traditional. The better questions are uglier. Who had no ordinary route into income? Who was denied school, housing, and work? Who learned discomfort pays better than invisibility? Who benefits from the group structure? Who is protected? Who is trapped?

The child at the window is another accusation. Formal society says children belong in school. Lived society says the child may be earning, being watched, being rented, or simply present in a family where childhood is not fully protected from survival.

Public order matters too. A humane city cannot tell every citizen to endure harassment as authenticity. Women, elders, children, disabled people, and anxious travelers experience street pressure differently. Footpaths must be walkable. Traffic lights cannot become coercive toll booths. Hospitals and schools should not be surrounded by predatory desperation.

But order without rehabilitation is just sweeping with a stick.

Move people from one crossing and they appear at another. Criminalize homelessness and you have not created housing. Remove a child from one corner and you may not have created safety. Chase pavement dwellers before a VIP visit and you have staged cleanliness for a motorcade, not justice.

The practical work is not mysterious, only difficult: safe shelters, outreach teams, disability support, child protection, addiction services, documentation help, transgender livelihood pathways, employment routes, credible local organizations, and public spaces that are usable by the weak passerby and the weak beggar alike.

The middle class wants fewer people knocking on windows. Fewer people want the boring system required for fewer people to need the window.

Calcutta still has its terrible mercies. It lets modest lives float where costlier cities might drown them through invoices. But intimacy without procedure becomes exhaustion. Warmth without justice becomes dependency. Negotiability without rights keeps dignity underpriced.

The light turns green. The taxi moves. The person outside shifts to the next window. A decent city would not make every red light a little court where hunger, fear, guilt, fraud, pity, and failure all ask for judgment at once.
