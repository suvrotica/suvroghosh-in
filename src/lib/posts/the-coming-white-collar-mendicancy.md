The missing vacancy is colder than the layoff notice. A notice at least admits something happened. A missing vacancy is silence with a budget line behind it.

AI will not arrive for most office workers as a machine breaking down the door. It will arrive as the role not posted, the fresher not hired, the junior team made thinner, the contractor not renewed, the support queue supervised by fewer people, the first draft produced by a tool, the entry rung quietly removed.

For the rich, this is inconvenience. For the educated lower-middle class, it is weather entering through the window gap.

Many people are not middle class in the glossy advertisement sense. They are educated people holding the lower rungs with their teeth. They know English, Excel, reports, support tickets, billing, documentation, basic code, project coordination, client politeness, and email phrases that conceal panic. They have clean shirts and narrow margins.

This is the class AI may hurt quietly.

The old promise was simple enough to organize a life around: study, learn office manners, speak properly, swallow small humiliations, earn a salary, support the household, and avoid falling. The promise was never fair, but it had a staircase.

AI does not need to replace every worker to damage that staircase. It only has to remove enough beginner tasks, routine writing, checking, reporting, back-office reconciliation, and first-step work. A ladder does not become useless only when every rung disappears. Remove the bottom three and watch the young hover below it.

Work is not only income. It is rehearsal. People become competent by doing small, supervised, boring things badly, then less badly, then with judgment. If tools consume the boring first tasks, companies may save money now while burning tomorrow's apprenticeship.

Of course the tool is useful. AI can draft, summarize, classify, translate, search, compare, and test ideas. Refusing it out of pride is foolish. But "learn AI" is not a social policy. It is a verb tossed at the drowning.

The real question is who owns the tool, who captures the savings, and who is told to become resilient after the savings have left the room.

The descent may not be called unemployment. It may be called flexibility. Stable employee to contractor. Contractor to freelancer. Freelancer to platform worker. Platform worker to person refreshing an inbox at midnight. The words will be scented: portfolio career, fractional talent, independent consulting, creator economy.

Underneath is uncertainty.

One month the restaurant is skipped. Next month the dental work is delayed. Then a call is avoided because someone may ask how work is going. Then tea outside feels guilty, not unaffordable exactly, but guilty. Class can fall like a shirt fading in repeated washing. One day it is simply not the same color.

The lower-middle-class worker does not have a private island of savings. He has a few months, maybe less. Family duties, rent, health bills, school fees, old obligations, and a landlord who becomes weather after the due date. Hope is not legal tender.

The answer is not to smash the machine or worship it. Learn the tool. Learn where it lies. Learn where it helps. Learn where it produces confident nonsense. Bring judgment, because judgment is the thing employers will pretend to value only after they have automated the training ground.

Institutions must also stop pretending. Colleges need to build reasoning, writing, practical work, and apprenticeship. Employers should not destroy entry-level tasks without designing new paths into competence. Government needs portable benefits, payment protection for freelancers, and training tied to real demand rather than banners.

The fan turns above the desk. A person opens the job portal again. Respectability has become another way of asking.
