# The Curriculum Vitae of a Vanishing Man

Failure has the texture of a damp bus ticket peeled from the sole of a shoe, pulpy, gray, anonymously adhesive, bearing the purple corpse of a number that once promised a destination but now resembles a bruise inflicted by municipal stationery, and this morning—or afternoon, because chronology becomes theatrical when a man has nowhere urgent to appear—I feel that ticket stuck beneath my existence, following me without loyalty, purpose, or even the decency to become collectible.

I dislike the large ceremonial words.

They are handled too much.

They have become greasy from circulation, like coins passed through fish markets, pharmacies, wedding halls, condolence meetings, management seminars, dating applications, detergent advertisements, and those online declarations where a person professes devotion to coffee, an operating system, an air fryer, a leadership philosophy, a dog wearing spectacles, and the inspirational possibilities of Monday morning, until language itself begins to smell of ten thousand unwashed thumbs.

Still, there are old words whose original furniture remains somewhere inside me, covered in dust sheets, and I know what they meant before marketing departments entered with measuring tape and converted the rooms into premium studio apartments.

I know attachment.

I know allegiance.

I know the unreasonable tenderness one feels toward certain people, places, customs, mistakes, obsolete machines, chipped pavements, inherited phrases, bad habits, and entire countries that repeatedly place banana peels beneath their own feet and then appoint committees to investigate gravity.

There is a photograph from my annaprashan, probably taken in late 1975 or thereabouts, the exact date having dissolved into that blue-green chemical twilight in which old photographs preserve everything except certainty. My grandmother sits on the left wearing dark-rimmed glasses, her face composed in the grave, affectionate manner of someone temporarily entrusted with an unstable national asset; her friend sits on the right, smiling toward the camera; and between them I am being held upright in ceremonial clothes, my eyes turned toward something beyond the frame with the alert suspicion of a small animal discovering that existence consists largely of adults arranging it for photographs. The print is blotched, faded, slightly bruised by time. Behind us are fragments of walls, cloth, furniture, another body cut off by the edge—the ordinary backstage debris of a family ceremony that nobody imagined would someday become evidence in a private prosecution concerning the future of the infant.

That infant, naturally, knew nothing.

He had recently been introduced to rice.

Ambition would come later, being a less digestible carbohydrate.

There were expectations, although the most destructive were mine because I manufactured them personally in the primitive workshop of childhood, where the brain, still soft and combustible, converts every humiliation into legislation and every admired adult into architectural evidence that a human being can be deliberately designed, like a bridge, provided sufficient steel is inserted before the concrete dries.

I had plans against mediocrity.

Plans! Christ’s embroidered undergarments, I had plans the way governments have development plans: multicolored, numerically confident, laminated against doubt, and completely helpless before rainfall.

I would become capable.

That was the word.

Not famous, perhaps, because fame seemed vulgar even then, a brass band hired to announce indigestion, but capable in the unmistakable manner of a well-made instrument, a person whose presence altered an equation, repaired a mechanism, clarified confusion, constructed something durable, or at least prevented the surrounding incompetence from achieving complete sovereignty.

Instead I became ordinary.

Worse, I became ordinary after training myself not to be ordinary, which is like studying marine navigation for thirty years and then drowning in a bucket behind a sweet shop.

By the standards presently advertised, I have not merely failed. I have produced a deluxe commemorative edition of failure, printed on acid-free paper, stitched by hand, numbered, signed, and accompanied by a scholarly appendix explaining how the catastrophe might have been prevented had the subject possessed discipline, luck, charm, inherited property, emotional stability, commercial shamelessness, vascular youth, or the energetic buttocks required to remain seated through networking dinners.

The manic prosecutor inside my skull leaps onto the table, scattering papers, and declares that ordinary is not innocent, ordinary is what cowards call the sediment left after courage evaporates, ordinary is the gray intestinal paste of abandoned intention, ordinary is a person who owned books but became furniture, who understood the mechanism yet failed to pull the lever, who diagnosed the rot but lacked the muscle, money, timing, temperament, or intestinal brasswork to replace the beam before the roof descended upon his professionally informed head.

Then the depressive defense attorney rises very slowly, buttoning a coat that smells faintly of closed cupboards, and says nothing for several minutes because even self-defense requires energy, before suggesting that perhaps most lives are ordinary, that civilization is maintained by people whose names never become typefaces, that every celebrated figure stands upon an invisible pyramid of clerks, mechanics, nurses, drivers, coders, cooks, cleaners, teachers, technicians, and exhausted human beings whose biographies would not interest an investor unless rewritten as scalable suffering.

The prosecutor objects.

The judge is asleep.

I am all three and cannot afford lunch.

What offends me is not that I remained uncelebrated, because public applause is frequently the sound of one herd congratulating another herd for discovering a slightly improved fence, but that I did not become useful enough even by my own private accounting, did not convert whatever intelligence I possessed into a dependable shelter against contingency, did not accumulate the ordinary safeguards—money, standing, authority, a recognized place in the machinery—that allow a fifty-one-year-old man to suffer without his suffering becoming an administrative emergency.

This is where the modern world performs its favorite trick: it takes material insecurity, pours motivational syrup over it, and serves it back as a personality defect.

You lacked consistency.

You failed to optimize.

You did not build your brand.

You neglected to leverage your network.

You failed to monetize your authenticity.

Authenticity, incidentally, is now something one monetizes, which is like bottling municipal tap water under the label Natural River Memory and charging extra because the cap is beige.

The professional internet is particularly magnificent in this regard, a vast illuminated latrine of self-congratulation where vice presidents of strategic vapor congratulate directors of operational fog for completing certificates in inspirational gas, each message beginning with humility and ending with a photograph of a face swollen by triumph, while beneath it hundreds of people type “well deserved” with the mechanical obedience of temple bells operated by software.

Nobody writes: I received this position because my uncle knew the chairman.

Nobody announces: Delighted to share that I have converted fourteen years of aggressive mediocrity into a senior leadership role by remaining physically near powerful people and laughing before everyone else understood the joke.

Nobody posts: After a challenging quarter, our team successfully transferred blame to a contractor in another time zone.

No, the language remains perfumed, antiseptic, upholstered, a hotel lobby erected above a slaughterhouse.

I never mastered that language.

This may be moral failure disguised as moral superiority, and I know the disguise intimately because self-deception does not arrive wearing a false mustache; it arrives wearing one’s own face, only better lit.

Perhaps I call the game hollow because I lost.

Perhaps I denounce status because status declined to denounce anything on my behalf.

Perhaps my scruples were merely fear with a library card.

Perhaps I remained outside certain rooms not because I rejected them but because nobody held the door, and afterward I developed an intricate philosophy of corridors.

That is the difficulty with introspection: dig long enough and every virtue begins to resemble a rat tunnel connecting two embarrassments.

I can apologize, certainly, although apologies at this age possess the ceremonial usefulness of placing a handkerchief over a collapsed tram.

I am sorry I did not become what I indicated I might become.

I am sorry that potential, that most fraudulent of intellectual currencies, was issued in my name and never redeemed.

I am sorry to the imaginary tribunal whose members change according to my mood: severe ghosts during depression, contemptuous contemporaries during agitation, indifferent strangers during clarity, and during the occasional electrical carnival, the entire species, which I briefly suspect has been waiting for my unpublished memorandum on how existence should be reorganized.

That carnival is persuasive.

For several hours the connections blaze: failure joins economics, economics joins appetite, appetite joins evolution, evolution joins the Cambrian explosion, the Cambrian explosion joins office politics because both rewarded creatures that developed hard shells and alarming mouths, office politics joins language, language joins deceit, deceit joins civilization, civilization joins sewage, sewage joins public health, public health joins statistics, statistics joins the mathematical laundering of misery, and suddenly I understand everything, absolutely everything, including why cheap plastic chairs injure the soul and why every corporate logo eventually resembles either a sphincter or a sunrise.

Then the current falls.

The grand unified theory becomes three unfinished notes and an abandoned browser tab.

The magnificent engine, moments earlier capable of hauling history uphill, cannot lift a spoon.

The unsuccessful individual is then advised to improve his résumé with artificial intelligence, a machine trained upon the accumulated evidence of humanity so that it may explain, in cheerful bullet points, why humanity is no longer competitive.

“Emphasize measurable impact,” it says.

Very well.

I measurably aged.

I reduced my remaining lifetime by approximately one year per annum.

I maintained carbon-based operations despite irregular revenue.

I achieved cross-functional collaboration among dread, indigestion, insomnia, and misplaced pride.

I led a sustained initiative to convert oxygen into regret with minimal external funding.

The metrics are excellent.

The outcome is meat pudding.

There is an old philosophical belief that an examined life possesses some special value, but nobody mentions what happens when examination becomes repetitive industrial labor, when the mind installs floodlights, summons auditors, drills core samples through every decision, and produces nightly reports concluding that the property is structurally unsound yet unsuitable for demolition because several tenants—habit, appetite, fear, vanity—refuse to vacate.

Self-knowledge is advertised as liberation.

Often it is merely accurate imprisonment.

The man who misunderstands himself may at least walk into walls with confidence; the man who understands himself watches the wall approach, calculates the velocity, identifies the masonry, recalls the history of fired brick, criticizes the architect, and then walks into it anyway.

Bonk.

Scholarship completed.

I do not offer excuses because an excuse implies a clean causal chain, and lives do not possess clean causal chains; they possess tangled wiring installed by successive electricians, none of whom labeled the fuse box, after which one switch controls the bathroom light, another controls a forgotten bell in a distant building, and a third causes the neighborhood to smell briefly of scorched hair.

Was it temperament?

Bad judgment?

Pride?

Cowardice?

Economic accident?

A failure of persistence?

Excessive persistence in the wrong direction?

The answer is yes, no, sometimes, and also a pigeon landed somewhere in 1997, altering the universe by the width of one claw.

Causality is a magnificent bureaucrat. It can explain everything after refusing to prevent anything.

Still, I tried.

The sentence embarrasses me because it resembles the inscription on a school prize for losing gracefully, but it remains factually stubborn.

I tried intelligently and stupidly, nobly and selfishly, consistently and in spasms, with competence, delusion, sincerity, resentment, hope, and the sort of optimism that should require a prescription.

Trying does not guarantee dignity.

A mosquito tries.

A fungal infection tries.

A man opening a packet of biscuits from the wrong end may display extraordinary perseverance while accomplishing only crumbs and elevated bowel-language.

Yet effort was present.

Achievement was not.

The modern tribunal admits only the second as evidence.

So I recede—not romantically, not like a wounded prince entering fog, but administratively, like an obsolete form removed from circulation without announcement, my name gradually absent from invitations, discussions, searches, plans, and the little electrical weather of other people’s attention.

This is not tragedy.

Tragedy requires scale, spectators, costumes, perhaps a horse.

This is inventory shrinkage.

One ordinary unit misplaced between warehouse and retail.

And perhaps that is the final obscenity: not that I became nothing, because nobody becomes nothing while the kidneys continue their night shift, but that I became statistically normal in a culture where everyone must advertise exceptionality, a plain potato compelled to maintain a public profile among truffles wearing leadership badges.

I remain unconvinced by success and wounded by its absence.

I despise applause and notice when it goes elsewhere.

I reject the parade and resent not being offered a chair.

I claim no desire for monuments while privately checking whether the cement has arrived.

Contradiction is the only enterprise I have managed profitably.

In the annaprashan photograph, the infant remains suspended between two women’s steady hands, dressed for a future he cannot yet fail, his mouth perhaps still carrying the ceremonial grain of rice, his enormous eyes fixed beyond the camera upon some unidentified object that must have seemed, for one clean second, more interesting than destiny.

The photograph survived.

That was apparently the successful one.
