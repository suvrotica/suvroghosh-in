The phone glows before the tea cools.

That is the new morning arrangement. A cup, a screen, a body not yet fully convinced it has returned to the world. Messages. Headlines. Clips. Outrage. Small jokes. Large disasters. A sale. A face. A notification pretending to be a doorbell for the soul.

Dopamine has become one of those words everyone uses and few use carefully.

It is not simply pleasure juice, though the internet enjoys saying so. It is involved in reward, motivation, learning, anticipation, and the assignment of importance. The simplified version is enough for this essay: modern life has become very good at tugging the wanting system. Not satisfying it. Tugging it.

The wanting economy does not ask whether you are happy.

It asks whether you will click again.

This creates a strange bankruptcy. The day fills with small pulls and fewer deep satisfactions. Food becomes content. Music becomes background. Anger becomes fuel. News becomes a slot machine with moral vocabulary. Even rest becomes optimized, tracked, compared, and improved until it stops being rest.

In Calcutta, this sits oddly beside older forms of wanting.

The sweet shop window, the fish market, the bookstall, the cinema poster, the political banner, the coaching-center advertisement, the tea stall argument: the city has always known appetite. But older appetite had friction. You walked. You waited. You smelled the thing before you bought it. You argued with a person. The new feed removes friction and calls the removal freedom.

Friction had value.

It gave desire time to reveal whether it was real. The feed gives desire no such dignity. It supplies the next object before the previous one has landed. A mind trained this way becomes restless even in abundance. It has received too many prompts and too little nourishment.

The result is not always excitement. Often it is dullness.

Too many small rewards flatten the larger ones. A person can scroll through jokes and feel no laughter. Listen to music and feel no voltage. Eat and feel only completion. Argue and feel only residue. The machinery of wanting continues, but the world behind it loses depth.

This is not only individual weakness.

Platforms are designed. Notifications are designed. Infinite scroll is designed. Recommendation systems are designed. The modern attention economy is not a neutral field where people simply fail at discipline. It is an engineered environment built to turn attention into revenue.

The response cannot be puritanical.

I dislike purity campaigns. No phone, no sugar, no screen, no pleasure, no modernity. That way lies another kind of vanity. The practical question is smaller: which pulls leave me more alive, and which leave me emptier? Which habits create appetite for the world, and which merely scratch at the wanting system until it becomes raw?

Some pleasures return only when they are not crowded.

Tea without the phone. Music without the feed. A walk without converting it into proof. Food eaten as food. A conversation not mined for later display. A few pages read without stopping to become a public person. These are not noble acts. They are repairs to attention.

The wanting economy will not close.

It is too profitable, too clever, too woven into daily life. But a person can still refuse some of its smallest taxes. Not heroically. Just practically. Put the phone down before the tea is gone. Let one ordinary sensation arrive without competition.

The cup cools.

The hand remembers it is holding something real.
