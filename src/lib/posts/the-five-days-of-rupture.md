## I. Mahashashthi: The First Sound

In the mirror above his wife's dressing table, Prabir Bose watched his throat accept the thing that would kill him.

It was an ordinary swallow. His chin lifted, a little water escaped into his beard, and he wiped it away with the edge of his thumb. Outside the Ballygunge apartment, the dhakis had begun again. Their drums made the windows tremble in their expensive frames. Every few seconds the reflection of his face shook almost imperceptibly, as though someone behind the glass were trying to remember how to be him.

The last MOKSHA SLIM tablet had tasted faintly of mint.

He pressed the empty blister against his palm, counting the hollows without knowing why. Then he put it in the small brass wastebasket under the dressing table, where Sharmila discarded cotton pads and the hair that came out in her brush.

"Well?" she said.

He turned sideways.

She held a gold earring halfway to her ear. "What am I supposed to be looking at?"

"The kurta."

It was blue tussar, bought two years ago and worn once. He had been unable to fasten its lowest button last Puja. Now it closed, although closing and fitting were different things, and he stood unusually straight to conceal the distinction.

"You look very nice," she said.

This pleased him more than he wanted to show. At fifty-six, he had arrived at the age when a compliment from his wife could still make him feel that marriage was something he was doing well.

From the living room came the clatter of a dropped spoon. Their granddaughter was arguing with her mother about a plastic crown. The television showed a presenter under a pandal's artificial waterfall. Somewhere in the apartment, a message notification played the first four notes of a devotional song and stopped.

Nothing about the evening seemed capable of becoming history.

Prabir owned a packaging business, two apartments, and several firm opinions about people who took shortcuts. For the previous three weeks he had been taking a shortcut so small that he sometimes forgot he had taken it. The advertisement had appeared between a video about retirement income and a doctor's explanation of belly fat. There had been a green leaf, a white coat, and an animated waistline narrowing until the trousers fell down.

He had laughed at that.

The man in the white coat had said this supposedly Ayurvedic supplement worked better than GLP-1 medications. No injections, no starvation, no unpleasant conversation with a specialist. Ancient knowledge, modern confidence. The word *clinical* had appeared several times without bringing a clinic with it.

He could afford the injections. That was not the point. He did not want to become a man who required injections in order to occupy less room.

His granddaughter came in wearing the crown sideways.

"Dadu, are you ready?"

"Almost."

"You said almost before."

He picked her up, felt a small tightening low in his abdomen, and put her down again more carefully than he had lifted her.

"Getting heavy," he said.

"I'm growing."

The tightening passed.

On the dining table, covered dishes waited for their return. Luchi, alur dom, a bowl of chholar dal with coconut. Prabir lifted a lid and took a little potato with a spoon. The cook, Malati, caught him and smiled.

"You'll spoil dinner, dada."

"Dinner can survive a potato."

She asked whether she might leave before they returned. Her bus route was already jammed with pandal traffic. He told her to take money for an auto and not argue about it. She argued anyway, briefly and for form's sake, then thanked him.

Later, Sharmila would remember this. She would remember it with an almost aggressive precision, because the first people on the internet would call her husband greedy and vain, and she would need something small and actual to put against those words.

They left just after seven.

The city had dressed itself to be forgiven. Strings of lights crossed the road. A bamboo gateway wore the face of a palace. Schoolgirls in new saris stepped around a puddle with their sandals in their hands. Beyond an open pandal, the evening rites were underway, the goddess being welcomed amid incense and the sound of a conch.

Prabir watched a boy asleep against his father's shoulder, mouth open, entirely defeated by enjoyment.

"We used to do that with Mumpi," he told Sharmila.

"You used to hand her to me."

"History is being revised."

They had agreed to meet their daughter at a shopping mall before going north. She wanted a pair of shoes for the child. Prabir did not understand buying shoes on Shashthi evening, but he understood that understanding was no longer required of him.

In the underground parking lot the tightening returned.

It was not pain. It was the familiar, faintly insulting message the body sent when it had plans of its own.

He waited while the others got into the lift. He watched the number change. He breathed through his nose.

"You all go," he said. "I'll catch up."

Sharmila glanced at him, recognized the expression, and pointed toward the sign.

"Don't disappear."

The men's room on the next floor smelled of lemongrass. The music was softer there, an instrumental arrangement of a song his daughter had once loved. Under the washbasins, concealed lights gave the marble an unnatural, almost underwater glow.

An attendant was folding hand towels.

Prabir chose the farthest cubicle.

There are decisions a person makes a hundred thousand times without leaving a trace in memory. He would have been unable to explain why he chose that one. Greater privacy, perhaps. Distance from the door. The quiet animal preference for a corner.

He hung his phone on the little shelf. He undid the button of the blue kurta. He sat.

For a few moments there was only relief beginning.

Then it stopped.

The pressure remained, but something about it had changed. It no longer felt like a message from his body. It felt like something waiting for his body to finish speaking.

He looked down.

His phone lit up.

SHARMILA: *We're at the shoe shop.*

He began to type a reply, deleted it, and set the phone down. A bead of sweat moved along the inside of his elbow. He could hear someone washing his hands. He could hear the attendant saying, "Happy Puja, sir."

Prabir tried to stand.

At 8:17 p.m., the attendant heard a sound like a knuckle cracking behind a closed door.

There was no blast in the room. No shattered mirror. Nothing dramatic enough to belong to what had happened.

Prabir's hand struck the partition once.

"Dada?" the attendant said.

Something wet touched the floor on the other side.

The attendant stepped closer. A shoe was visible under the door, its polished toe pointing sideways. He called again. Prabir tried to answer, but the breath that should have carried the answer had become a thin, astonished whistling.

Blood reached the gap beneath the partition.

It did not spread all at once. It arrived in a narrow line, found a seam in the marble, and followed it toward the attendant's shoe.

He would remember the deliberateness of that movement long after he had forgotten what he shouted.

By the time security forced the door, Prabir had folded forward. His blue kurta was caught on the toilet-paper dispenser. His phone lay under one knee, still illuminated. There was enough blood to make the body look smaller than it should have been, as though some necessary portion of the man had already left.

He was alive when they lifted him.

He caught the attendant's sleeve.

"Sorry," he said.

The attendant bent to hear him.

"Sorry about the floor."

***

Dr. Leena Das had seen a man's hand detached by machinery, a woman brought in with burns concealed beneath a wedding sari, and a boy who insisted that the knife in his side belonged to someone else.

She had learned that astonishment was something to postpone.

Still, when they transferred Prabir onto the emergency trolley, she looked up at the paramedic and said, "What happened to him?"

"Collapsed in a toilet."

"Before that."

"They don't know."

She examined him, then looked again.

His abdomen was tense. His skin had the dull, grayish sheen of a man whose circulation was abandoning the less urgent parts of him. There was catastrophic injury deep in the pelvis, but no wound on the outside that could account for it. It looked as though violence had begun in a place where violence should have needed permission to enter.

The surgical team came. Then another consultant. The room acquired that peculiar silence which forms inside coordinated work when everyone understands how much is missing.

Leena asked about an assault. A fall. An object. Prabir was no longer able to answer.

His wife stood just beyond the curtain holding the child's plastic crown. Someone had given it to her in the confusion. She kept turning it over, searching its underside as though an instruction might be printed there.

"He was talking," she said. "In the car. He was perfectly well."

Leena did not say that perfectly well was often a phrase people used to describe the time before information arrived.

She said, "We're doing everything we can."

There were times when that sentence was an honest description and times when it was a place to hide. Tonight it was both.

At 9:06, while Prabir was in surgery, Leena received a call from a former classmate at a hotel clinic. A businessman had collapsed in a suite bathroom. Massive bleeding, no evident external injury. Did this sound like anything she had seen?

Leena looked through the glass doors of the operating-theater corridor.

"Where?"

Her classmate told her.

"Don't let them clean the bathroom yet."

At 9:41, there was a young woman from a rooftop restaurant. Her name was Neelanjana Roy. She had a small but profitable following online and had been filming the skyline when she bent forward, dropped her phone, and asked the waiter for help in a voice her followers would later replay millions of times.

The help did not arrive quickly enough.

At 10:23, a retired schoolteacher in Salt Lake was found by her husband. He called an ambulance, then their daughter in Pune, then the ambulance again. By the third call he was begging the person at the other end not to put him on hold.

The hold music was festive.

***

Inspector Farzana Ali reached the mall after ten.

The corridor outside the toilets had been screened off. Shoppers stood at a distance with their phones raised. Their faces held the same puzzled resentment Farzana had seen at road accidents: something terrible had happened, and it was inconveniencing people who had made other plans.

She asked security to move them back.

The attendant sat on a plastic chair. His name was Sanjoy. He had changed his shirt, but one sleeve of the old one protruded from the carrier bag at his feet.

"I didn't touch anything except him," he said.

"That's all right."

"They told me not to. Afterwards. But he was alive."

"You did the right thing."

He looked at her with immediate, desperate gratitude, and she wished she could afford to mean more than the narrow thing she meant.

Inside, a forensic photographer worked under the clean white ceiling. Farzana stood at the cubicle entrance without crossing it.

There was no damage to the wall.

Her first thought had been a concealed explosive, but the room would not support the thought. Her second was an assault, but the cameras outside would not support that. Prabir had walked in alone. Nobody had followed him before the attendant.

"A sound?" she asked Sanjoy when she came out.

He showed her his right hand and bent one finger with the other.

"Like that. But inside something."

"Inside what?"

He shook his head.

The head of mall security wanted to know whether they could reopen the other toilets.

"People have to use them," he said apologetically.

It was an entirely reasonable thing to say. Within twenty-four hours, Farzana would hear people discussing that fact as though it were a defect in human design.

On the way out she noticed a wellness kiosk beyond the escalators. A smiling cardboard woman held a narrow bottle against an impossibly flat stomach. The slogan was printed in gold.

*Lose the weight. Release the burden. Experience Moksha.*

Farzana looked at it for no longer than she looked at the shoe shop, the ice-cream counter, or the security guard eating a hurried dinner behind a pillar.

Later she would hate herself for that.

***

Tara Chatterjee was twenty-four and had spent the day telling women that the cream would make them look rested.

She worked at a cosmetics counter on the mall's ground floor. By ten-thirty the balls of her feet felt bruised. There was a red line at her waist where the uniform pressed into her skin. Her manager had recently suggested that she order a larger size, and then added that the next delivery might take a while.

He had said it kindly. This had made it harder to be angry.

She heard about the man in the toilet from a sales assistant who had heard from a guard. By the time the story reached her, the man had been attacked, or had injured himself, or had suffered from a condition nobody could name without laughing.

Tara did not laugh.

Her younger brother had arrived to meet her after work. Riju was twelve, solemn about electronics, and currently wearing a watch so large that it resembled a tracking device. He was seated on the floor behind the counter, eating chips from a packet he had smuggled past security.

"People are saying someone died upstairs," he told her.

"People say everything. Get up."

"You should see the group."

"I should see you standing."

He got up. She straightened his collar. They had planned to see the lights near home, just one pandal and then phuchka if the queue was reasonable. Their mother had made them promise not to stay out late.

In her bag, under a comb with three missing teeth, was a strip of MOKSHA SLIM. There were tablets left. She had meant to buy another strip before the price went up after Puja.

She had not wanted to be thin in any grand or consuming way. She wanted to bend at work without feeling the waistband. She wanted to wear the yellow sari a cousin had given her without three aunties discussing her arms. These had seemed modest wishes. The advertisement had treated them as problems for which a solution was now available.

At the exit she looked up at the glass levels of the mall.

Somewhere above them people were taking photographs of a closed door.

Riju took her hand to pull her toward the street.

For the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be, Tara would remember that he had been the one to reach first.

***

North of College Street, behind an old hospital's darkened administrative wing, Dr. Ishani Mukherjee was sitting in her car eating a banana when Leena called.

Ishani had spent the evening negotiating a bed for a patient who had arrived with less money than illness. The negotiation had involved three departments, a charity account, and a man who kept saying that policy was policy. She was tired enough to feel that speech itself had become an unnecessary extra duty.

"Tell me slowly," she said.

Leena told her.

Ishani put the unfinished banana in its skin on the passenger seat.

"Are you certain those are separate patients?"

"I know what a separate patient is."

"I know. I'm sorry."

Leena sent images through their secure clinical system. Ishani looked at them without immediately touching the screen. She had been a gastroenterologist for seventeen years. She knew the ordinary disguises of catastrophe: perforation, bleeding, a neglected symptom that turned out to have been a warning. What she saw here resembled several of those things and obeyed none of them.

She called back.

"Has anybody asked about supplements?"

"We're asking about everything."

"Ask again. Not medication. Say slimming pills, powders, herbal products. People don't always count those."

Then she got out of the car and went back into the hospital.

Its corridors smelled of phenyl, damp plaster, and food carried in steel containers. A ceiling fan clicked with every revolution. Near the outpatient stairs, a man was sleeping with his sandals tucked under his head. Beyond him, in a corner where an electrical outlet still worked, a family watched Puja coverage on a phone.

The first local case arrived as she reached emergency.

The patient was a school clerk named Ajit Dey. He was conscious, which made things worse. He could answer questions. He could identify exactly when the pain had begun. He could say that he had not fallen, had not been struck, had not put anything where he should not have put it.

He was more frightened of not being believed than of the blood.

Ishani crouched so that he did not have to lift his head.

"I believe you," she said.

Only then did he begin to cry.

While the surgical team prepared him, she asked his wife to empty the cloth bag she had brought. Medicines, prescriptions, an electricity bill, a small packet of fennel seeds. Then a green-and-gold blister strip.

"That is only herbal," the wife said. "For weight."

Ishani photographed it.

When she called Leena, the emergency physician answered before the first ring finished.

"Moksha," Leena said.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Leena added, "Bose's wife found the empty packet at home. The hotel patient had it. The influencer's assistant says she was promoting it. We're checking Salt Lake."

Ishani looked at the strip on the counter.

The tablets were tiny. She had to bring the packet nearer the light to see them properly. Through the clear plastic they looked clean, faintly pearlescent, each nestled in its separate pocket like something that had been thoughtfully protected from the world.

***

Under a mansion in North Calcutta, a man watched the first news bulletin with the sound turned down.

Above him, roots had entered the brickwork. A plaster nymph on the courtyard wall had lost both arms. The rooms on the upper floors smelled of mildew and old trunks, and rainwater collected in a blue bucket beneath a skylight. The house had once belonged to people who considered its decline somebody else's responsibility. Dr. Arindam Sen had found that arrangement useful.

He was fifty-one. He wore an ironed shirt, house slippers, and the expression of a person reviewing work submitted by an inferior colleague.

On the television, a reporter outside the mall said the word *mysterious* three times.

Sen smiled.

There were other screens in the room. He did not look at them for long. Their images were close, moist, and almost without orientation. On one, a field of pink darkness moved slowly past. On another, the picture trembled and went white.

He reached for his notebook.

His handwriting was small and orderly.

*They have begun with the wrong question,* he wrote. *They want to know what happened in the toilet.*

Above him, a dhak sounded from the lane. For an instant his face changed. A child's expression passed through it, quick and ugly with remembered effort.

Then it was gone.

At the hospital, Ishani received the last confirmation. The retired teacher in Salt Lake had taken the same product.

She placed the green-and-gold strip in a clear evidence bag and wrote its name on the outside.

MOKSHA SLIM.

It was the first thing they knew for certain.

It was already in thousands of homes.

## II. Mahasaptami: The City That Feared the Toilet

Biren Mondal knew where the city hid its keys.

There was a public lavatory near the ghat whose caretaker kept the spare behind a broken electrical box. The toilet at a small municipal office opened if you lifted the door before turning the lock. At a market off Shyambazar, the shopkeepers maintained a clean washroom upstairs and told customers that there was no toilet anywhere in the building.

Biren had cleaned that one too.

He was a contract sanitation worker, which meant he belonged to the city when something needed removing and to a contractor when he asked for wages. At forty-eight, his knees had become a second, less reasonable supervisor. He had taken a festival shift because his daughter had begun a hospitality course and the next installment was due.

On Saptami morning, he reached the river before sunrise.

The sky was the color of an unwashed glass. Men carried the nabapatrika down the steps for its ceremonial bath, the leaves moving gently above their shoulders. A conch sounded. On the far bank, windows appeared one by one through the dimness.

Biren stopped with his broom resting against his hip.

For a few minutes there was nothing to improve. People went about the ritual with the quiet concentration he liked best about Puja, before the amplifiers and traffic and competitive announcements began. An old man slipped on the lowest step and two strangers caught him. The banana leaves shook. Someone laughed softly. The bundle of nine plants was lifted clear of the water.

Then Biren's supervisor called.

"Don't open the toilets."

"Why?"

"Order."

"Whose?"

"Just don't."

At the lavatory, three people were waiting. One was an elderly woman who had come for the bathing ritual with her family. She stood with both hands clasped around the handle of her bag.

"Kaku, please," her granddaughter said.

He looked at the padlock. Overnight, somebody had stuck a printout on the wall. A photograph showed a white-tiled floor covered in red. Under it, a warning in Bengali said the municipal sewage system had been poisoned.

There was no name or number to call.

The old woman said, very quietly, "I can't go home like this."

Biren opened the door.

He had no idea whether he was helping her.

That uncertainty was the first new thing.

***

By eight in the morning, the city's bathrooms had acquired audiences.

Husbands stood outside doors asking whether their wives were all right. Mothers timed their children's visits. In an apartment block in Lake Town, the residents' association chained the ground-floor toilets and sent out a message congratulating itself on taking prompt preventive action.

The residents upstairs still had to use theirs.

In a house near Hatibagan, a family removed the latch from the only bathroom. Their teenage daughter sat on the edge of her bed holding the screwdriver, unable to decide whether she was more frightened of dying or of never again being allowed to close a door.

Videos arrived through WhatsApp faster than corrections. The Hooghly was poisoned. It was a curse attached to imported sanitary fittings. A parasite had come through the water. A foreign government had invented an intestinal fever. The sound of flushing activated it. Sound itself was dangerous. Do not allow children near running water.

Someone posted that the victims had all used Western toilets. A reply showed a death in a house with a squat latrine. The original post was edited to say that both kinds were dangerous.

People emptied medicine drawers onto dining tables. They held familiar tablets under phone cameras as though a sufficiently enlarged image might disclose intention.

The city had never been short of advice. Now every frightened person had become a distributor.

> **UNVERIFIED FORWARD — 8:32 A.M.**
>
> My cousin's friend's husband is a senior doctor. Do NOT go to the toilet until further notice. Share urgently. Media will not tell you.

> **HOSPITAL ADVISORY — 9:10 A.M.**
>
> A number of patients with severe internal injuries report using MOKSHA SLIM. Investigation is ongoing. Retain packaging and contact medical services. Avoiding the bathroom is not a protective measure. Do not attempt home remedies or stop prescribed treatment because of unverified messages.

The forward reached Tara first.

She was brushing her teeth when her mother called from the other room.

"Tara. Come here."

Her mother was holding the phone at arm's length, as she did whenever something on it displeased her. A newsreader stood beside a photograph of a green packet.

Tara stopped in the doorway.

The toothpaste in her mouth suddenly seemed impossible to swallow and equally impossible to spit out. She went back to the sink, rinsed, and watched the water disappear into the drain.

It was the same packet.

There were imitations, surely. Products looked like other products all the time. She went to her bag and checked the spelling. The logo. The gold line beneath the name.

Her fingers became clumsy.

"You've used this?" her mother asked.

Tara did not answer quickly enough.

"How many?"

"Ma."

"How many?"

She could not make the number mean anything. It belonged to the past, to little acts done without fear while she waited for the bus or washed a cup. Those moments had changed their nature while she was asleep. She imagined each one preserved somewhere inside her, patient and intact.

Riju came in carrying his charger.

"Didi?"

Their mother's face broke into an expression Tara had not seen since the day her father died. She began to cry before she made a sound.

Tara felt a movement low in her stomach.

All three of them looked down.

***

Ishani had stopped being able to count patients in her head.

The overnight cases had become a list; the list had become several lists; the lists no longer agreed. Some patients were dead. Some were in surgery. Some had taken the supplement and had no symptoms at all. Others were bleeding from ordinary conditions and arrived convinced that they had swallowed a device while taking a familiar prescription.

The people who could still walk formed the largest group.

They stood in the courtyard with packets, bottles, photographs, shopping receipts, and the kind of attention to their bodies that made every sensation intolerable.

"Can you scan me?"

"Can you take it out?"

"Can I go home?"

"If I have swallowed it, am I already dead?"

Ishani found that she could answer the last question more easily than the others.

"No. You're here. We're talking."

It was an answer about the present. She could offer no reliable tense beyond that.

In the operating theater, Ajit Dey had survived the night. The surgeons had repaired what they could and diverted the damaged bowel. His wife sat beside him holding his glasses, polishing lenses he did not need because his eyes remained closed.

On a tray in a secured room lay something recovered during the operation.

Under ordinary light it resembled an off-white speck. Magnification made it less comprehensible. The surface did not resolve into anything the pathologist recognized. It suggested purpose without revealing a maker's mark, a seam, or an arrangement that ought to have been possible at that size.

"Could it have come from an instrument?" Farzana asked.

"Not one of ours," the surgeon said.

"Could it have caused this?"

"Something did."

Ishani was watching Ajit's postoperative images. Another small anomaly appeared in a region the first examination had not explained. It might have been artifact. She wrote that possibility down carefully because it was still important to distinguish suspicion from knowledge.

Across the cases they had reviewed, the initial destruction centered on the terminal bowel. Something had arrived at the end of the digestive tract and done what no ordinary swallowed tablet could do.

The recovered speck made disease feel less likely. It did not make the alternative more believable.

Farzana's phone rang. She stepped into the corridor, listened, and returned.

"The product office is empty. The people whose names are on the lease say they rented a desk there. The endorsement doctor says his photograph was used without permission. We're checking that."

"The manufacturer?"

"There are several answers to that question."

"Which is right?"

"I don't know yet."

Ishani looked at her properly for the first time. Farzana had a shallow scratch on her wrist and a crease on one cheek where she had apparently fallen asleep against something hard. She seemed neither reassuring nor defeated. Ishani preferred that to both.

"Tell me when you do."

"You too."

Outside, a man was shouting that his packet had a genuine seal. He had paid more for the genuine one. Surely that counted for something.

***

Biren's daughter called at eleven.

Mitali had bought a green sari for Puja. It had small white flowers and a border she had described in considerable detail, none of which he had retained except the price. Now she wanted him to know that she would help serve the community meal at their neighborhood pandal that evening.

"Don't stay late," he said.

"You're staying late."

"I'm being paid."

"Eventually."

He smiled despite himself.

Then she asked whether he had seen the news.

He stopped smiling.

"You haven't taken that rubbish?"

The silence was enough.

"Mitali."

"It was herbal. Everybody at class was taking it."

"Why do you need to be smaller? Are you taking up too much of my house?"

It was meant as reassurance. It came out as anger.

She said that she should not have told him and ended the call.

He stood beside his cart in the noise of Shyambazar and looked at the phone. Around the crossing, the festival lights were visible even in daylight, pale shapes waiting for darkness to give them authority.

He called back.

She did not answer.

He called again.

When she finally picked up, he said, "I was frightened. That's why I spoke like that."

She breathed into the phone. He could hear traffic behind her too.

"I feel fine," she said.

"All right."

"I don't feel anything."

"All right."

He had never before understood that the same two words could mean please, please, please.

At the hospital, Tara was given a number written on a scrap of paper.

The number was two hundred and eleven.

She asked the volunteer whether that meant two hundred and ten people were ahead of her. He said no, not necessarily, and was pulled away before he could explain what else it might mean.

Her mother wanted to telephone an uncle who knew someone. Tara asked who. Her mother said she did not know yet.

Riju sat on a low wall beside a woman holding an empty supplement bottle. He offered her a biscuit. She stared at it, then began to apologize. He put the packet back in his pocket.

Tara's mouth was dry. She was afraid to drink. She was afraid not to drink. Her body had become a house in which she could hear someone moving, although she could not tell whether the sound came from a burglar or from the plumbing.

When Ishani reached her, Tara held out the packet before saying her name.

"How long have you been using it?"

Tara told her.

"Any severe pain? Bleeding?"

"No."

"All right. We need to examine you and record the exposure."

"Can you tell if it's still there?"

Ishani paused.

Tara would come to trust that pause more than anything the doctor said. It was not the hesitation of someone preparing a comforting lie. It was the effort of someone refusing one.

"Not reliably in every person."

"But you can look."

"We can assess you. A normal examination would not let me promise that there is no risk."

Her mother began asking a different version of the question, because surely there was some arrangement of words that would unlock a better answer.

Tara watched a porter wheel an elderly man past them. The man had a towel around his shoulders and bare feet. His wife followed with a pair of sandals held against her chest.

She thought, absurdly, that she had not washed her own work uniform.

The thought was so ordinary that it hurt.

"I just wanted it to fit," she told Ishani.

The doctor glanced at the narrow navy waistband, then looked back at her face.

"You don't have to defend that to me."

***

By late afternoon, Farzana had interviewed two distributors, three frightened clerks, and a wellness promoter who insisted that the word *recommend* did not constitute a medical recommendation.

He had made videos from the back of an expensive car. In person he was shorter than his videos and smelled of stale deodorant.

"I didn't know," he said.

"What did you check before telling people it was safe?"

"They sent documents."

"Did you read them?"

He began to cry.

She let him. Then she asked again.

He had not read them. He had been paid to hold a packet and say that his aunt had lost weight. He did not have the aunt he described. The payment had arrived on time. This had seemed to him an encouraging sign about the business.

In his messages was a voice note from a consultant who had corrected his pronunciation of the product name. The voice was male, educated, and faintly impatient. Farzana played it twice.

"Who is that?"

"Doctor Sen. I never met him."

"First name?"

"Arindam, I think."

The promoter looked at her face and hurried to add that Sen was a common name.

It was. So were death and error and the discovery that a person had mistaken familiarity for safety.

She sent the name to a colleague and went outside. A crowd had gathered in front of a closed pharmacy. Someone had written MURDERERS on its shutter. The pharmacist's daughter was pleading with a constable to let her get her father's insulin from the refrigerator inside.

Farzana made a path for her.

When the girl came out, a man shouted that she was removing evidence. Farzana turned on him with enough force in her voice to make him step back.

The girl hurried away without thanking her.

Farzana was glad. Gratitude would have taken time.

***

The neighborhood pandal behind Shyambazar had been built to resemble an old library.

Its false bookshelves were made of painted board. Along the entrance, enormous open books displayed lines of Bengali poetry. Volunteers had spent three nights getting the lighting right. From the street, the goddess seemed to stand inside a warm room in which someone had been reading for a very long time.

Biren had always liked this Puja. The organizers paid his team late, but they paid. One of the older women brought the cleaners tea in the same cups she used for everybody else. It was a small thing, and he resented knowing exactly how small.

Mitali was there in her green sari.

He had taken her to a nearby clinic after their call. The doctor recorded the supplement and advised medical follow-up, but she was well, and there were no beds for people who were well. There were scarcely beds for people who weren't. Mitali had refused to spend the rest of the day at home imagining herself dying.

"I'll be here," she said. "You'll be here. What is the difference?"

He could not answer without confessing that he wanted to keep her where he could see her breathe.

By evening the lane was full.

Some people had come because they did not believe the news. Some had come because they believed it and wanted to be near other people. A woman said that the goddess would protect her, then moved aside to let a volunteer carry a first-aid box through. Faith and practical arrangements occupied the same space without arguing.

The committee secretary spoke into the microphone. He urged everyone to remain calm. There had been no official order to close their Puja. Food was available at the rear. Anyone feeling unwell should tell a volunteer immediately.

Behind him, a sponsor banner carried the MOKSHA logo.

A young man climbed onto a chair and pulled it down.

The applause was relieved and unexpectedly loud.

Biren watched the folded banner pass from hand to hand. It looked harmless at last, deprived of its height. He wanted the same thing to be possible with fear.

At the toilet enclosure, a volunteer had put a chair across the entrance.

"No one should go in," he told Biren.

"Then where should they go?"

"It's for their safety."

"They will still need to go."

The volunteer was about twenty, pink-faced with anxiety. Biren recognized that he wanted to do the right thing and had settled for doing something visible.

He moved the chair.

"I'll stay here," he said. "You watch the queue outside. Don't let them block the lane."

The boy looked grateful for an instruction with edges.

Biren washed the floor, checked the water, and hung the key where everybody could see it.

He had sometimes locked toilets early. He thought about that now. There had been evenings when the bus home mattered more than the stranger approaching across the road. He could name reasons. He could still believe they were reasons. None of them helped him look at the empty lock tonight.

Mitali came to bring him tea.

"You're making a face," she said.

"It's my face."

"Then make another one."

He took the cup. She had pinned the sari badly at one shoulder. He reached to tell her, then stopped because she was old enough to be annoyed by it.

From the pandal came the dhak, steady and close. Incense drifted into the enclosure. The tea was too sweet. He drank it anyway.

The first scream came from beside the donation table.

Biren turned with the cup still in his hand.

A man in a cream panjabi stood very still. He seemed to be listening to something beneath his feet. Then his knees unlocked.

People reached for him.

Before they touched him, a woman near the shoe stand made the same small folding movement. A dark patch spread through the back of her sari. She looked over her shoulder, as if someone had struck her from behind, and reached for a bamboo post.

For several seconds the crowd did not understand that there were two emergencies.

Then a third person fell.

The dhaki stopped halfway through a beat.

In the sudden gap, Biren heard the little sound.

It came from his daughter.

Mitali set the tea tray down with surprising care. Her eyes found his. Her mouth opened, but what came out was a breath so shallow it scarcely made a sound.

"Baba."

He caught her before she reached the floor.

Warmth spread through the cloth against his forearm. Her body tried to curl around an injury it could neither escape nor understand. He lowered her on her side, shouting for help, and saw that she was staring at the entrance of the pandal.

"I didn't go," she said.

"Don't talk."

"I didn't go to the toilet."

Across the lane, someone screamed that the air was poisoned.

The crowd moved.

It did not move as people normally move, each person selecting a direction. It shifted as a single structure whose supports had been kicked away. A railing went down. The chair the volunteer had removed from the toilets disappeared under feet. A child lost a sandal. A man bent to lift him and vanished beneath the shoulders of the people behind.

Biren held Mitali against the base of the wall.

He could not lift her. He could not leave her. He could not make the people understand that the thing they were fleeing might already be traveling inside them.

The young volunteer climbed onto the donation table and began shouting instructions. His voice cracked. He kept going. Another volunteer pulled open a side curtain and cut through a decorative board so people could escape into the neighboring courtyard.

The old woman who brought tea knelt beside Biren. She took off her shawl and helped him cover Mitali.

"She's here," she said. "Look at her. She's here."

He did.

Mitali's lips were moving.

He bent his head close enough to feel her breath.

"Don't let them look."

He put his body between his daughter and the phones.

The recording reached Ishani while she was examining Tara.

At first she thought the notification was another request for a bed. Then she saw the location, then the first frames. Farzana had forwarded it with a single line.

*Several at once. None in a bathroom.*

Tara watched the doctor's face.

"What?"

Ishani put the phone away.

"There have been more cases."

From the courtyard came the rising noise of people receiving the same video.

Tara felt an ordinary bubble of air move through her abdomen.

She caught Ishani's wrist.

The doctor did not pull away.

On the phone, hidden in Ishani's pocket, the clip began playing again. The drums. The first scream. The strange silence before the crowd understood.

Thousands of people watched it before the first ambulance reached the lane.

For two days Calcutta had been afraid of a room.

Now it understood that there was no room it could leave.

## III. Mahaashtami: The Night of Ten Thousand Screams

On Ashtami morning, someone placed a bowl of shiuli flowers on the nurses' station.

Nobody knew who had brought it. The flowers had white petals and orange stalks, and a few were already bruising at the edges. They stood between a half-empty box of gloves and a register of patients transferred overnight.

Ishani smelled them before she saw them.

For one painful instant she was in her mother's courtyard, eight years old, collecting flowers in a steel plate before breakfast. Then somebody called her name and she was forty-six, wearing yesterday's clothes under a fresh gown, with dried blood at the edge of one shoe.

Mitali Mondal had survived surgery.

That statement occupied several pages of work and suffering. It did not mean that she was safe. It meant that the surgeons had stopped the bleeding they could reach, removed damaged tissue, and given her a route through the next hours. There was a bag against her abdomen now. Biren kept looking at it and then forcing his gaze back to her face.

"Will she have it forever?" he asked.

"We don't know yet."

"She has classes."

"I know."

"She has to go to class."

It was the future he could picture. Ishani did not take it away from him.

Across the ward, Ajit Dey had opened his eyes. His wife was feeding him small pieces of information: where he was, who had called, that the house was locked, that he did not have to worry about the ceiling fan he had left on.

He listened with an expression of exhausted courtesy.

Then he asked whether he could be alone for a minute.

His wife froze.

Ishani saw it happen: the ordinary wish for privacy arriving in a room where privacy had become indistinguishable from abandonment.

"I'll be just outside the curtain," the wife said.

"I know."

"I'll hear you."

"I know."

She stepped away but left a narrow gap in the curtain. He turned his head so he would not have to see it.

***

In a lane near Kumartuli, a priest began pushpanjali for the people who had remained.

There were eleven of them. The microphone had failed, so he spoke in his ordinary voice. They repeated the words and offered flowers. Behind the altar, someone had stacked cartons of drinking water for the hospital volunteers.

A potter named Haripada Pal had slept beside those cartons. Most of his workshop was empty now, the completed idols carried away before the festival. In the back stood an unfinished clay hand, palm open, its fingers wrapped in damp cloth.

Biren had stopped there on his way to fetch clothes for Mitali.

Haripada offered him tea. Biren said he could not stay. Then he stayed anyway, sitting on a low stool, because his legs had begun to tremble.

The old potter listened without interrupting.

"They took my daughter's insides out," Biren said.

It was not medically accurate. It was the only sentence that corresponded to what had happened to him.

Haripada put a hand on his shoulder.

For a moment Biren wanted to hit him. The impulse shocked him with its uselessness. Here was a hand, a cup, an old man's attempt to give him somewhere to sit, and he wanted to strike something that could feel it.

Instead he put the cup down and covered his face.

Outside, the last responses of the anjali drifted through the lane. The sound did not cure anything. It gave the morning a shape that had existed before this one.

When Biren stood to leave, Haripada handed him a clean towel from the workshop.

"For her," he said.

Biren took it. He would remember, later, how near he had come to refusing.

***

Tara had become useful because she could write clearly.

The hospital's registration staff were overwhelmed. People who had swallowed the supplement needed to be recorded, contacted, and separated in the paperwork from people who had already been injured. The categories were provisional and kept changing. Tara knew how to ask for a telephone number twice without sounding as though she blamed someone for forgetting it.

She sat at a borrowed school desk near the courtyard.

Her mother sat beside her. Riju helped put completed forms in a box. He had written EXPOSURE REGISTER on it with such concentration that nobody told him the spelling was slightly crooked.

Every few minutes Tara became certain she was about to die.

Then someone asked where to put an address, and she answered, and the certainty receded.

Her examination had found no present injury. There was no sound basis for admitting every exposed person, and no capacity to do it. She had stayed because home contained a bathroom and silence. Here, at least, there were people whose fear had different faces.

A woman in a beautifully draped sari refused to give her weight.

"That's not necessary on this sheet," Tara said.

The woman began to cry with relief.

A delivery rider wanted to know whether the supplement counted if he had taken it only because someone gave him a free packet. A man who sold rolls said that he had hoped less weight would help his knees. A nurse from another hospital arrived in uniform, placed her empty strip on the desk, and asked Tara not to tell anyone she knew.

There was no shared appetite for vanity here. There were wages, marriage proposals, aching joints, relatives, uniforms, photographs, a promise that a person might be allowed to feel less ashamed.

The promise had been priced to travel.

At noon a man came carrying a packet of his mother's heart medicine. He had thrown away every tablet in the house except this one. He wanted them to test it.

"Has she taken MOKSHA SLIM?" Tara asked.

"No."

"This register is for that."

"But they can put it in anything."

She had no answer he would believe.

He began to shout. Riju stood up. Their mother pulled him back down by the wrist.

Farzana arrived before the argument worsened. She took the man aside and found someone who could speak to him about his mother's prescription. Then she asked Tara whether anyone had photographed the pages of the register.

"Why?"

"Because people are being turned out of lodgings. Drivers are refusing passengers who say they've taken it. Keep the names covered."

Tara put a clean sheet over the page.

She had been thinking of the list as evidence that they existed.

She had not considered that it could become a list of people to fear.

***

The first report from the recovered material reached Ishani in the afternoon.

It came with qualifications, which made her trust it more. The examining team could establish that the material was manufactured. They could not explain its full composition or function. Other hospitals had recovered similar remnants. Their relationship to the supplement was no longer a loose association. The same impossible objects had been found in unopened tablets held as evidence.

There was something else.

One specialist believed the damaged remnant contained a structure that had collected images.

"Images of what?" Ishani asked over the phone.

The answer came after a long silence.

"Whatever was around it."

She looked through the glass at Mitali sleeping.

All medicine involved some negotiated surrender of privacy. A patient undressed, opened a mouth, consented to a camera traveling where eyes were never meant to go. The negotiation mattered. It was the difference between care and trespass.

Someone had abolished it.

She imagined Ajit, afraid she would accuse him of bringing this on himself, while something inside him looked around.

"Is it still transmitting?"

"We can't answer that."

"Could he be watching now?"

"We can't answer that either."

She thanked the specialist because politeness was a structure she could still use, then went to find Farzana.

The inspector was in the stairwell with a laptop balanced on the windowsill.

On its screen was a photograph of Arindam Sen receiving an award in an American university auditorium. He stood beside a small robotic instrument, one hand resting on its case. His expression was reserved, almost embarrassed by the attention.

"That's him?" Ishani asked.

"The voice matches a public lecture. His former company has confirmed that he consulted for them. We're getting records through the appropriate people."

She turned the laptop slightly.

Sen had trained in medicine before moving into biomedical robotics. His medical license had been revoked after investigators established that he had used patients in research without meaningful consent and concealed serious harm. His research appointments had ended. So had the grants, the invitations, the marriage.

There were gaps in the record associated with a classified American program. Its purpose was described only in the guarded language of remote observation and intervention. The public documents did not explain what intervention meant.

Ishani thought of the sound behind the cubicle door.

"He helped build this there?"

"Something related. We don't know how much."

"And then they let him leave?"

Farzana looked at her.

"There are several countries in that question."

On the next page, Sen's lawyer had argued that his client lived with bipolar I disorder and had experienced periods of severe illness. An appended timeline recorded the ethical breaches over several years. Many had occurred during periods in which colleagues described him as steady, organized, and fully capable of understanding his decisions.

Ishani read the dates.

This was not a five-minute catastrophe inside one man's head. It was sustained work. People had been hurt on Tuesdays when he had slept well and on Thursdays when he had answered emails and paid the electricity bill.

At the bottom of one deposition, a former colleague had written: *He believed that the importance of the question made consent an inconvenience.*

Ishani closed the page.

"We need to find him."

"We're looking."

Below them, someone began to scream for a doctor.

They both went down.

***

In 1984, when Arindam Sen was nine, his mother bought him white shorts for Puja.

He hated them before anything happened. They were stiff, the waistband scratched, and the buttons were too small for his fingers when he was frightened. His father said that he looked smart. His mother rubbed the waist between her hands to soften it.

For much of that year, Arindam had arranged his days around the possibility of needing a toilet and the equal possibility of being unable to use one. Pain could keep him bent over a desk while other children ran outside. On better days he remained afraid of the next bad one. Adults offered remedies, advice, irritation. He learned to recognize the moment when concern became boredom.

That Ashtami they went pandal-hopping with relatives.

The pain began near dusk.

At first he tried to keep walking. His cousins were buying toy pistols. Someone had given him a paper cone of chanachur. He held it without eating while the pressure became a private, overwhelming emergency.

He told his mother.

They asked at a sweet shop. No toilet. At a house with a lighted courtyard. Family only. At a club, a man waved them around to the side, where another man told them that the facilities were for members.

His mother said please.

Arindam would remember the word more clearly than her face.

The man at the club looked down at him. "Couldn't you go before leaving home?"

His mother tried to explain. The man looked past her toward someone important.

Arindam felt the first warmth inside the white shorts.

What happened next survived in him without sequence. His cousin laughing, then stopping. A stranger's exclamation. The cone of chanachur splitting under his hand. His mother putting herself in front of him. Someone saying, "Take him away from here."

A woman with red bangles came from a doorway with a length of cloth. She said something sharp to the men and helped his mother wrap it around him.

Years later he would remember the bangles but omit the woman.

At home, his mother washed him in the courtyard bathroom. He cried angrily, more humiliated by her tenderness than by the water. She told him that bodies did these things, that nothing wicked had happened, that he was her son.

He did not believe her.

Somewhere outside, the celebration continued. That was the part he could not forgive. Nothing had stopped. His suffering had not altered the drums.

As an adult, he would turn that fact into a theory of civilization.

Before he did, it was simply a child sitting on a low stool, listening to his mother rinse the shorts.

***

There had been other possible lives.

For several years in America, Sen wrote proposals for devices that would make examinations less painful. He learned that an instrument could be technically elegant and still be an indignity to the person who had to endure it. He gave a lecture about that. Students applauded.

During a severe manic episode in his thirties, he slept scarcely at all and believed he could solve problems that had resisted entire laboratories. Afterward came a depression that made answering the telephone feel like lifting a beam off his chest. Treatment helped him return to work. He had colleagues who covered for him, a wife who sat beside him, and stretches of ordinary life that he later edited out of his account of universal abandonment.

His cruelty did not disappear when he recovered.

It became better organized.

After the first ethical complaint, he wrote that people who benefited from research should not pretend to be shocked by its costs. After the second, he used the word *ingratitude*. When a harmed participant asked to meet him, he referred her to the lawyers.

The irreversible moment came much later, in a rented room after his career had ended.

He had been drafting a proposal for a foundation that would fund accessible public toilets in the city where he grew up. He had photographs, expense estimates, letters from people who had tried similar work. The folder was real. So was the afternoon he spent reading it.

He called a former associate about funding. The associate listened, then said he would need a local partner who could be trusted.

Sen heard the word *need* as an insult.

After the call he sat in the kitchen until the refrigerator motor stopped. He had slept seven hours the previous night. He was taking his medication. There was no racing thought, no voice, no revelation.

He opened another folder.

He chose the work that would make people beg.

Later he would describe the choice as destiny, because destiny could not be cross-examined about alternatives.

> **PRIVATE JOURNAL — UNDATED**
>
> Repair would allow them to thank me and go on living. They would put a plaque over a door. My name above their waste. They would convert the accusation into a facility.
>
> I no longer intend to improve their city.

Now, beneath the mansion, Sen watched the evening approach through the footage of other people's Puja.

He had selected the festival because it filled the streets and hospitals with the city at its most confident, because people made promises to themselves about renewal, because there would be cameras everywhere. The goddess had nothing to do with it. He had made use of an occasion on which people expected to be together.

One of the screens beside him displayed a patient's identifier twice.

He frowned.

He checked the associated record. An earlier event was marked complete. The second image should not have existed.

He wrote *duplication in display* in the margin of his notebook.

Then he underlined it.

***

The evening's first power failure was local.

A cable failed in a neighborhood where generators were already running. An ambulance found the road blocked by vehicles whose drivers had abandoned them to look for relatives. A hospital waiting for replacement fuel could not get its delivery through. These were familiar failures, each survivable by itself.

Then the next wave began.

It happened in enough places close together that later nobody could establish a clean beginning. A restaurant near Park Street. A bus halted at a signal. A woman standing before her dressing-table mirror. A man who had decided to spend the evening in the street because he no longer felt safe at home.

Some fell without making a sound. Some cried out. Some remained standing, holding the lower part of their bodies with the shocked, protective instinct of people trying to stop themselves from coming apart.

The wounds did not resemble an attack from outside. Clothing remained almost intact until blood found its way through it. A man on a bus pressed his hands into his lap, staring at what pooled between his fingers. A woman in a rented room crawled toward her phone and could not stretch far enough to reach it. In a hotel, a guest's body struck the shower glass, and the running water continued to warm his feet.

Behind one locked door, a daughter heard her father trying to turn the latch. It clicked once, twice, then stopped. She would live for years with the conviction that if she had reached him between the first and second clicks, the world would have kept its shape.

The drums continued for a while because the dhakis could not hear the screams over their own playing.

Then they stopped too.

Conch shells, sirens, fireworks, shouting: the city had no unused sound with which to announce what was happening. Each noise could still belong to celebration until somebody close enough saw a face.

At a Metro entrance, the crowd learned that the gates were closing after injuries on the platforms. People at the back kept pushing because they could not see the locked grille. Those in front tried to turn sideways, tried to make their rib cages narrower, tried to persuade the people behind them that breathing was a practical requirement.

At Howrah Bridge, headlights formed a solid line. Rumors traveled between vehicles. The railway was evacuating everyone. The railway had stopped. The bridge was closing. The bridge was the only safe place because the river interfered with whatever was doing it.

Nobody knew who had started that last rumor. People climbed out of buses to believe it more efficiently.

By the time authorities opened a route for ambulances, the route contained people who had nowhere else to stand.

***

At the hospital, the lights went out while Ishani was speaking to a man who refused treatment.

He had a condition unrelated to the supplement. He needed urgent care. He believed that every medical product had been compromised and that allowing anything into his body would be surrender.

"You don't understand," he told her.

"I'm trying to."

"You keep wanting us to trust you."

Then the room disappeared.

For several seconds she heard him breathing. Somewhere a metal tray fell. A woman called a child's name. The emergency supply restored light to part of the ward, leaving the corridor beyond it dark.

The man looked toward that corridor.

"See?"

Ishani wanted to say that a power failure was not evidence. She wanted to say several true things that would do nothing for him. Instead she sat down so that she would no longer be looming over his bed.

"Tell me what you're most afraid of," she said.

"That you'll give me something and then go away."

She stayed until he agreed to let the team continue.

It took eleven minutes.

In those eleven minutes, people elsewhere needed her. She knew it while she sat there. It was possible to do something right and still feel that you were stealing.

In the courtyard, the new arrivals surged toward the emergency entrance.

Tara's desk had been moved against the wall. She and her mother were helping keep a narrow passage clear when a man being carried between two relatives went limp. Someone saw the blood. Someone shouted *It's spreading*. A group near the gate tried to run out against the people coming in.

Riju had gone to the volunteer table for water.

Tara saw the box he had been carrying fall.

She saw the bottles roll.

Then she could no longer see him.

She called his name once, in annoyance, as though he had hidden for a joke. The second time she heard herself and knew.

Farzana and two porters were trying to make the crowd stop moving. A volunteer climbed onto the wall and screamed that there was no infection in the courtyard. Nobody could hear the whole sentence.

Tara pushed toward the fallen bottles.

A man's shoulder struck her face. Her mother's fingers slipped from her arm. She found herself pressed against the desk, unable to lift her chest enough to shout. The edge of the wood dug into her hip.

For one moment she became certain the pill had begun.

Then Farzana caught her under the arms and pulled her into the narrow shelter behind a pillar.

"My brother."

The inspector turned.

A porter was carrying Riju through the gap they had made. His oversized watch flashed as his arm moved.

Tara followed without feeling her feet.

Inside, the doctors laid him on a trolley. They worked. She recognized procedures without understanding them. A woman asked his name. Tara answered. Somebody asked her to move back, and she did, furious at herself for obeying.

She waited for Riju to complain.

He hated being called a little boy. He hated strangers touching his watch. There were so many things in the room he should have objected to that she could not understand his silence.

Her mother arrived and made a sound Tara had never heard from a human throat.

The doctors continued for a while longer.

Later, one of them spoke very gently.

Tara hated the gentleness first. The words took longer to reach her.

***

Ishani found them in the small room beside emergency.

Riju lay under a sheet with his face uncovered. His mother was smoothing his hair into the shape she preferred. Tara stood near the wall, holding the watch. One of its buttons had been damaged, and the little alarm kept sounding at irregular intervals.

"He didn't take it," Tara said.

"I know."

"Put that down somewhere. In your register. He didn't take anything."

Ishani nodded.

"He offered someone a biscuit this morning. She wouldn't take it."

The detail arrived without explanation. Ishani understood that it was one of the things Tara could not bear to let disappear.

She stayed.

After a while a nurse came to the door. There were more patients. There would always be more patients until there were fewer living people than injured ones, and perhaps even then.

Ishani told Tara where she could be found.

Tara did not look at her.

In the corridor, a junior doctor was crying into a paper towel. He had asked to leave. His wife was exposed; his parents were alone. He kept saying that he knew this was not an excuse.

"It isn't an excuse," Ishani said. "It's your family."

"Then can I go?"

Behind them, someone called for another pair of hands.

She could not answer immediately.

She would remember that about herself.

***

Near midnight, Farzana received the invoices.

They were imperfect, duplicated, and full of trading names that required checking. At first she thought several documents concerned different products. Then the records linked them through the same supplier and marketing company.

The addresses were in Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, and cities farther away.

She called Ishani into the corridor.

"It's not only here."

Ishani read the page.

At the bottom, a promotional note promised special festival allocations. The sentence had been written before any of its nouns became terrible.

Farzana's phone vibrated again.

A clinician in another city had reported an unexplained pelvic injury. The patient had taken a slimming product under a different label. Verification was pending.

From somewhere beyond the hospital walls came a sound like thunder. It might have been a transformer, a firework, a vehicle collision. Neither woman turned toward it.

They were looking at the addresses.

Calcutta had mistaken the beginning for the boundary.

## IV. Mahanavami: The God of Empty Bowels

By Navami morning, the city had begun to make rules it could not explain.

Some neighborhoods refused entry to anyone carrying a medicine bag. Others demanded proof that visitors had not taken the supplement, then accepted handwritten declarations because there was no proof to demand. A taxi driver displayed a sign saying his car was MOKSHA FREE. Nobody knew what that meant. Passengers paid extra for it.

The government announced emergency controls on the product, priority corridors for medical transport, and investigations into its distribution. The messages were necessary. They were also late in reaching people whose phones had died, who could not leave relatives, or who no longer believed any sentence beginning with *there is no need to panic*.

Roadblocks intended to stop suspect stock became barriers to people. At one crossing, frightened residents tried to stop an ambulance because they believed its passenger would contaminate the neighborhood. At another, young men formed a human lane and stood in the rain until three ambulances passed.

Farzana saw both on the same journey.

She no longer trusted statements about what the city was like. It was capable of changing from one thing to another within twenty yards.

College Street lay under wet tarpaulins. The bookstalls had closed, their narrow interiors filled with the smell of paper absorbing water. A few stray pages moved in the gutter. Farther north, an enormous illuminated peacock turned its electric tail above an empty road, renewing its splendor every eleven seconds for nobody.

The air smelled of rain, incense, smoke, sewage, and disinfectant. These odors should not have belonged together. Now they formed a recognizable weather.

At a small Puja, a priest performed the Navami homa before a handful of people. Volunteers were using the courtyard behind him as a rest station. Someone had hung a cloth so an exhausted nurse could sleep without being watched. A man was charging phones from a battery and carefully writing owners' names on scraps of paper.

Farzana borrowed enough power to call her mother.

Her mother answered immediately.

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"Don't lie."

"I'm coming when I can."

"I didn't ask that."

They argued about food for thirty seconds. Farzana leaned against the wall and let herself be someone's badly behaved daughter.

Then another call arrived and she had to go.

The manifesto appeared at 9:14 a.m.

It reached newsrooms first, then messaging groups, then people who had no working connection except the person beside them reading it aloud.

Its title was written in capitals.

> **THE CITY THAT DENIED ME A TOILET**
>
> You are searching for a monster because a monster would leave the rest of you innocent.
>
> I was a child. I required no privilege. I asked for a door, water, and a few minutes beyond the attention of strangers. Your city had space for temples, clubs, marble staircases, offices, statues, and the dead. It had no space for a living boy in pain.
>
> You taught me that the human body was a private disgrace. You taught me that need must earn admission.
>
> Now you ask how this could have happened in a civilized city. I ask where your civilization was when a child begged to use a lavatory.
>
> The rich have walls. The devout have prayers. The beautiful have mirrors. All have mistaken these arrangements for protection. Beneath them is the same soft necessity. I have made it visible.
>
> A city that would not let a child relieve himself shall learn what it means to be afraid of its own bowels.
>
> You will call this indiscriminate. Your indifference was indiscriminate first.
>
> I have not invented your humiliation. I have removed your right to assign it only to other people.

The channels that had advertised MOKSHA SLIM began showing his face above the word MONSTER.

They had sold certainty in two colors now.

One presenter asked whether his diagnosis explained the attack. Another described him as a genius without pausing long enough to ask whether the word improved anything. A third interrupted a doctor's account of the victims to request more details about the childhood incident.

People posted laughing emojis beneath photographs of Sen as a boy. They made toilet jokes. They wrote that he should have taken a laxative.

The cruelty that had helped furnish his imagination was still available. He watched it arrive and mistook its continued existence for proof that he had been right to kill.

***

Ishani read the manifesto beside Mitali's bed.

Mitali was awake. Her father had brought the towel from Haripada's workshop and the wrong charging cable. She had scolded him for the cable, gently at first, then with sudden anger she could not explain. He had accepted both versions as evidence that she was still there.

"Is that him?" she asked.

Ishani lowered the phone.

"Yes."

"Does he say how to stop it?"

"No."

Mitali looked away.

After a moment she said, "Then why are you reading it?"

Ishani had no answer that would not sound like a defense of curiosity.

At the next bed, Ajit's wife was trying to persuade him to touch the bag against his abdomen. He refused. He could tolerate looking at it if he regarded it as equipment. Touching it would make the arrangement belong to him.

"You can take your time," the nurse said.

It was a small mercy, to be given time in a place where everybody needed it.

Ishani went to a room where the hospital recorded brief public updates. A volunteer propped a phone against a stack of files.

She had not intended to reply to Sen. She intended to describe what was known, correct the worst rumors, and ask people to let ambulances through. Then she looked at the camera and thought of Biren carrying his daughter under a blood-soaked shawl.

"Some of our patients have spent their lives being denied toilets," she said. "Some clean toilets for other people. Some are injured because a crowd moved, because treatment arrived late, because fear kept someone from accepting medicine. Their suffering does not prove the man who attacked them understands injustice. It proves that he has added to it."

She paused to steady her voice.

"We cannot yet promise that removing a visible object removes every risk. We are seeing things we do not understand. Please don't turn that uncertainty into a reason to abandon people who need help."

The volunteer stopped the recording.

"Was that all right?" he asked.

Ishani sat down.

"I don't know."

***

At eleven-thirty, Ajit began to hurt again.

It was not another catastrophic rupture. At first it was only a new pain, precise enough that he could point to it. His wife pressed the call bell and began apologizing before anyone arrived.

By noon, there was fresh bleeding.

The surgeons reviewed him. Ishani stood with them while images appeared on a monitor. Where the first object had been removed, another abnormality now appeared. A second lay nearby.

There were ordinary explanations they had to examine first. Something missed. Something remaining. A misleading image. None could be dismissed simply because a worse explanation was imaginable.

Then a call came from the laboratory holding the recovered material.

The specimen had changed.

No one wanted to put that sentence in a report. Several people checked the records. The container's identification had been verified. Photographs from the previous day showed a broken remnant. The present specimen appeared intact, and there was another beside it, carrying the same tiny irregularity that had distinguished the first.

The caller's voice remained formal until the last sentence.

"It was broken," she said. "I saw it broken."

At another hospital, a patient with no further exposure had developed new objects after an apparent removal. At a third, the scans were ambiguous. Those facts did not explain one another yet. Ishani wrote them in separate columns to keep the fear from joining them prematurely.

But the possibility was already clear enough to alter every conversation.

They might not be dealing with a pill that entered, injured, and left.

They might be dealing with something that remained capable of making the injury possible again.

"Is it alive?" Farzana asked.

"I don't know what that word would mean here."

"Can it spread from one person to another?"

"We have no evidence of that. Don't let anyone turn this into evidence of that."

Farzana nodded.

"But removing it doesn't finish it."

Ishani looked toward Ajit's room.

"We can't assume it does."

They had given him a painful, frightening future and called it survival. Now even that bargain might not hold.

***

Beneath the mansion, Sen watched a recovered specimen appear on a screen it should no longer have occupied.

The new images did not obey the divisions he had assigned them. Completed records reopened. Some identifiers appeared more than once. A system he had treated as a collection of individual instruments was beginning to behave as though individuality had been an administrative convenience.

He could observe the change. He could not command it away.

There were words in the old research files for uncertain persistence. He had read them years ago and judged the concern exaggerated. His former colleagues had been cautious people. They had mistaken caution for intelligence and then used committees to defend the confusion.

Now he read the passages again.

The words had not changed.

He called a former collaborator. The call did not connect. He wrote a message, deleted it, and wrote another that was less incriminating and therefore less useful.

Above him, rain tapped the bucket beneath the skylight.

There was a dull pressure low in his abdomen.

He had carried a supposedly inert observation specimen inside himself since an earlier phase of the work. He had liked what that said about his courage. He had described it in his journals as proof that he asked no one to accept an intimacy he would not accept himself.

That interpretation required forgetting what he had withheld from everybody else.

He opened his own record.

There were two entries.

For several seconds his hand lay perfectly still on the desk.

Then he looked toward the laboratory's little bathroom, a clean white door beneath the stairs.

He had ordered it fitted before anything else.

***

Tara's mother wanted to know why they had stayed at the hospital.

The question came while they were waiting for the papers that would allow them to take Riju's body. There were many bodies. A man with a register apologized every time he turned a page.

"We could have gone home," her mother said.

Tara stared at the wall.

"We were here because of you."

Her mother said it without raising her voice. Then her face changed, and she put her hand over her mouth as though the sentence could still be caught there.

Tara nodded.

"I know."

"No. Tara, no."

They sat beside one another with the sentence between them.

There would be no useful apology for it today. Her mother held out a hand. Tara could not make herself take it. After a while she moved her chair closer, and they stayed that way.

On the wall, a poster instructed patients not to spit. Someone had drawn a mustache on the smiling face beneath it. Riju would have noticed. He would have shown her. It seemed unbearable that the mustache could continue to exist when he could not.

At last the man with the register called their names.

The rituals after a death were supposed to tell people what came next. Now every step required permission from an office that had too few people, a vehicle that might not come, a relative who could not cross the city. An uncle had reached a cremation service. He called to say he was on his way, then called again to say the road was blocked.

Tara's mother began folding the end of her sari into a smaller and smaller square.

"He hasn't eaten," she said.

Tara closed her eyes.

They remained there until the uncle arrived.

***

Farzana found the house through an old property file and a living person who was ashamed he had not called sooner.

The landlord's representative had seen Sen's photograph on television. He recognized the tenant who paid for the lower portion of a decaying mansion near Kumartuli. The rent was handled through a company. The tenant had described his work as medical instrumentation. The representative had been pleased that anyone wished to spend money repairing the basement.

"You never went down?" Farzana asked.

"There were private experiments."

"What did that mean to you?"

"That I shouldn't interfere."

For much of her career, Farzana had investigated what people did. Lately she found that the larger file concerned what they had agreed not to ask.

There were still tenants in the upper part of the house: an elderly woman and her nephew, who had developmental disabilities and became distressed by unfamiliar noises. A caretaker had left before Puja to visit family. The route through the main courtyard was partially blocked by a fallen section of balcony.

Biren knew the building. His crew had worked there after a drain overflow the previous monsoon. He had not seen the laboratory. He knew which stair led to the tenants, which service passage had a door to the lane, and which section of courtyard would not support a heavy load.

He offered to guide the responders.

Farzana said no at first.

"My daughter is waiting for me," he said.

"That's why I don't want you there."

"I know which stair still holds. I can show your people. Then I'll come back."

He spoke as though coming back were part of the arrangement he could guarantee.

She arranged for him to show the rescue team the access route once a perimeter could be established. She did not promise to take him farther. There were too few available officers, a crowd moving toward a pharmacy three streets away, and reports of more injuries along the route. Planning a single journey now involved deciding which emergency would be left waiting.

Then the hospital switchboard received a call from Arindam Sen.

He asked for Ishani by name.

They brought Farzana into the room before she answered.

"Dr. Mukherjee," he said. "You have been making confident statements about matters you don't understand."

His voice was calmer than she expected. She disliked herself for expecting a sound that would identify him as different from other people.

"Which statement?"

"Your description of my purpose."

"I'm concerned with your patients."

"They are not my patients."

"You put something inside them and watched what happened. What word do you prefer?"

He did not answer.

Farzana made a note without looking up.

"We are finding new objects after removals," Ishani said. "We have a recovered specimen that no longer looks the way it did. Tell me what we're seeing."

She heard a small change in his breathing.

"How many cases?"

"You first."

"You can't expect to interpret this from a handful of observations."

"Then help us."

The word offended him. She could hear that too.

He began to explain that his work had been intended for singular events, that the recurrence was not consistent with his expectations. He stopped before saying more. There was nothing usable in the explanation, only an admission he had not meant to make.

"Can you stop it?" she asked.

Silence.

"Arindam. Can you stop it?"

"There are records here. Original observations. They won't mean anything to your police."

Farzana's pen stopped.

"You know we've identified the house," Ishani said.

"I know you need me."

"We need information."

"You won't get it from a corpse."

Only then did she understand that he had not called to defend the manifesto.

"Are you unwell?"

He said nothing.

"Have you exposed yourself to the same material?"

The line went quiet long enough that she thought the connection had failed.

"A different version," he said at last.

Farzana looked up.

Ishani closed her eyes. Not with relief. There was no satisfaction in hearing a human being become afraid of the machinery of his own body, even this one. She discovered that she wanted satisfaction anyway.

"We'll arrange medical assessment and custody," she said.

"You must come personally."

"You don't choose the conditions."

"You are the only person there who may understand what not to destroy."

Farzana took the phone and began speaking about surrender. She used plain words, repeated them when he tried to change the subject, and refused to make promises outside her authority.

Before he ended the call, Sen said, "The screens are still working."

Farzana asked what he meant.

He hung up.

***

That afternoon, Tara returned from the arrangements for her brother with her mother and uncle.

There had been no crowd of relatives, no familiar sequence of hands taking over each task. A priest had spoken softly and left room for their confusion. The uncle did the things he knew how to do. Her mother had walked as though the ground required consideration at every step.

Back at the hospital, Tara asked for Ishani.

"Have you found him?"

Ishani hesitated, then said yes.

"I want to come."

"No."

"I want to ask him something."

"It's not safe."

Tara looked at the ward behind her. "I know what safe means now. It means you haven't found out yet."

Ishani did not argue with the sentence. She argued with the request.

They settled on a recording. Farzana could take it as a witness statement; Ishani could show it if there was an opportunity. Tara insisted that the photograph accompanying it show Riju alive, in the red shirt he had worn on Shashthi, smiling crookedly because he disliked being asked to smile.

She recorded the statement three times.

On the first, she called Sen names until she could no longer speak.

On the second, she tried to tell him about her brother and forgot what she had intended to say.

On the third, she looked straight into the camera.

"His name was Riju Chatterjee. He was twelve. He didn't take your pill. He was carrying water to people who were waiting for help."

She held the photograph below her face.

"You don't get to call him Calcutta."

Then she stopped the recording herself.

***

The international notices went out that evening.

They described confirmed links, suspected links, labels under investigation, and the limits of what was known. Some countries acknowledged them quickly. Some requested more information. Some distributors promised to investigate, using the same polite phrases that had appeared in their earlier promises about delivery.

The accounting records kept arriving.

There were shipments that might be duplicates. There were orders that had been canceled on paper but marked fulfilled elsewhere. There were product names nobody at the hospital recognized. MOKSHA SLIM had been one face of a business that sold other faces to other markets.

The first complete reconciliation was not complete at all. It was simply the first one with enough entries to make its omissions frightening.

More than six million tablets were accounted for in dispatch records associated with the suspect supply chain.

That was not six million confirmed weapons. It was not six million people. It included stock still in warehouses, returns, shipments whose contents had not been verified, products that might prove unrelated. Ishani made Farzana repeat those distinctions when they spoke to the emergency coordinator.

The distinctions mattered.

They did not make the figure smaller.

Several consignments had left India before Mahalaya.

On one page, beside an overseas shipment, someone had stamped a green circle containing the word CLEARED.

Ishani studied it for a long time.

In the ward, a patient began calling for someone to accompany him to the bathroom. The nurse answered that she was coming.

He called again before she could reach him.

## V. Vijayadashami: The Last Immersion

On Dashami morning, Ishani could not swallow her own medicine.

She stood in the staff room with a tablet in her palm and a paper cup of water in the other hand. The packet was familiar. She had used the same prescribed medicine for years. It had been in her bag before the first man entered the mall toilet.

None of these facts moved her hand.

Through the wall, somebody flushed.

She spilled water on her shoe.

For a moment she stood absolutely still, listening for what might follow. Then a colleague came out of the bathroom drying her hands on a paper towel. Their eyes met. Neither said anything.

Ishani put the tablet in her mouth and swallowed.

She had spent her professional life telling people that every intervention involved risk. Until this week, she had not understood how much civilization depended on allowing that sentence to remain mostly theoretical.

***

The streets toward Kumartuli were wet and strangely spacious.

Ash drifted through a thin rain. A warehouse fire had burned through the night beyond the route they took, and the air carried its charred sweetness. The decorated arches remained. Lights moved across them in programmed waves, advancing from one end to the other and beginning again.

Farzana sat beside the driver of the medical van, speaking into a radio that kept losing words. Biren rode in the back with Ishani and an orderly. He held an empty stretcher strap between his hands.

The police had established contact with the house and arranged the evacuation of the upstairs tenants. Sen had agreed to surrender for medical assessment. He had also changed his mind twice, insisting that the laboratory records be protected and that Ishani be present before he left. A medical team had been requested because his voice now sounded weak and because arrest did not abolish the need for care.

None of them believed the journey was safe.

Biren had kissed Mitali on the forehead before leaving. She had asked where he was going. He told her that he was helping at a house nearby.

"You always say nearby."

He had not answered.

On the way out, she caught his hand.

"Don't be good to everyone," she said.

He looked at her.

"Just come back."

Now he watched the road. Outside a closed shop, a dog slept on a discarded promotional banner. In one stretch of lane, every balcony contained a person watching them pass. In the next, nobody appeared at all.

At the house, an elderly woman waited under a tarpaulin with her nephew. The rescue workers had brought them out. He kept asking whether the puja lights would be turned on that evening. She answered yes each time, then looked at Biren with tired, apologetic eyes.

He helped them into a vehicle.

The mansion's front door was taller than the entire room he rented. Its lower panels were swollen with damp. Above the courtyard, fragments of colored glass remained in the fanlights. A plaster woman gazed down with her broken arms lifted toward something that was no longer there.

Ishani noticed a line of ants carrying away crumbs from a plate.

She found their industry offensive, then was ashamed of the thought.

Farzana received confirmation from the officers at the lower entrance. Sen was inside. He was not resisting. The immediate approach had been checked as far as the circumstances allowed. The rest of the building would remain restricted while specialists dealt with it.

She looked at Ishani.

"You can still let the other doctor assess him."

"He asked for me."

"That isn't a reason."

"No," Ishani said. "It isn't."

She went down anyway.

***

The laboratory did not look like the future.

It looked like a place where one man had spent too much time. There were old desks, newer screens, stacks of paper, a kettle with mineral deposits around its spout. Damp had lifted paint from the wall behind a cabinet. A dehumidifier hummed with a domestic, almost comforting sound.

The objects on the benches were less comforting. Ishani could not tell what most of them were, and she did not try. The room's danger did not become smaller because it declined to announce its purpose.

Sen was seated beside a desk.

He had shaved. A small square of tissue remained under his chin where he had cut himself. He wore a pale shirt tucked into dark trousers. One hand rested low on his abdomen.

"Dr. Mukherjee," he said. "Thank you for coming."

She had expected to feel something decisive on seeing him. Hatred, perhaps. Instead she registered the details that came automatically: his color, his breathing, the protective angle of his body, the sweat at his hairline.

She resented her training for making him a patient before she had finished seeing him as the cause.

Farzana identified herself and told him that he was in custody. She explained what would happen next. He listened with the expression of a conference speaker enduring an introduction that had run too long.

"There are things here that should not be mishandled," he said.

"Then tell us what we need to preserve."

"You won't understand."

"Then explain it to someone who will."

He turned to Ishani, attempting to make the two of them a separate class of person within the room.

"You've seen the recurrence."

"We've seen evidence of it."

"It should not happen."

"It is happening."

He pressed his lips together.

Behind him, one screen displayed rows of identifiers. Another showed a close, slowly moving image of living tissue. A third held several smaller pictures. Some were black. Some trembled. One appeared to show a small amount of blood moving across a wet surface.

Ishani moved in front of it before Biren could see.

Biren had come down with the orderly to help carry the stretcher. He saw enough.

"Is that someone?" he asked.

Sen glanced at the screen.

"Observational data."

"Is that someone?"

"Yes."

The word changed the room.

Biren looked at the other screens, trying not to look, trying to identify something that should never have been available to him. Somewhere there was a bed, a floor, a home, a person whose most private existence had become a moving picture in this damp cellar.

"Turn it away," he said.

Sen reached toward a control. Farzana stopped him until an officer could record what was on the display and secure the evidence. Ishani took a clean cloth from the medical bag and draped it over the live images.

The cloth rose and fell slightly in the air from a fan.

It looked like a small shroud.

***

Sen's records occupied a locked cabinet and several secured systems. Some were clinical observations. Some concerned the earlier research. Some might eventually help people understand what had been done to them. Others might allow the work to survive its author in a more dangerous form.

Nobody in the room could immediately separate those possibilities.

He had prepared a short index by hand.

"These are the relevant observations," he said, sliding it across the desk. "The assumptions about finality were incorrect."

Ishani read the first page.

The word *finality* appeared beside the deaths of human beings.

"What does it do after the first injury?" she asked.

"I don't know yet."

"Do you know how to stop it?"

"If I had time and proper facilities—"

"Do you know?"

He looked at her angrily.

"No."

There it was. No code, no hidden command, no last-minute concession by a man whose power depended on withholding rescue. He had built the possibility of catastrophe and mistaken the fact that he could begin it for the ability to finish it.

Ishani kept reading.

Some of the tablets had been consumed weeks earlier. The material had persisted. The later observations suggested further persistence beyond damage or removal, and more than one presence where there should have been one. Sen's language grew less assured in the newest notes. There were questions in the margins. A few were underlined hard enough to tear the paper.

"You released this without knowing," she said.

"I knew enough for its intended purpose."

"You mean killing people."

"You are reducing an argument to its most sensational element."

Biren made a small sound behind her.

Ishani put the page down.

"There is a nineteen-year-old woman in my ward. Her name is Mitali Mondal. She was serving food. Her father carried her through a crowd while she tried to apologize for bleeding on him."

"Anecdotes do not—"

"He is standing behind me."

Sen looked past her.

For the first time, he seemed to understand that Biren had not come only to carry something.

"Your daughter," he said.

Biren nodded.

Sen began a sentence, stopped, and rearranged it.

"I regret that individual suffering is—"

"Don't," Biren said.

His voice was quiet enough to make everyone listen.

"Don't practice on me."

Sen leaned back. The movement hurt; Ishani saw him conceal it.

"You clean this city's waste," he said. "You know better than most how it treats the people it depends on."

"Yes."

"And you defend it?"

"My daughter is in it."

"That is not an answer to what I said."

"It's the answer I have."

Biren looked toward the white bathroom door under the stairs.

"You put one down here."

Sen said nothing.

"A toilet. For yourself."

"Everyone needs one."

"I know."

The simplicity of the reply seemed to irritate Sen more than an accusation would have.

Biren went on. "You know what I did when the people started getting frightened? I opened the doors. Not because I'm special. Because they still needed to go. I have locked doors on people before. I have wanted to finish my shift. I have been tired and rude. That doesn't mean my girl owes you her body."

Sen looked away.

"There were people who laughed at you," Ishani said. "People who refused to help. That happened. They were wrong. You had every right to be angry."

His eyes returned to her, briefly receptive.

"You keep extending that moment until it includes everyone you want permission to hurt."

The receptiveness vanished.

"You have no idea what it was like."

"You have no idea what it was like for Mitali. You didn't ask."

Outside the laboratory, somebody moved the stretcher against the wall. Its wheels made a soft, awkward squeak.

Sen watched the door as though the sound had come from very far away.

Ishani took out her phone.

"Someone asked you to listen."

She played Tara's recording.

The young woman's voice sounded thin in the low room. Riju's photograph filled most of the screen, the red shirt, the unwilling smile, the watch he had been so pleased to own.

Sen watched without speaking.

When the recording finished, Ishani did not put the phone away.

"He wasn't exposed," Sen said.

"He died in the panic."

"Then the failure of crowd management—"

Farzana turned her face aside for a moment.

Ishani felt something in herself go very cold.

"Yes," she said. "That too. There were too few people controlling the entrance. We couldn't make a safe passage quickly enough. I was elsewhere in the hospital. We all have things to answer for. You don't get to use that to disappear from what you did."

He looked at the photograph again.

The boy's expression had not changed. It offered no argument for Sen to defeat.

"You have shown me individual cases," he said. "I am speaking of a structure."

"A structure is where these people live. You don't get an empty one."

For a while there was only the hum of the machine behind the cabinet.

Then Sen said, almost conversationally, "My mother had to wash my clothes while I stood there. She was crying too."

It was the first thing he had said that did not sound rehearsed.

Ishani let the silence remain.

"I thought she was ashamed of me."

"Was she?"

He stared at the desk.

His mother had said that he was her son. The words returned now without fitting into the argument he had spent decades constructing. Beside them came another fragment: red bangles, a woman's hand holding cloth around his waist, a voice saying that the men ought to be ashamed of themselves.

He pushed the memory away. It did not erase what had been done to him. He would not permit anyone to pretend it did.

But it complicated the version in which nobody had helped.

He had needed that version to be simple.

"I wanted them to understand," he said.

"They understand pain," Ishani replied. "What were you going to teach them after that?"

He opened his mouth.

No answer came.

His hand tightened against his abdomen.

For an instant he appeared offended by the interruption. Then he looked toward the bathroom.

"I need a moment."

Ishani moved closer.

"Stay seated. Let me examine you."

"I know my own condition."

He began to stand.

The sound was very small.

Biren knew it immediately. His hands moved before his thoughts did. Sen's knees buckled; Biren caught one shoulder, and the orderly caught the other. Together they lowered him before his head struck the floor.

Sen looked down with an expression that was not intellectual, not grandiose, not capable of sustaining a manifesto. Blood spread beneath him. He pressed a hand to the floor as though he could use its solidity to hold himself together.

"No," he said.

Ishani knelt.

"Arindam. Look at me."

"It was inactive."

"Look at me."

He did, and she saw that he was not looking at a colleague now. He was looking at the person who might know what to do.

He caught her wrist.

"Don't let them look."

Biren shut his eyes.

The same words. His daughter's words. They did not make Sen innocent. They made the work of remaining human much harder than Biren had expected it to be.

Ishani covered him while the team prepared to move him. Farzana called for the van. Sen's grip weakened, then tightened again when he saw the white bathroom door.

"Close it," he whispered.

The door was already closed.

***

The message about Ajit reached them halfway to the hospital.

He had suffered a second catastrophic injury. The surgical team was preparing him. The functioning theater available for this kind of emergency had one team, limited supplies, and no certainty about when reinforcement would arrive.

Sen needed the same resources.

The surgeon spoke to Ishani over the phone. He described both patients as plainly as he could. Neither could safely wait. Neither had a guaranteed chance. There was no spare team at a neighboring hospital ready to accept one of them; they had already asked.

"What can this man actually give us?" he asked.

Ishani looked at Sen on the stretcher.

He was half-conscious. Blood had soaked the covering beneath him. The officer beside him was holding the evidence index in a sealed bag, careful not to crease it.

"He doesn't have a cure," she said.

"Then is there a clinical reason to put him first?"

She closed her eyes.

There were observations that only he might be able to interpret. Records whose meaning could take others months to reconstruct, if they could reconstruct them at all. There were patients in other cities, unopened packets in other countries, things remaking themselves in places where no one knew to look.

There was also Ajit Dey, who had asked to be believed and whose wife kept his glasses clean.

She could not turn either set of facts into a number.

The surgeon waited.

"He may be the only person who can explain enough of this to help us," she said. "Take Sen first."

The words were quiet. She had imagined that a decision of that size would require a different voice.

"Are you sure?"

"No."

Another pause.

"All right," the surgeon said.

Farzana had heard. She looked at Ishani but did not rescue her with agreement.

"Put my recommendation in the record," Ishani said.

"I will."

Biren sat with both hands flat on his knees.

The van moved through an intersection where the festival lights still showed a lotus opening. Its petals opened, shone, and closed. Ishani watched it until the turn took it out of view.

***

Ajit died before Sen's operation was finished.

That did not prove he would have lived if the order had been reversed. His injuries were severe. The hospital's failures had begun long before this morning. There were several true statements Ishani could arrange around his death.

She found she did not want to stand behind any of them.

His wife was sitting beside the empty bed when Ishani came.

"They said another emergency went first."

"Yes."

"Were you involved?"

"I recommended it."

The woman looked at her for a long time.

"You said you believed him."

"I did."

"Then why did he have to wait?"

Ishani explained. She did not use the word *necessary*. She did not say *for the greater good*. She said what she knew, what she had hoped, and what she had chosen.

Ajit's wife listened without interrupting.

Then she put the glasses in their case.

"You should have asked me," she said.

Ishani could not explain how little time there had been without sounding as though time belonged only to the people making decisions.

"Yes," she said.

The woman turned away.

Ishani left her with someone she knew, because even after forgiveness became impossible there were practical things left to do.

***

Sen survived the operation.

He was moved to a guarded room and remained in critical condition. His survival was not a solution, and nobody honest called it one.

The laboratory records were secured. Independent clinical teams would receive relevant observations. The original material required examination by people whose names now arrived with official credentials and instructions about restricted access. A national security representative asked Farzana to refer to Sen as a protected detainee in external communications.

"He is a man under arrest for mass murder," she said.

"Both descriptions can be correct."

She knew they could. That was what frightened her.

There were researchers who wanted to save patients. There were officials who wanted to know whether another country had created the technology. There would be people who wanted to know what else it could do. The room around Sen's bed was already acquiring purposes that had nothing to do with Ajit Dey.

Farzana made certain the arrest record, the witness statements, and the chain of custody could not quietly vanish into a different kind of file. She could preserve the existence of the crime. She could not dictate what every powerful person would learn from it.

When Sen briefly regained awareness, Ishani was standing outside the glass.

He tried to speak. A nurse bent closer.

Later she told Ishani what he had asked.

"Whether the records were safe."

Nothing about the people in them.

Ishani looked down at her hands.

She had kept open the possibility of help. She had also kept him open to the world.

It would be a long time before anyone knew whether those were the same decision.

Biren returned to Mitali with another wrong cable.

She looked at it, then at him.

For a moment he thought she would laugh. Instead she began to cry, and he sat beside her until she could speak.

"I want to go home."

"I know."

"I don't want this."

Her hand hovered over the bag against her abdomen.

"I know."

"Stop saying that."

He stopped.

The nurse came to help with her care. Biren rose to leave, but Mitali held his wrist. She wanted him there and did not want him to see. They negotiated a position for the chair, a fold in the sheet, a way he could remain without occupying the whole of her humiliation.

It took time.

When the nurse had finished, Mitali asked whether he had seen the man.

"Yes."

"Did he look frightened?"

"At the end."

She considered that.

"Good."

Biren did not correct her.

He had helped carry Sen. He had held him when there was blood on the floor. He could not turn the act into a lesson for his daughter. His hands had known what to do before his anger had organized itself, and now the man who hurt her was alive.

That was what had happened.

Outside the ward, the hospital's cleaners were changing shifts. A woman stopped Biren to ask whether he knew where spare keys were kept for the toilet at the end of the corridor. Somebody had locked it after a patient panicked.

He knew.

He went with her and opened it.

Sharmila Bose found the empty blister on Dashami afternoon.

Malati had not emptied the brass basket. She had come to help, seen the state of the household, and spent her time on tasks that were more urgent than making a room look as though nobody had died in it.

The packet lay beneath a cotton pad.

Sharmila lifted it with a tissue and put it on the dressing table. The mirror showed her hand, the empty hollows, the place where Prabir had stood turning sideways.

His granddaughter had asked whether Dadu was still at the hospital. Her mother had explained. The child had listened and then asked again the next morning, testing whether an answer so unreasonable could remain the same.

Sharmila sat down.

She had been angry with him for buying the pills. Angry in the car on the way back from the hospital, angry in the dark while relatives slept on mattresses in the living room, angry when someone found the order confirmation on his phone. The anger had offered her a sequence in which a different decision could restore him.

Now she remembered his face when she told him he looked nice.

She took out her phone and called the mall attendant. Farzana had helped her get his number after asking his permission.

Sanjoy answered in a small, careful voice.

"I wanted to thank you," she said.

"I couldn't do anything."

"You stayed with him."

The line was quiet.

"He said sorry," Sanjoy told her. "I keep thinking about that."

Sharmila closed her eyes.

"He shouldn't have had to."

Neither could think of what to say next. They stayed on the call a little longer, giving one another the company of someone who had been near him.

Tara used the bathroom that afternoon.

It was not a victory. Nothing about it could bear that word.

Her mother waited outside. Tara had asked her to, then asked her not to speak unless she was spoken to. The latch remained open. She sat with one hand on the edge of the basin and listened to every ordinary movement inside herself.

There was still no reliable way to tell her that she was free of the device.

The absence of pain was only the absence of pain.

When it was over, she remained seated for a while, afraid that standing might be the act that changed one condition into another. Her mother shifted outside the door.

"Ma?"

"I'm here."

Tara flushed.

The sudden rush made them both cry out.

She began to laugh, once, sharply, and then could not stop crying. Her mother came in and held her without speaking. Tara's clothes were not properly fastened. For once neither of them tried to improve the scene.

Later they sat beside the exposure register. Tara had returned to it because there were still people whose names needed writing down.

She came to the page with Riju's hurried heading and could not turn it.

Her mother placed a hand over the empty part of the paper.

"We'll leave that one," she said.

They began a new page.

At the river, a small group prepared the last immersion permitted at their ghat that evening.

Many idols remained in their pandals. Some committees had no transport. Some had no people willing or able to come. A few had decided to wait because the ordinary meaning of a farewell required more strength than they possessed.

This idol had come from the neighborhood behind Shyambazar, from the pandal made to look like a library.

The painted books had been removed to make space for a temporary relief station. The goddess stood on a low vehicle beside the water, her face illuminated by a portable lamp. Rain had darkened the cloth around the base. A garland hung unevenly across one shoulder.

The priest completed the rites without amplification.

Ishani had come in the medical van to check on the volunteers at the ghat. Farzana was there because her remaining assignment concerned the immersion route. Biren had come for a few minutes at Mitali's request. He held his phone so she could watch from the ward if the connection stayed.

For a while it did.

On the screen, her face was pale against a pillow. She watched without speaking.

The river carried reflections from lights whose streets she could no longer imagine walking. Across the water, a few lit windows seemed close enough to belong to a kinder city. Then a boat crossed them and broke the illusion into separate pieces.

The men at the ghat lifted the frame.

Someone began the familiar call about next year.

*Aschhe bochhor abar hobe.*

The words came out uncertainly. Nobody could make them a prediction. A few people repeated them anyway, as something they were asking of the world.

Ishani watched the hands on the frame. A bandage on one thumb. A red thread around a wrist. Dirt beneath fingernails. The ordinary human equipment with which everything had always been carried.

Farzana's phone vibrated.

She read the message, then read it again.

"What?" Ishani asked.

"Singapore. Two reported cases. One product link confirmed."

Another message followed.

A hospital in Birmingham was seeking information about an unexplained internal injury. A clinician in the Gulf had reported a similar case and attached photographs of packaging. It was too early to know whether every report belonged to the same event.

It was no longer possible to believe every report came from somewhere else.

Farzana handed Ishani the phone.

The goddess tilted toward the Hooghly.

For a moment the painted face remained above the water, its eyes unchanged by the angle. Then the lower part of the frame settled, the garland lifted, and the face disappeared.

The light on the river broke apart.

Biren's connection failed. Mitali's image froze with her mouth slightly open, about to say something. He kept looking at it until the screen informed him that the call had ended.

Across the city, messages about the foreign cases began arriving in wards, homes, police stations, and shelters. People read them beside mattresses on floors. They repeated city names they had associated with jobs, relatives, holidays, escape.

In the guarded room, Sen slept under sedation.

In the laboratory, the covered screens continued to change.

Nobody at the river could hear them. There was no sound to hear.

In London, Helen Ward got home before her husband.

The flat was warm. Someone had delivered a parcel while she was at work, and her neighbor had left it just inside the door. She put her bag on a chair, took off her shoes, and stood for a few moments enjoying the silence.

On the kitchen counter was a bowl of apples, a school letter she needed to read, and the little box of supplements she had ordered the previous month.

She had seen something about India on her phone. A dangerous slimming pill. Her sister had sent a warning, followed by a message saying that it seemed to concern a particular product.

Helen had checked the name.

Hers was different.

The seller had replied promptly to her inquiry. There was no reason for concern about this range, the message said. The company took safety extremely seriously. Its products were rigorously checked. She should consult a professional if she had individual concerns.

She had found the promptness reassuring.

Now she turned the box over once more. The print was crisp. The seal was intact. A small green leaf rested above a gold line. There was a smiling woman on the enclosed card whose age Helen guessed to be close to her own.

The supplement did not promise anything extravagant. It promised lightness, balance, help with the little difficulties she had not thought serious enough to bring to a doctor. She liked that. She had grown suspicious of extravagant promises.

She pushed a tablet through the foil.

It landed in her palm with no sound at all.

For a moment she examined it. Pale, smooth, smaller than expected. Her husband's key sounded in the front door. He called hello. She called back.

She swallowed with a mouthful of water.

Then she went down the short hallway to the bathroom, thinking about dinner, and closed the door.

The end begins where everything else ends: in the room you lock because you believe you are safe.
