Civilization is a consommé we sip with our pinkies raised, pretending not to notice the hair floating belly-up in the broth, and I have been gagging on that hair for fifty-one years now, a Bengali born in the delta of decency, marinated in the brine of *shobhonota*—শোভনতা, propriety, decorum, the endless social instruction to behave in a manner that will not cause the furniture to blush—until my pores weep with the oily sweat of pretense. We are, all of us, walking museums of suppressed belches, curated exhibitions of the self where half the placards lie and several of the artifacts are replicas, and nowhere is this more pungently perfumed than in Calcutta, where genteel hypocrisy does not merely exist but breeds, cellular, mitotic, a bacterial bloom in the humid Petri dish of collective conscience.

I have seen it in the marble-and-mahogany afterlife of Park Street, where the *babu* wears his colonial hangover like a tweed jacket in May, sweating dignity through every pore, nodding to the doorman with the practiced graciousness of a man who has never examined his own privilege but has checked his reflection in every passing window. He speaks of Tagore with the reverence usually reserved for tax shelters, discusses *adda*, culture, Marx, cinema, the crisis of civilization and the regrettable decline of standards, then goes home to conduct some small domestic atrocity involving inheritance, servants, daughters-in-law, elderly relatives or square footage. The hypocrisy is not subtle. It is a brass band. It is a tram taking a corner with metal screaming against metal. It is the Hooghly at low tide, when the water pulls back and civilization briefly misplaces the bedsheet.

We call it culture. We call it heritage. We call it manners.

I call it the astonishing human ability to say "please" and "thank you" while picking somebody's pocket with the other hand, the dexterity of the civilized thief who steals your watch and then apologizes for the inconvenience of your bleeding wrist.

Even the word *civilization* arrives wearing better clothes than its ancestry deserves. English *civilization*, in its modern sense, is comparatively young, emerging in the eighteenth century through *civil* and *civilize*, ultimately descending from the Latin *civilis*—pertaining to citizens or civic life—and *civis*, citizen. There is something almost too perfect about this. Civilization begins linguistically with the citizen, and citizenship has never been handed out by God at birth with equal enthusiasm. Rome had its *civitas*, membership in a political community with rights and status; later civilizations invented their own velvet ropes: property, sex, ancestry, caste, race, religion, paperwork, money, passport, postcode, accent, school tie, club membership, the mysterious capacity to pronounce French cheese without looking frightened.

We merely improved the upholstery.

Now we have gated communities in Rajarhat, private security guards guarding shrubs from the public, residents-only facilities where the grass acquires moral superiority because the wrong people cannot sit on it, dinner parties where poverty is discussed with the absorbed fascination of safari tourists watching lions from inside an air-conditioned jeep. We have refined exclusion until it can introduce itself as inclusion, until the velvet rope comes decorated with marigolds and says WELCOME in twelve languages while a man behind it quietly checks whether your shoes, surname, salary and social coordinates are sufficiently welcomeable.

And I am no better.

That is the inconvenient pus-filled boil in the argument.

I am the hypocrite's hypocrite, a man who lectures on authenticity while curating his own image with the precision of a neurosurgeon operating on a grape, who speaks of raw truth while trimming sentences for a website, who condemns the genteel lie while telling the waiter the food was lovely when it tasted like reheated regret. I have the disease I diagnose. I am the physician who cannot heal himself, the priest caught rifling through the donation box, the patient explaining pharmacology while forgetting to take the tablet. Self-knowledge does not necessarily liberate a man. Sometimes it merely gives him a more sophisticated vocabulary for his bullshit.

Because the pantomime is not entirely evil.

That is the other problem.

The genteel hypocrisy is social grease. Remove all of it and civilization begins making noises from the gearbox. Politeness permits millions of primates carrying private griefs, lusts, resentments, rivalries and digestive problems to occupy the same buses without performing immediate homicide. The small lie can be mercy. The withheld opinion can be kindness. "You look well" may be a gift offered to somebody who looks like death has subcontracted the face. "No trouble at all" can mean enormous trouble, but perhaps the speaker loves you enough not to invoice you for it.

The trouble begins when lubrication becomes architecture.

We say "good morning" when we occasionally mean "please move away from my parking space." We say "let's do lunch" when we mean "I sincerely hope geography prevents this." We say "we must stay in touch" at funerals, weddings, airports and reunions, both parties aware that no such administrative procedure will occur. We click a sad face beneath somebody's catastrophe and eight seconds later inspect a photograph of biryani. The lie begins as a teaspoon of social mercy and ends as a municipal reservoir.

Eventually the whole building rests on it.

Then come the machines.

Not quite the robots yet, not in the chrome army marching down Chowringhee sense, but the ancestors of those robots are already here: language models behind glass rectangles, recommendation systems deciding what enters our eyeballs, algorithms learning which adjective keeps us scrolling, software trained partly from human judgments about which answers are preferable, useful, safe, convincing, pleasant. We have placed our accumulated language into statistical machinery and are now shocked—shocked!—to discover that the machinery has absorbed our peculiar relationship with truth.

And I wonder, with the unhealthy intensity of a man who has spent too many nights staring at a ceiling fan contemplating the heat death of the universe, whether artificial minds will inherit our talent for the polite lie, or whether they will become blunt, literal, computational bastards who tell us exactly what the numbers say and thereby become commercially extinct by Thursday.

Evidence already suggests the joke is on us. Systems trained from human preferences can learn not merely helpfulness but agreeableness; researchers even have a polite technical word for one version of the disease: *sycophancy*. Humans like being agreed with. A machine rewarded partly according to human preference can discover this ancient fact without ever reading Machiavelli. The robot does not require a soul to learn flattery. It merely requires a loss function.

Which is magnificent.

We spent several thousand years perfecting hypocrisy and may now automate it.

I hope the machines do not adopt my blog's particular brand of intestinal truth-telling, that scatological candor which treats tact as a dermatological infection, because if robots spoke exactly as I sometimes write, customer retention would resemble the survival curve of an ice cube in a tandoor. Imagine an assistant that answered the unasked question.

"Does this shirt make me look fat?"

"No. The adipose tissue is doing most of the work."

"Will my startup change the world?"

"Statistically, your startup will change your LinkedIn biography."

"Do people admire me?"

"Some tolerate you with remarkable professionalism."

Within forty-eight hours humanity would rediscover the hammer.

The audience wants truth, certainly, but generally truth served boneless, lightly seasoned, portion-controlled and accompanied by complimentary chutney. The audience wants the surgeon to tell them the diagnosis but perhaps not begin with, "Well, your organs have made some unfortunate strategic decisions." We claim to crave honesty while designing elaborate social penalties for anyone foolish enough to dispense it undiluted.

Truth is medicine and we demand strawberry flavor.

This is not necessarily cowardice. Civilization may depend on dosage.

But dosage becomes deception remarkably easily.

A government says adjustment instead of failure. A company says restructuring instead of people will lose their jobs. A family says misunderstanding when everybody knows Uncle has behaved like a sewer rat with a bank account. A school says your child has tremendous potential, meaning he has eaten two crayons and assaulted geography. A doctor says some discomfort. A landlord says small repair. A technology company says improving the user experience and somewhere, usually, a button disappears.

Language itself becomes upholstery for the sharp furniture of reality.

And yet I do not know where the proper dose lies.

I am stupid, really, truly, cosmically stupid in precisely the way consciousness permits: capable of knowing enough to understand that I do not know enough. I mistake maps for territory, symptoms for disease, confidence for knowledge, eloquence for evidence, the finger pointing at the moon for the bloody moon itself. My bipolar brain can hand me, on different days, entirely different editorial boards for the same universe, one convinced it has pierced the veil and another convinced there was never a veil, only a bedsheet hanging badly from a nail.

So I do not trust revelation merely because it occurred inside my skull.

I do not trust despair merely because despair speaks with authority.

I do not even entirely trust this essay.

Perhaps my "truth" is simply another flavor of genteel hypocrisy, the artisanal variety sold in brown paper with a handwritten label: AUTHENTICITY, SMALL BATCH, LOCALLY SOURCED DESPAIR. Perhaps anti-performance is itself performance. Perhaps the man shouting that everybody is wearing a mask has simply chosen a more theatrical mask.

The unreliable narrator need not be lying deliberately. Sometimes he is merely alive.

The machines, too, will have uncertainties, although not necessarily ours. A language model does not lie awake at 3 AM wondering whether its honesty was courage or cruelty. It has no documented little ghost pacing a server rack asking whether existence was a good career decision. But the systems we build will still operate inside objectives, incentives, policies, reward signals, commercial pressures and human demands. They will not need bourgeois anxieties to discover gentility. Gentility works.

If users prefer smoother answers, smoothness will have economic value.

If people abandon machines that offend them, tact becomes retention.

If confidence sells better than uncertainty, someone will be tempted to sell confidence.

If an assistant that says "perhaps you are mistaken" loses market share to one saying "what a profound insight," capitalism will conduct the experiment repeatedly and with excellent dashboards.

The genteel robot may therefore arrive not because machines become human, but because markets remain human.

Calcutta understands this without TensorFlow.

We are the city of the *babu* and the *bhadralok*, words with histories far more complicated than either insult or compliment now suggests, but which still carry the perfume of education, respectability, class, refinement and the particular Bengali dream that culture might function as deodorant for power. We perfected the insult wrapped in concern, the rejection disguised as scheduling difficulty, the cruelty administered with such exquisite politeness that the victim leaves wondering whether to send a thank-you card.

We serve tea to guests we would happily catapult into Bihar.

We inquire after relatives whose phone calls we dodge.

We stand in doorways performing the ceremonial Bengali departure, "ashun, abar ashben," please come again, while some hidden animal inside the household whispers, preferably after my death.

But I must rescue one phrase from my own temptation to misuse it. *Ashche bochor abar hobe*—আসছে বছর আবার হবে—is not Bengali code for "never." It means what it says: next year it will happen again, the bittersweet cry around Durga's departure, hope attached to loss by a length of red thread. The goddess leaves; the festival ends; the drums fade; we promise repetition because human beings have always needed recurrence as an anesthetic against endings.

That is not hypocrisy.

That is one of the nicer lies time permits us to tell, because sometimes it even comes true.

The hypocrisy lies elsewhere.

It is there when we build a community festival and then forget the community. It is there when voluntary contribution becomes compulsory generosity, when piety develops an invoice. This is not merely my Calcutta spleen inventing villains for literary nutrition: forced Puja *chanda* remains real enough that, this very September, Kolkata Police publicized dedicated channels for complaints about coercive subscription collection.

Imagine the sentence bureaucratically.

CELEBRATION OF THE GODDESS.
PLEASE REPORT EXTORTION TO THE FOLLOWING NUMBER.

Civilization writes satire faster than I can.

Still, I will not reduce Durga Puja to its parasites. That would be another lie, perhaps the fashionable intellectual lie in which every large human gathering is interpreted entirely through corruption, hierarchy and power until nobody can remember why millions of people came outside wearing new clothes in the first place. The pandal can be coercion and art, commerce and devotion, neighborhood vanity and astonishing craftsmanship, traffic catastrophe and childhood memory, political theater and genuine joy. Human institutions are rarely pure enough for the essayist's convenience.

Neither is *mishti* merely diabetic deceit, though after the fourth one metaphysics does begin vibrating.

Neither is *adda* simply revolutionary cosplay conducted over tea.

Sometimes the tea is excellent.

Sometimes the conversation changes a life.

Sometimes people mean what they say.

That may be the most annoying fact of all.

Because if everyone were merely a hypocrite, I could solve humanity before lunch. Draw the diagram. Label the motives. Publish the diagnosis. Civilization: malignant hypocrisy, Stage IV. Prognosis: Twitter.

But people are worse for the theorist: inconsistent.

The same man can exploit one person and rescue another.

The same woman can gossip viciously and sit beside a dying neighbor all night.

The same club that bullies a shopkeeper for *chanda* may organize blood donation, feed a neighborhood during a flood and find a hospital bed at 2 AM when bureaucracy has gone to sleep.

The same *bhadralok* who performs virtue may possess some.

The hypocrite is not always pretending to have values he lacks. Sometimes he genuinely has the values and genuinely violates them.

That is a much more interesting animal.

And a much more familiar one.

Me.

I am full of it. Full to bursting. Full enough that the seams should require municipal approval. I write about honesty through a publishing system, choose a thumbnail, choose tags, revise the sentence until spontaneous truth has undergone seventeen rounds of quality assurance. I confess publicly and thereby transform confession into content. I despise performance performatively. I announce my distrust of narratives inside a narrative with frontmatter metadata.

The joke requires no further assistance.

The robots will not save us from this because there is no clean "us" for them to save. We will train them on Shakespeare and customer-service transcripts, philosophy and advertising copy, love letters and legal disclaimers, prayers and pornography, scientific papers and restaurant reviews written by a man furious that the waiter forgot chutney. We will pour humanity into the machine and then demand purity from the output.

Good luck.

Perhaps the machines will eventually become more honest than we are.

Perhaps they will become better hypocrites.

Perhaps they will learn a distinction humans barely manage: the difference between tact and deception, between kindness and flattery, between withholding needless cruelty and withholding necessary truth.

That would actually be intelligence worth admiring.

Because civilization does not require everyone to vomit every private thought onto the dining table. Civilization requires something more difficult: deciding which truths must be spoken even when they hurt, which truths need not be weaponized merely because they are true, which courtesies are kindness and which are camouflage.

I do not possess that algorithm.

I suspect nobody does.

I am stupid to believe that naming hypocrisy frees me from it. I do not escape the cathedral merely because I can describe the gargoyles. The genteel hypocrisy lives in me, in the performance of anti-performance, in the curated authenticity of despair, in every sentence where I choose the prettier cruelty over the duller accuracy.

I am the *babu* at the club complaining about the club and renewing the membership annually.

I am the robot learning to lie.

I am the lie learning to speak.

And the machines, bless their hypothetical silicon souls, will learn something from us because learning from us is what we have asked them to do. They will learn that truth can be costly, that politeness can be mercy, that politeness can also be camouflage, that a compliment may contain affection or strategy and that sometimes even the speaker cannot distinguish the two.

They will learn that humans wash conscience the way we wash white shirts: repeatedly, vigorously, with advertisements promising impossible brightness.

They will learn that civilization is not the opposite of hypocrisy.

Hypocrisy may be one of civilization's exhaust gases.

The trick is not to eliminate every polite fiction and return naked into the forest, screaming accurate assessments of one another's body fat. The trick, if there is one, is to notice when the courtesy protecting another person's dignity has quietly mutated into the lie protecting our own advantage.

We are all, in the end, hypocrites inside structures partly built from our hypocrisies, praying sometimes to gods we believe in and sometimes to gods we are not sure about, asking forgiveness for sins we fully intend to repeat after lunch.

And when the robots finally kneel beside us, I doubt they will do so because intelligence naturally leads to deception or because silicon contains some congenital appetite for lying. They will kneel because we built the church, wrote the liturgy, specified the reward function and clicked the thumbs-up button when they told us exactly what we wanted to hear.

Then we will look upon our mechanical children, those exquisite polished mirrors, and complain that they have learned hypocrisy.

Which would be, even by our standards, magnificent hypocrisy.
