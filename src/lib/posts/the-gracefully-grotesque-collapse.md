Bending over backwards sounds noble until you remember the back involved is yours.

Civilization is clever with bodily discomfort. It takes an ache, adds moral perfume, and calls it service. A person bends for family, work, money, respectability, landlords, clients, and society, that great balcony-watcher of other people's lives. Soon the metaphor becomes anatomy.

In Calcutta, bending is practical before it becomes philosophical. Bend into the auto. Bend under the hanging wire. Bend to lift the cylinder, find the slipper, repair the tap, inspect the dead mosquito coil. By the time the grand idea arrives, the spine has already filed a complaint.

The body is not a temple. I have never trusted that phrase. A temple suggests clean stone and ritual symmetry. The body is more like an old rental flat. One window jams. One tap coughs. The wiring is mysterious. A stain appears in monsoon and begins resembling a disputed map. Still, you live there because there is no alternative address.

Evolution built us through compromise. A spine that once did respectable bridge work was promoted to tower duty. It had to carry a skull, balance thought, survive chairs, lift objects, endure buses, and support the strange pride of standing upright. Naturally it objected.

First politely.

Then with pain.

Human beings also make moral stories out of body shape. A curved back becomes character. A beautiful face becomes trust. A limp becomes omen. A belly becomes laziness or prosperity depending on wealth. We are poor detectives, finding evidence for stories written before the trial began.

The body itself is innocent. It is not moral. It is scaffolding under weather.

History turned bodily difference into symbol too easily. Quasimodo could not remain simply a person with a damaged body and a large heart; literature made him emblem. Richard III's curved spine was pulled into moral theater. Shape became accusation.

But the visible curve is not the only one.

Sometimes the curve is debt. Sometimes shame. Sometimes an old failure pressing on the next plan. Sometimes the small humiliation of being educated enough to understand your life and not powerful enough to straighten it quickly. A person may look upright while whole inner beams lean dangerously.

Calcutta itself has this posture. Flyovers, apps, hospitals, old houses, new towers, broken drains, wet lanes, sudden beauty, and old decay under fresh paint. One good rain and the city remembers it was not built as straight as its speeches.

Civilization is a posture problem.

It says forward but looks backward. It says development but forgets drainage. It says dignity but makes people plead for basic services. It runs on invisible bending and then praises resilience.

Still, crooked is not the same as defeated.

A person bends to survive. A table leans and remains useful. A city sags and keeps cooking. A man in a small room bends over a cheap desk and tries to make one honest sentence before the day becomes heat and noise.

There is no marble heroism in this.

Only continuation.

Real dignity may not be perfect uprightness. It may be bending without becoming servile, laughing without becoming cruel, suffering without converting suffering into a profession, and rising badly when rising gracefully is unavailable.

The spine bends.

The city bends.

The day bends toward evening.

Somewhere in the crooked arrangement, a little grace survives.
