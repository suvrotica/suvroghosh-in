The phone screen glowed before the room had decided to become morning.

News, markets, AI, war talk, startup prophecy, machine confidence, men in clean shirts explaining how the future would automate whatever dignity had survived the present. The rectangle was small. The claims were enormous.

There is a kind of unemployment that is not only about income.

Income matters first. Let no polished speaker pretend otherwise. Rent does not accept philosophical nuance. Rice is not purchased with insight. But beneath ordinary unemployment sits another vacancy: the feeling that one's place in the human arrangement has been quietly abolished.

AI sharpens this feeling.

A model writes. A model summarizes. A model codes. A model answers politely at all hours and never needs tea, rent, rest, or encouragement. It does not sit in a Calcutta room wondering whether usefulness is still available in human sizes.

This is not an argument against machines. I have lived too long with software to pretend the old world was pure. Much of human work was always repetition with better shoes. Many offices were ceremonies arranged around delayed decisions. If machines expose that, perhaps the humiliation is partly deserved.

Still, exposure is not replacement for meaning.

The soul, if one may use that suspicious word without ringing a temple bell, wants work that confirms presence. It wants to touch a problem and leave a mark. It wants evidence that time was not merely consumed.

The phone keeps offering futures.

Outside, Calcutta offers the present: heat, voices, wires, tea stalls, someone arguing over price, someone repairing something with faith and tape.

I put the phone down.

The room remains inefficient, which is to say human.
