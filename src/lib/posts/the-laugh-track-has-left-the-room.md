The laugh track came out of the laptop like a tin box full of old buttons.

Somebody on screen fell over a sofa. The people inside the machine laughed. The music told me this was light. I sat in my Calcutta room with tea going cold and understood the engineering perfectly.

The joke was visible.

That made the absence worse.

Once laughter arrived through the body before the mind could issue objections. The lungs jumped. The face lost dignity. For three seconds the world stepped aside and let foolishness do useful work.

Now I often see the mechanism without ignition. The proud man is deflated. The liar is caught. The official sentence collapses. The timing is correct. The carpentry is sound. Yet the laugh does not come.

This is one of the indignities of middle age. Not sadness exactly. Something flatter. Like a switch that still clicks but does not turn on the light.

Comedy requires a small surplus of life. Enough air in the chest, enough looseness in the jaw, enough trust that the fall on screen is not merely practice for one's own. Without that surplus, jokes become diagrams.

And yet, humor has not vanished.

It no longer arrives where summoned. It slips out sideways. When I spend five minutes looking for spectacles already on my face. When the fan makes a tragic sound and I briefly imagine it resigning from service. When I write three severe sentences about dignity and then spill tea on the notebook.

Those laughs are smaller.

They are also less obedient.

The laptop goes dark and my reflection appears in it, badly lit and unadvertised. Somewhere inside the old machinery, one small bubble rises, considers becoming laughter, and changes its mind.
