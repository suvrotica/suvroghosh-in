Reality has never learned to package itself properly.

It arrives without a ribbon, without a slogan, without a comforting explanation of why your troubles have selected you personally. It does not say your exam went badly because a planet was sulking. It does not say your office life is unfair because destiny has a bookkeeping problem. It says, more rudely, that systems fail, people flatter power, luck matters, incentives matter, and evidence is not always pleasant.

Naturally, the market prefers softer goods.

I learned this first in school. Praise in a classroom is not a flower; it is a lit match. If one boy is praised while another is scolded, the room records the event politically. Merit, before it becomes a slogan, becomes a social irritant. The child who answers correctly is not merely correct. He has accidentally joined the side of authority.

Later, in offices, the same drama returned with better chairs. Meritocracy was spoken of with respect, like an old photograph garlanded once a year. The actual ladder rewarded other skills too: timing, flattery, silence, social ease, and the art of turning someone else's weak thought into a strategic vision.

I lacked some of these arts.

This is not praise. A man should be careful before converting social awkwardness into moral superiority. I have made errors, trusted poorly, mistimed decisions, and confused intelligence with judgment. Still, one defect stayed: I could not sell comforting nonsense smoothly enough to profit from it.

There is money in fog.

Fog does not require method. It requires confidence, repetition, and a face solemn enough to make nonsense look ancient. Science is harder to sell because it is not cruel but it is unsentimental. It asks for evidence. It accepts uncertainty. It says that some problems need policy, medicine, mathematics, repair, education, and honest work rather than cosmic explanation.

That does not always comfort people. It may even insult the room.

But comfort built on falsehood has a later bill. The bill arrives as bad public reasoning, bad treatment, bad politics, bad education, and families where fear dresses up as tradition. By the time the bill is due, the seller of fog has moved to a larger flat.

So I remain here, in the unfashionable business of describing the chair with the loose leg. Not because reality pays well. It does not. Reality has the market value of bitter medicine in a sweet shop.

Still, if the chair is broken, someone should stop praising the cushion.
