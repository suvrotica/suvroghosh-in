The mirror is most dishonest before tea.

It presents the face with legal seriousness. Puffy eyes, bad hair, a mouth that appears to have resigned from optimism, skin carrying the minutes of sleep like poor accounting. The morning verdict arrives quickly: look at you. This is the evidence. Case closed.

The morning is a bad judge.

It has not heard testimony from lunch, evening, sunlight, work completed, water drunk, one decent sentence written, one practical errand finished, or the simple fact that bodies look unreasonable after sleep. It speaks first and therefore thinks it speaks law.

Older mornings are especially theatrical.

The body sends reports from departments one did not know existed. The back has an opinion. The stomach has policy concerns. The knees produce minutes from an overnight meeting. The mind, receiving these bulletins, adds biography. A minor ache becomes decline. A tired face becomes failure. A slow start becomes proof that the whole enterprise is finished.

This is bad reasoning with excellent timing.

It catches you before defenses are assembled. Before the kettle. Before movement. Before the street outside has fully entered. A person can lose an entire day by believing the first ten minutes too completely.

I try not to.

Not always successfully. Some mornings still arrive with the authority of a tax notice. But I have learned to treat the first hour as procedurally unreliable. Evidence taken under low light, dry mouth, and insufficient tea should not be admitted without review.

The practical rebellion is small.

Do not inspect the face as if it contains a national report. Wash it. Do not ask the body for a five-year forecast. Stretch badly. Do not decide the value of your life while searching for a clean cup. Boil water. Let the day gather witnesses.

There is humor in this if one can reach it.

The mind is capable of turning a misplaced towel into a philosophy of ruin. It can see an unwashed plate and infer moral collapse. It can wake, locate one old regret, and place it at the center of the room like a committee chair. This is not wisdom. It is bureaucracy at dawn.

Calcutta understands bad bureaucracy.

The city has survived enough stamped paper, delayed counters, and officials who confuse delay with depth. The inner morning office is similar. It produces verdicts because verdicts are easier than work. The work is slower: tea, bathing, food, one task, one call, one line of movement.

The morning verdict is not law.

It is an opening argument from a tired clerk.

Sometimes the day remains difficult. That must be admitted. Not every morning improves. Not every hour becomes generous. But even then, refusing the first verdict creates a small space in which other evidence may arrive.

The kettle clicks.

The face remains imperfect.

The case, for now, is adjourned.
