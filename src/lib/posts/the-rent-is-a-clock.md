The calendar looked harmless until I counted the days.

That is how rent works. It turns paper into machinery. A date becomes a gear. A week becomes a tightening spring. The room remains still, but time begins moving through it with official purpose.

Rent is not only money paid for space.

It is a clock installed in the nervous system.

Every cup of tea, every delayed invoice, every message left unanswered, every small purchase, every repair postponed, all begin to tick inside the same mechanism. The room that shelters you also measures you. It says: another month, please.

People with ownership speak differently about homes. They speak of walls, light, furniture, plans. A renter hears duration. How long can this arrangement hold? What if the payment slips? What if the landlord changes tone? What if the next room costs more and offers less?

In Calcutta, rooms have moods. Damp corners. Old paint. Wires that look philosophical. A window that opens toward somebody else's argument. The place becomes intimate without becoming secure. That is a cruel combination.

The clock keeps ticking.

Not metaphorically after a point. One lives by dates. Payment due, work expected, bill cycle, recharge, cylinder, repair, deadline. Dreams are measured in months because months are what the room accepts.

I wish suspicion were not useful.

But pretty lies are expensive, and rent has a way of charging interest on them.

The calendar remains on the wall.

I turn the page when the month ends, not because time has improved, but because the clock requires a clean face.
