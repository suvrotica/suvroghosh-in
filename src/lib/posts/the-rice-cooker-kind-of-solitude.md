The rice cooker clicked, and the room accepted the decision.

There is a particular solitude that belongs to such a sound. Not mountain solitude. Not noble silence. The rice cooker kind: plastic lid, red light, steam, one plate, one spoon, no negotiation over who wants what.

It is practical. That is its sadness.

A rice cooker does not care whether the person using it is lonely, proud, hungry, tired, educated, underpaid, or full of old theories. It cooks the rice. The machine has a purpose. This gives it an advantage over many men.

I sit with the plate on my lap and mix dal into the rice. Outside, Calcutta continues its public noise: vendors, engines, pressure cookers, arguments, the city chewing its own evening. Inside, the sound is smaller. Spoon against plate. Fan overhead. Phone lighting up without anything urgent enough to become company.

Eating alone changes food.

It becomes less performance and more maintenance. There is no one to praise the salt, complain about the texture, ask for more, or make a small remark that proves the meal occurred in shared time. The rice enters the body quietly, like a bill paid without receipt.

Still, there is comfort in the procedure.

Wash. Measure. Switch on. Wait. Eat.

When larger parts of life refuse sequencing, a small working sequence becomes valuable.

The cooker sits in the corner afterward with the peaceful arrogance of completed duty.

I rinse the pot, badly but sincerely, and leave it upside down to dry.

The room remains large in the wrong way.
