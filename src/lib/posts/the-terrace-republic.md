Tea is a dangerous liquid in Bengal because it pretends to be harmless.

It sits quietly in the cup, brown and obedient, while opening trapdoors under the floor of memory. One minute I am in my present room in Calcutta, checking whether the milk has turned unreliable. The next minute I am back in South Sinthee, in a brick house without plaster, where the walls were too poor to lie.

That house still owns part of me.

It had heat, dust, school pressure, family expectation, and a terrace that felt larger than its actual dimensions. Childhood often survives through such disproportions. A lane becomes a kingdom. A roof becomes a republic. A boy with no authority over anything discovers one rectangle of sky and mistakes it, usefully, for freedom.

The terrace was not beautiful in any approved sense. There was no design, no curated nostalgia, no tasteful restoration. It had rough edges, practical stains, stored objects, and the honest ugliness of a house spending all its money on survival. But it allowed a different relation to the world. Below were rooms, rules, voices, duties. Above was air.

I was not a simple child, which is not the same as saying I was exceptional. I was curious in the inconvenient way. I wanted to know what lay behind the statement, under the machine, inside the rule, beyond the polite answer. Some children collect stamps. I collected objections.

This habit is not always profitable.

A society runs on a certain amount of accepted nonsense. Families do too. Schools certainly do. The child who keeps looking behind the curtain may learn something, but he also learns how much of adult life depends on curtains remaining in place.

Tea brings all this back because taste is an archivist. A particular bitterness, a film on the surface, the heat of the cup against the finger, and the old terrace returns. Not as a golden age. Let us not insult the past by polishing it into a lie. It was difficult, cramped, ambitious, anxious, and often unfair. But it was formative in the way rough ground forms a foot.

The private republic did not save me. It gave me a place from which to look.

That may be all childhood can honestly give.

The tea cools. The present room comes back. The old terrace remains somewhere above it, holding a piece of sky.
