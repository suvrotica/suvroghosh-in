It is never the teeth. Never the claws, never the shambling horror of some Lovecraftian beastie nipping at my heels through the corridors of sleep. No, my unconscious is far more sophisticated than that, far more cruel. It does not traffic in monsters; it traffics in memory. It reaches back into the cluttered attic of my fifty-one years and pulls out the cast of characters I thought I had buried, the dramatis personae of a hectic life I left behind, and it reinvents them. It redramatizes. It takes the raw footage of past events, those grainy reels of indignation and insult, and it edits them with a malicious artistry that would make Eisenstein weep with envy. The result? I wake not from terror, but from fury. A hot, helpless, impotent rage that leaves my jaw aching from grinding and my chest tight with the phantom pressure of arguments I never finished, injustices I never avenged, humiliations I thought I had metabolized decades ago.

The amygdala, that almond-shaped autocrat of emotion, does not distinguish between the remembered and the imagined. It processes the dreamt slight with the same ferocity as the real one, flooding my bloodstream with cortisol and adrenaline before my eyes even open. I lie there in my small flat on the boondocks of South Calcutta, my heart a clenched fist pounding against my ribs, and I am angry. Not at the dream, mind you, but at the people in it. People I no longer speak to, people I have deliberately excised from my waking life like tumors, people who exist now only as neurological ghosts, synaptic patterns that refuse to fade. My mind, that bipolar carnival of misfiring circuits, has taken the liberty of resurrecting them, of placing them in new scenarios where they betray me with fresh ingenuity, where they wound me with dialogue I never heard but somehow remember.

This is the cruel mechanics of memory consolidation. During REM sleep, the hippocampus—that seahorse-shaped archivist—replays the day's events, transferring them from short-term to long-term storage. But it is not a faithful recording. It is a remix. The prefrontal cortex, that rational moderator, is largely offline during dreams, leaving the emotional centers to run wild, to concatenate, to invent. The brain takes the emotional tag of an old wound and slaps it onto a new scenario, creating a pastiche of pain that feels prophetic, feels true. I dream of arguments that never happened with people who are gone, and I wake with the physiological signature of trauma. My blood pressure is elevated. My muscles are tense. I am ready for a fight that exists only in the theatre of my skull.

And then, the coffee. The ritual of salvation or the chemical lie, depending on your philosophy. Caffeine, that bitter alkaloid with the formula $C_8H_{10}N_4O_2$, crosses the blood-brain barrier and blocks the adenosine receptors that have accumulated their sleep-pressure debt throughout the night. It is a temporary reprieve, a molecular masquerade that tricks my neurons into firing with something resembling clarity. I stand in my kitchen, watching the steam rise from the cup, and I feel the anger dissipate—not because I have resolved it, but because the chemistry shifts. The parasympathetic nervous system begins to counter the sympathetic storm. The vagus nerve, that long wandering cable connecting brain to gut, sends its calming signals. I breathe. I remember that I am alone by choice, that the incarceration of self is also a sanctuary, that the characters in my dreams have no power here in the waking world.

But the indignity lingers. The indignation. The sense that I have been made a fool of by my own mind, forced to feel emotions for people who no longer matter, to fight battles that were lost or won long ago. This is the special torture of the reflective consciousness, the curse of being a creature that remembers. A dog does not dream of the insult from last year's vet visit and wake up growling. A dog simply is. But I am a man, and worse, a man with bipolar type II, which means my emotional weather is already prone to sudden squalls and unexpected fog. The dreams are just another layer, another stratum of sediment in the geological record of my psychic pain.

I look out the window at a Calcutta November morning, the light the color of weak tea, the air already thick with the promise of pollution and noise. I am here. I am awake. The people from my dreams are not. They are scattered across the globe or across the city, living their lives, unaware that they have been cast in the nightly melodrama of my subconscious. And I, the director, the playwright, the unwilling audience of one, must now face the day with the residue of their phantom performances still sticky on my skin. The show is over. The curtain falls. But the anger, ah, the anger takes its sweet time exiting the stage.
