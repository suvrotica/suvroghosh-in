The phone lights up on the table and I know, before touching it, that someone wants a map.

Not a real map. Those are easier. A life map. A job map. A technology map. A shortcut through confusion. Someone has heard that I know computers, America, healthcare data, writing, AI, or some other sufficiently vague noun, and therefore I may be able to say the one sentence that turns their fog into road.

I wish I could.

There is a special discomfort in being asked for help when you no longer trust the old formulas. Earlier in life I might have spoken confidently. Study this. Learn that. Send these emails. Build a portfolio. Network. Apply. Move. Persist. The verbs were clean because I had not yet seen how often clean verbs enter dirty rooms and lose their shoes.

Now every piece of advice arrives with footnotes.

Yes, learn the skill, but the market may shift. Yes, apply abroad, but borders are not moods. Yes, build something, but distribution is its own empire. Yes, be honest, but honesty may not be the fastest vehicle. Yes, work hard, but effort is not a currency accepted at every counter.

People do not enjoy advice with footnotes. They want direction, not weather.

So I have become, in some eyes, unhelpful. I hesitate. I qualify. I say "it depends" with the exhausted sincerity of a man who has lived long enough to know that it almost always does. This is mistaken for pessimism. Perhaps sometimes it is. But often it is simply respect for complexity.

Calcutta teaches this respect daily. A lane may be blocked because of a repair, a procession, a stalled vehicle, an argument, a wedding tent, a water leak, or no reason that can be defended in court. The person asking for directions wants the shortest path. The honest answer is: today, I do not know.

A life is worse.

Still, refusal has to be handled carefully. To say "I cannot help" can sound like cruelty when the other person is anxious. But false confidence is also cruelty, only delayed. It sends someone down a road you have not inspected and then calls the injury motivation.

So I try to offer smaller help. A document checked. A link sent. A warning about a course that promises too much. A paragraph made clearer. A call taken when energy permits. Not salvation. Not mentorship as theatre. A cup of water, not a bridge.

The phone waits. The room is warm. I pick it up and read the message properly.

Sometimes that is the most help I can honestly give.
