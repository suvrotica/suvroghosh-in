This blog began in the air, which is a risky place to begin anything except sleep.

I was flying from Los Angeles toward Fiji with a laptop, a few posts, and the fragile optimism that should probably be wrapped in newspaper before travel. Below was the Pacific. Ahead was Fiji. Behind was a life that had become hard to summarize politely.

The blog had started. That was the main thing. It had not conquered the internet. No editors had fainted. No strangers were arguing over the subscription button. But the thing existed. For a man alone with his thoughts, that is not nothing.

Fiji was beautiful in the obvious way, almost too beautiful for someone carrying private confusion. The water looked edited by a better planet. The air had manners. Then came kava, which I respected culturally and failed to enjoy personally. To my mouth, it tasted like earth had been persuaded to become a beverage. Hospitality was accepted. The tongue filed a separate report.

Still, I wrote.

Then came New Zealand, scenic enough to become dangerous for a drifting mind. Mountain, lake, sky, distance: all the materials of clarity, and yet clarity did not automatically arrive. A bad drinking spell turned travel into less of a plan and more of a sequence of airport-shaped consequences. The details matter less than the lesson: movement is not the same as direction.

Somehow I reached Abu Dhabi. Its orderliness had the quality of a rich place where chaos has been issued a visitor badge and told to behave. I looked around and told myself the useful sentence: focus, go back to the blog.

Naturally, I did not obey immediately.

I was busy pouring hope into other people's machinery. That is one of the world's most common unpaid jobs. Someone else has the large scheme. You bring your bucket. The machine makes impressive noises. Later, you notice the bucket is empty and no receipt was issued.

Then Dubai entered the story.

Dubai shines with a confidence that can make ordinary human objects feel underdressed. Glass, malls, perfume, gold, watches, indoor waterfalls, outdoor heat, and money quietly checking its reflection. What did I do there? I bought second-hand books.

This was not the approved Dubai activity. But there are few pleasures more sincere than an old book in a rich city. A used paperback does not flatter. It waits with its cracked spine and says: read, or do not pretend.

That bookstall is part of this blog's history.

The blog has never been only a website. It has been a small room. Some days badly ventilated, but mine. A place where technical thoughts, essays, jokes, anger, memory, doubt, and occasional common sense could sit together without needing to impress a committee.

For a long time I could not make it look right. The categories wandered. The design sulked. Some posts were personal, some technical, some almost decent, some only half-built. The whole thing felt like a cupboard opened in summer: useful, overcrowded, and faintly risky.

Lately, it feels closer.

Not famous. Not grand. More like a small stall with a clean kettle, a working bench, and a proprietor who may argue with you but will not knowingly serve nonsense.

The next problem is readers.

Writing is hard, but it is an old hard. Sit. Think. Delete. Make tea. Remove the sentence that looked too pleased with itself. Eventually something readable appears. Getting people to read is a different machine.

The internet is a bazaar where everyone is shouting, selling, reacting, explaining, posing, optimizing, threatening, and holding a light to the face of civilization. Somewhere inside that bazaar, a small blog post says: excuse me, I have something to say. Naturally, nobody hears it at first.

This is where SEO enters with practical shoes.

SEO is useful and slightly comic. The writer must think not only about rhythm, memory, truth, and human feeling, but also whether invisible machines have properly sniffed the page. One writes for people, but the road to people passes through crawlers, snippets, keywords, rankings, and search engines with mysterious digestion.

The old dream was simple: write something good, publish it, and readers will come. That belief belongs beside telegrams and people who answered unknown phone calls without suspicion. Attention now is hunted, rented, bought, borrowed, begged for, or received because someone already visible points in your direction.

Money helps. It helps with design, promotion, networking, hiring, polish, and the appearance of seriousness before anyone checks the substance. I do not have that kind of money. This is a small difficulty, like lacking a boat during a flood.

The blog therefore has to earn its seconds. It must open a door, then another. It must be clear enough to enter and interesting enough not to leave. This is not manipulation. It is hospitality. If you invite someone in, you switch on a light before showing them the strange machine in the back room.

Books have their own sadness now. Some are bought as promises to a future self. The future self reads, exercises, wakes early, understands history, and does not lose temper over slow internet. The present self arranges the book near a lamp and returns to scrolling. I am not innocent. I own books that look at me with disappointment.

A blog cannot even become furniture. Nobody sees it unless they open it. A blog behind an obscure address is a small thing in a muddy pond.

Then there are friends.

When money was easier, encouragement was easier too. People said the blog would grow. They advised, predicted, nodded, and smiled. Later, when the rough patch arrived, replies slowed. Warmth cooled. People have their own bills, work, illnesses, ambitions, and private collapses. Still, there is an education in watching attention become scarce exactly when usefulness declines.

The blog remained.

It waited through travel, bad focus, wrong turns, second-hand books, misplaced trust, design repairs, money anxiety, and the attempt to rebuild the small inner engine that keeps a person from turning into furniture.

Some days I ask why bother. Another part answers: because this still sounds like you.

Not the polished version. Not the employable version. The actual person who has lived in America and Calcutta, seen airports and bookstalls, worked around technology, argued with systems, watched expensive cities and damp lanes, and cannot pretend things are fine simply because pretending is easier to distribute.

I want readers. I want traffic. I want the graph to rise. I want the work to travel beyond acquaintances, polite avoiders, and people who promise to read links before placing them in the great dark warehouse of unread intentions.

But the world does not reward effort automatically. Effort is the background noise of the planet. The fruit seller, nurse, coder, driver, guard, clerk, cook, and exhausted commuter are all making effort. The universe does not hand out medals for sweating.

So the blog must become sharper, clearer, easier to enter, harder to forget. It must carry airports, old books, Calcutta lanes, technical systems, failed plans, small jokes, and the stubborn need to write without a proper budget.

I keep returning to that second-hand bookstall in Dubai. All that shine around it, and there it was: small, odd, findable by the right wanderer. Maybe the blog is not meant to compete with the glitter. Maybe it is meant to be the stall.

Small. Specific. Waiting beside the noise.

A blog does not need to begin as a monument. It can begin with a few cracked spines, a sharp sentence or two, and one middle-aged Bengali man from the edge of Calcutta still arranging his thoughts before the next bill arrives.
