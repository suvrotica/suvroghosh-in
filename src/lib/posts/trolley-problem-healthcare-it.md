The trolley rarely arrives with a lever.

In healthcare it arrives as a queue, a dashboard, a denial rule, an alert threshold, a bed assignment, a risk score, a staffing grid, a prior authorization form, or a model output that says one patient should be seen before another. The moral choice has already entered the workflow before anyone calls it philosophy.

The famous trolley problem asks whether one should divert a runaway trolley to save several people at the cost of one. It is useful because it strips moral tradeoff down to an almost cruel clarity. Healthcare does the opposite. It hides tradeoffs inside process.

Who waits? Who gets the bed? Which alert fires? Which result is escalated? Which claim is approved? Which outreach list is called first? Which patients are considered reachable? Which risks are visible because the record captured them? Which risks disappear because the system never learned to see them?

Every one of these questions distributes harm and attention.

Healthcare IT often presents these decisions as optimization. Reduce readmissions. Improve throughput. Lower cost. Increase quality. Prioritize high risk. Detect deterioration. Standardize care. These goals may be good. But the details are where the trolley hides.

A triage model may prioritize those with the highest predicted benefit. A resource allocation dashboard may favor those most likely to complete follow-up. A population health program may contact patients who answer the phone. A payer rule may delay expensive care until documentation satisfies a definition. A hospital queue may reward the person whose data is complete enough to trigger urgency.

The system looks procedural. The consequences are moral.

AI does not invent this problem. It accelerates it. A model can sort thousands of people faster than a human committee. It can also turn hidden assumptions into automated action. If the training data reflects unequal access, the model may reproduce that inequality with polished metrics. If the objective is cost reduction, patient need may become an inconvenience. If the label is poorly chosen, the system may optimize a proxy and call it care.

This is why healthcare AI governance cannot be only about accuracy.

Accuracy answers whether the model predicted its chosen target. It does not answer whether the target was ethically defensible, whether the input data was fair, whether the output can be acted on, whether the burden falls unevenly, or whether patients have a route to challenge the decision.

Clinical Decision Support has the same problem in smaller form.

An alert threshold decides when attention is interrupted. A risk score decides who is watched. A pathway decides what is considered normal. A default order set decides what is easy. Defaults are moral architecture because they shape behavior while appearing neutral.

Good Healthcare IT should make tradeoffs visible.

For any system that prioritizes, delays, denies, escalates, predicts, or routes, we should ask: what is the objective, who benefits, who waits, who is excluded, what data is missing, what appeal exists, what monitoring is in place, and what harms will be counted after deployment?

The answer cannot be solved by a paragraph in an ethics policy.

It has to live in design: audit logs, bias checks, subgroup performance, plain explanations, override paths, governance review, patient communication, and post-deployment surveillance. The architecture must remember that a decision made through software is still a decision made by an institution.

In Calcutta, queues teach moral philosophy without naming it. The person with influence moves. The person with papers waits. The person who knows the counter staff receives an instruction. The quiet person becomes furniture. Healthcare systems can reproduce the same logic digitally if nobody watches the queue.

The trolley is already here.

It is not dramatic. It is administrative.

That is why it is dangerous.
