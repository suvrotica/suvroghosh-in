Acronyms used in this post:

AI means Artificial Intelligence, software that generates, classifies, predicts, summarizes, or acts on patterns in data.

UBI means Universal Basic Income, a policy idea where people receive a regular basic cash payment without a work test or poverty test.

PDS means Public Distribution System, India's subsidized food grain distribution network.

MGNREGA means Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, the rural employment guarantee law.

DBT means Direct Benefit Transfer, cash routed electronically to beneficiaries.

The job portal glows at midnight, and the refresh button begins to feel like a small religious object.

That is the scene in which Universal Basic Income becomes serious for India. Not as a fashionable policy toy. Not as charity wrapped in better typography. It becomes serious when the old national bargain breaks: study hard, learn a skill, behave yourself, and somewhere the economy will find a chair for you before the music stops.

That bargain is already limping. If AI and robotics begin removing not only drudgery but employable dignity from clerks, coders, drivers, nurses' assistants, call-center workers, junior analysts, accountants, translators, tutors, warehouse staff, and the great educated crowd of resume-bearing aspirants, UBI stops being a drawing-room thought experiment. It becomes a question of civil peace.

The usual reply is that new technology creates new work. Often it does. A cheaper process can create more demand, more industries, and more tasks. Cheaper computing produced more software work, not less. Cheaper transport moved more people and goods. That is the old Jevons paradox in a modern shirt: make something cheaper and society may consume much more of it.

But suppose this time the rebound is weaker. Suppose AI and robotics do not merely cheapen production but absorb enough cognitive and physical tasks that new demand cannot employ the people displaced. Suppose the economy becomes wonderfully productive and socially barren, like a fruit tree behind a locked wall.

India is combustible in that future because unemployment here is not only missing wages. It is the collapse of family investment, coaching-center debt, caste aspiration, urban rent, masculine identity, household negotiation, and the parental belief that education is a bridge rather than a painted line ending in air. Educated unemployment is cruel in a special way. A person is told to wait, improve, certify, apply, refresh, and slowly discover that the ladder was drawn on the wall.

The official labour picture can look calmer than lived reality because a person may be statistically employed while economically stranded. A few hours of work, unpaid household labour, irregular self-employment, gig work without bargaining power, or a tiny subsistence activity can soften the unemployment rate while leaving a family in permanent improvisation. In a country where informality is less a sector than a weather system, measurement can turn distress into a neat table.

The table may be correct by definition and still far from truth.

UBI in such a setting is not first a poverty scheme. It is a floor under citizenship. A poverty scheme asks whether you are poor enough to deserve help. UBI asks whether survival should be hostage to labour-market demand. That difference matters.

Means-tested welfare in India often smells of forms, middlemen, missing names, wrong names, local pressure, biometric failure, server failure, bank correspondent failure, and the oily little miracle by which the least powerful person must repeatedly prove what everyone already knows. UBI removes some of that theatre because it does not require the poor to perform poverty for the state.

But universality is expensive. Anyone who says otherwise is selling incense smoke in a spreadsheet.

India already runs a crowded semi-welfare bazaar: PDS, MGNREGA, fertilizer subsidies, food subsidies, pensions, scholarships, LPG support, housing assistance, farm income transfers, DBT. It is not clean architecture. Each scheme has its own eligibility logic, leakage pattern, political constituency, and administrative priesthood. A real UBI would either sit on top of this, becoming fiscally enormous, or replace pieces of it, becoming politically explosive.

The argument for UBI is not that existing welfare is useless. Food subsidies matter. MGNREGA matters. Pensions matter. School meals matter. Public health matters where it works. The argument is that India's welfare state is fragmented by design and humiliating by habit. People are not integrated into a social contract. They are processed through a maze.

Automation makes the issue sharper. Workfare becomes strange when work itself is scarce. Skill programs become cruel when the economy needs fewer people at the end of the training tunnel. Employment incentives may help at the margin, but they assume employers still need bodies if nudged with subsidy. If automation lets firms expand output without expanding payroll, the old grammar fails.

You cannot train everyone into the narrow balcony where the remaining jobs sit.

The deeper issue is distribution. If AI and robotics raise output while reducing labour's share, the fight is not between workers and machines. Machines do not vote, lobby, bribe, or draft tax policy. The fight is between citizens and owners of automated capital. Who owns the systems? Who taxes their output? Who captures the productivity gain? Who pays for the wreckage? If those questions are left to the market alone, the answer will arrive dressed as efficiency.

This is where corruption and democratic decay matter. A UBI in a clean republic is a payment system. A UBI in a captured order is a battlefield. It can free citizens from petty gatekeepers, or it can become an elegant patronage machine. If the payment is universal, automatic, legally protected, and difficult to suspend, it strengthens citizenship. If it is routed through discretionary dashboards, branding, biometric chokepoints, opaque exclusions, and pre-election generosity, it becomes digital feudalism with better graphics.

The design must therefore be anti-capture architecture.

The payment should be a statutory entitlement, not a favour named like a garland. Eligibility should be simple. Suspension should require due process, not database suspicion. Payment rails should be auditable without turning citizens into surveillance livestock. Grievance redress should exist outside the local machinery that caused the failure. Cash should not become an excuse to gut clinics, schools, food security, disability support, or employment guarantees.

A poor person with a small transfer and no functioning public service has not been empowered. He has been handed bus fare to a locked door.

A serious Indian UBI would probably begin modestly: a national floor for adults, stronger child and elder benefits, disability supplements, automatic top-ups during verified regional shocks, and retention of essential public goods. The boring phrase is social protection architecture. The human phrase is simpler: do not replace the roof because you bought everyone an umbrella.

Financing remains the boulder. A meaningful UBI would require some mix of subsidy rationalization, taxation of high incomes and wealth, better property taxation, resource rents, carbon levies, inheritance taxation, improved compliance, and less theatrical spending on vanity projects. A robot tax sounds satisfying but is technically slippery because automation is not always a machine with arms. It is software, workflow redesign, scheduling, document processing, platform routing, and a thousand quiet substitutions.

Better to tax rents, profits, land value, monopoly power, carbon use, and high-end wealth than to chase every algorithm with a moral invoice. The goal is not to punish productivity. It is to prevent productivity from becoming a private elevator rising through public ruin.

UBI should not become hush money for a jobless civilization. Humans need income, but they also need participation, routine, competence, recognition, and the sanity that comes from being useful beyond one's own anxious household. If AI reduces formal employment, India will need public work beyond old categories: care work, ecological restoration, urban maintenance, elder support, public health outreach, school assistance, archival digitization, local-language data stewardship, disaster preparation, and civic repair.

These are not fake jobs. They are the neglected metabolism of a country.

UBI paired with public service options may be more humane than either alone. UBI gives bargaining power. Public work gives anchoring. Together they say: you will not starve because the market has no use for you this quarter, and you will not be treated as waste because the machine types faster than you.

The final question is not only whether India can afford UBI. The sharper question is whether India can afford a future in which millions of educated and skilled people become economically unnecessary while wealth concentrates behind automated walls and politics decays into spectacle, extraction, and command.

UBI will not repair democracy.

But without something like it, a technologically triumphant India may discover that the machines work beautifully, the dashboards glow, the billionaires applaud, and the citizens outside the gate have begun to understand the joke.
