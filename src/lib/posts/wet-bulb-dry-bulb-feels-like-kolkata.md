A Kolkata summer does not merely enter the room; it occupies the chair, drinks the water, and asks why everyone looks defeated.

The phone may say 38°C. That sounds like one number. But heat is not one number when it reaches the body. One 38°C day may feel like punishment with paperwork. Another may feel only normally unpleasant. The difference is often humidity, evaporation, wind, sunlight, and whether sweat can actually do its job.

Dry-bulb temperature is the ordinary air temperature. It is what a regular thermometer reads in the shade. When the weather report says the air is 38°C, that is the basic headline.

Wet-bulb temperature asks a different question: how much cooling can evaporation still provide?

Wrap a thermometer bulb in a wet cloth and move air across it. As water evaporates, it cools the cloth and the thermometer. In dry air, evaporation is easier, so the wet-bulb reading falls well below the dry-bulb reading. In humid air, evaporation is harder, so the wet-bulb reading rises closer to the ordinary air temperature.

This mirrors the body's cooling system.

Sweat cools only when it evaporates. Sweat sitting on skin is not victory. It is only evidence that the body is trying. If the surrounding air is already heavy with water vapor, evaporation slows. The shirt becomes wet, the face shines, and the body may still fail to dump heat efficiently.

That is why humid heat can be dangerous even when the dry-bulb number looks familiar.

The useful little formula is:

$$Q=mL_v$$

$Q$ is heat carried away, $m$ is the mass of water that evaporates, and $L_v$ is the latent heat of vaporization: the energy needed to turn liquid water into vapor. In ordinary language, evaporating water steals heat. That theft helps keep the body alive.

Kolkata's humidity interferes with the theft.

The third phrase is "feels like" temperature, often related to heat index in hot weather. Heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity to estimate how hot conditions feel to the body in shade. The National Weather Service distinguishes this from Wet-Bulb Globe Temperature, which is a broader heat-stress measure that also includes sunlight, wind, sun angle, and cloud cover.

So the three ideas are different.

Dry-bulb asks: how hot is the air?

Wet-bulb asks: how well can evaporation cool?

Feels-like asks: how punishing does the combination feel to the body?

Wind matters because moving air can help sweat evaporate. That is why a fan helps even if it is not cooling the air itself. It moves air across skin. But if the air is extremely humid, the help is reduced. If the air is very hot, the fan may become less useful than expected.

Sunlight matters because official air temperature is measured under controlled conditions, not while you stand on a concrete pavement beside traffic. Roads, roofs, walls, and buses add heat. The body lives in the street version of physics, not the clean version inside an instrument shelter.

This is why "only 34°C" can be a foolish sentence. A humid 34°C day may stress the body more than a drier hotter day. Heat is a negotiation between air, sweat, skin, clothing, work, age, health, hydration, shade, wind, and the room you are stuck in.

Public health advice is therefore physics in plain clothes. Drink water before desperation. Seek shade. Slow down in the hottest part of the day. Keep air moving. Watch older people, children, outdoor workers, and anyone who seems confused, faint, unusually exhausted, or stops sweating during dangerous heat.

The old city instinct says we have survived heat forever.

True.

But survival is not proof of invulnerability. Sometimes it is only proof that suffering became normal.

On a dry hot day, sweat can be a cooling system.

On a humid hot day, sweat can become a failed report.

That difference is worth remembering before the next afternoon settles on the city.
