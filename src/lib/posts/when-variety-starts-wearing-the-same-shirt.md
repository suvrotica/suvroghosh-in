The cafe wall has the same fake brick, the same warm bulb, the same little plant, and the same sentence about dreams painted in a font that has travelled farther than most of us.

That is the warning sign. Not English. Not pizza. Not sneakers. Not foreign films. A new language or new food does not automatically reduce a culture. Often it adds a window. Sometimes it adds a staircase.

The problem is that everybody's life is being pushed through the same small polishing machine.

Growing up in Calcutta meant living among differences that were not always charming but were undeniably alive. One neighborhood had one rhythm, another had another. One family spoke softly. Another treated every sentence as a courtroom submission. One shopkeeper had pride in his bad temper. Another remembered customers by debt, cough, shoe size, and political preference.

People had edges.

Even foolishness had local flavor.

Then the showroom flat began to spread. Cities remained different, but the aspirational layer started looking the same everywhere: the same cafes, the same birthday photographs, the same apartment interiors, the same school admission anxieties, the same vacation poses, the same phone-camera meals, the same neutral furniture, the same captions that sound as if everyone received emergency training in tasteful enthusiasm.

This is not culture changing like a river.

This is culture being pressed into molds.

The poor are not usually flattened first. They receive heat, rent, queue, dust, medical bills, and food costs. Homogenization comes first for people who can buy the costume. The ambitious class, the mall class, the private-school class, the airport-lounge class, the coaching-center class, the families teaching children not only what to know but how to appear globally acceptable.

That ambition should not be mocked. In India, ambition is often fear with polished shoes. A parent pushing a child toward a better accent, school, laptop, city, passport, and future is not a villain. He is frightened. She is frightened. One bad exam, one illness, one job loss, one unpaid loan, and the floor can open.

So people adapt.

They trim.

They sand.

They imitate success because success looks less vulnerable.

The danger begins when imitation becomes shame. Local speech becomes embarrassment. Local habits become provincial. The old table, the old phrase, the old snack, the old house, the old gesture, the unpolished joke, the crowded room, the family story with no good lighting: all are edited away.

What replaces them is not always richer variety. It is often sameness pretending to be sophistication.

Social media accelerates this because social media is less a window than a grinder. It turns experience into feed-shaped pieces: short, bright, recognizable, repeatable. You do not need to understand a thing deeply. You only need to recognize the type.

New cafe.

Fitness journey.

Airport selfie.

Political outrage.

Spiritual quote.

Family gratitude.

Career announcement.

Grief with good lighting.

The form arrives before the feeling. The person pours life into the form and calls it expression.

Now AI enters the room carrying a steam iron.

AI did not invent flattening. Television, advertising, malls, streaming platforms, global brands, corporate language, migration, private schooling, and social media had already done much of the work. AI changes the scale because it helps produce smoothness on demand.

A caption. A speech. A complaint. A condolence note. A school essay. A brand line. A message of thanks to someone one barely likes. The machine supplies fluency, balance, polish, and the faint smell of nothing in particular.

Bad AI is easy to reject. Good AI is more dangerous because it is useful. It improves the sentence. It removes awkwardness. It gives the thought a haircut and a better shirt. It may not kill the voice. It may upgrade the voice until it no longer belongs to anyone.

That is how variety disappears now.

Not by force.

By helpfulness.

The archives feeding these systems are not neutral mirrors of humanity. They contain what was written, digitized, indexed, published, optimized, uploaded, repeated, and rewarded. Some countries, classes, professions, languages, styles, and institutions enter as mountains. Others enter as dust.

Then the dust is invited to sound more like the mountain.

That is where the unease lives. The exact timing of a Bengali joke, the pressure of a Calcutta rent negotiation, the mood of a lane after a power cut, the private arithmetic of a lower-middle-class kitchen, the way a family says no without using the word no: these things exist, but not always in the machine with enough weight.

The machine knows many things.

It does not know proportion.

The answer is not to become a cultural fossil shouting at sneakers from a chair. The old world had plenty of rot: hierarchy, nosiness, superstition, cruelty, and confident ignorance. The past should not be restored wholesale. Some of it should be thanked and left outside with the broken umbrella.

The answer is discernment.

Keep the window. Refuse the showroom.

Take from the world without becoming paste. Use English, Bengali, Hindi, and whatever else life gives as extra lenses, not erasers. Use AI as a tool, not an inner landlord. Use social media for reach, not as a measuring tape for the soul.

Let a child learn global manners, but also let the child know the smell of a local market, the joke of his own people, the awkward history of his own family, the foolishness and beauty of his own place.

Do not preserve culture like pickle in a jar.

Cook with it. Argue with it. Mock it. Repair it. Let it travel. Let it come back changed, but not bleached.

Purity is not the goal. Purity is usually nonsense with a loudspeaker. Texture is the goal: grain, friction, locality, the small difference, the crooked sentence carrying a real person inside it.

Once everything becomes smooth, we may discover too late that smoothness is not civilization.

It is only the absence of fingerprints.
