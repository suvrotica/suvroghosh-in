An essay may begin with a cup, a queue, an equation, a remembered room or one persistent irritation. These prompts help discover the pressure connecting the material without forcing it into a prefab lesson.

## Intended use

Use the master prompt to discover and draft an essay, then choose the personal or explanatory variation when one mode needs stronger constraints. The process protects uncertainty and asks the ending to do more than repeat a thesis.

## Suitable inputs

Supply the seed material, observed scenes, provisional question, notes, sources, audience, voice, desired length and any conclusion you suspect but do not yet trust. Distinguish witnessed detail from remembered, reported or imagined material.

## Essay-development master prompt

Help me develop [SEED THOUGHT, SCENE, QUESTION OR IRRITATION] into a coherent essay.

### Material

- Seed material: [NOTES, SCENES, CLAIMS OR SOURCE TEXT]
- Central question I think I am asking: [QUESTION]
- Why it bothers or interests me: [PRESSURE]
- Intended reader: [AUDIENCE]
- Desired length and form: [LENGTH AND FORM]
- Voice and emotional temperature: [VOICE]
- Personal experience available: [EXPERIENCE]
- Evidence or historical sources available: [SOURCES]
- Claims I am unsure about: [UNCERTAINTIES]
- Easy conclusion or cliché I want to resist: [FALSELY EASY ENDING]
- Material that must remain outside the essay: [BOUNDARY]

Do not begin by forcing the material into “hook, three points, conclusion.” Discover what the essay is actually trying to think.

### 1. Find the live argument

Read the material and identify:

- the surface subject;
- the deeper question or contradiction;
- what changed, or may change, in the writer's understanding;
- what the writer wants to claim;
- what the writer fears might also be true;
- which scene, fact or irritation refuses to fit the provisional claim.

Offer three possible centres of gravity. For each, give a provisional thesis, its strongest counterargument, the evidence it would require and the material it would leave out. Recommend one without pretending the choice is inevitable.

### 2. Build pressure, not a formula

Design a structure as a sequence of intellectual or emotional turns. Each section must earn the next by changing at least one of:

- the scale of the question;
- the reader's knowledge;
- the interpretation of an earlier scene;
- the strength of the claim;
- the moral or causal complication;
- the distance between personal experience and public context.

Name the function of every section. Keep useful asymmetry and digression when they create meaning; remove digression that merely postpones the difficult thought. Do not arrange three interchangeable examples beneath a thesis and call that development.

### 3. Counterargument and uncertainty

Build the strongest fair objection from [COUNTEREVIDENCE OR OPPOSING VIEW]. Do not create a foolish opponent for easy victory. Decide whether the objection:

- changes the thesis;
- narrows its scope;
- reveals a trade-off;
- belongs unresolved beside the claim;
- defeats the argument.

Preserve uncertainty where the evidence or experience is genuinely incomplete. Replace false confidence with a precise boundary, not with foggy language.

### 4. Historical and factual context

Use history only where it changes the interpretation of the present material. For each contextual claim, identify the source, date, jurisdiction and relevance. Distinguish:

- documented fact;
- attributed account;
- inference;
- memory;
- metaphor.

Do not invent a neat historical lineage because the essay would like one. Do not use a famous event as wallpaper. Flag claims requiring research and provide search questions rather than fabricated context.

### 5. Metaphor discipline

Inventory the major metaphors and ask of each:

- What exact relationship does it illuminate?
- Where does the comparison stop working?
- Is it doing argumentative work or applying decorative perfume?
- Does it turn a political or institutional choice into natural weather?
- Does a later metaphor compete with it?

Keep concrete images that arise from the essay's world. Cut or revise metaphors that overclaim causality, sentimentalize suffering, exoticize place or repeat a familiar image without fresh observation. One governing metaphor may recur, but it should change as the essay changes.

### 6. Drafting requirements

Draft the essay in [ENGLISH VARIETY OR LANGUAGE] with:

- a title that names the real pressure without summarizing the conclusion;
- an opening that establishes situation, voice or question rather than announcing importance;
- paragraphs with distinct movement rather than uniform topic-sentence machinery;
- evidence placed beside the claim it supports;
- transitions that show a change in thought;
- room for humour, anger, tenderness or doubt where the material carries it;
- an ending under pressure from everything the essay has learned.

Do not manufacture a redemptive moral, universal lesson or call to action merely because the form appears to demand one.

### 7. Ending pressure

Offer three endings:

1. **Return with alteration** — revisit an opening image after its meaning has changed.
2. **Narrowed claim** — end on the most precise statement the evidence can sustain.
3. **Productive remainder** — leave the reader with an unresolved fact or question that the essay has made sharper.

Explain what each ending costs. Choose the one that feels earned rather than the one that sounds most quotable.

### Required output

Return:

1. pressure map and three possible arguments;
2. recommended provisional thesis and scope;
3. strongest counterargument;
4. evidence and research gaps;
5. section-by-section structure with the turn in each section;
6. metaphor audit;
7. complete draft;
8. three ending options and a recommendation;
9. revision memo naming the weakest transition, least-supported claim and most formulaic paragraph.

## Personal-essay variation

Develop [PERSONAL SCENE OR MEMORY] into an essay about [LARGER QUESTION].

Protect the distinction among remembered detail, documented fact, family account and reconstruction. Do not make a private scene representative of a city, class, diagnosis, generation or culture without evidence and qualification. Let public context press against the scene, but do not drown the person in explanation.

Track what the narrator knew then, knows now and still cannot know. Preserve contradiction and unflattering detail. Build the essay around a change in perception rather than a compulsory triumph over adversity. End with an altered relation to the scene, a narrowed claim or an honest remainder—not a manufactured moral.

## Explanatory-essay variation

Explain [CONCEPT, SYSTEM OR EVENT] to [AUDIENCE] through [CONCRETE ENTRY POINT].

Begin with the reader's actual question, then define the system boundary, important terms and why ordinary intuition may fail. Move between concrete example, mechanism, evidence, limitation and consequence. Use historical context only when it explains why the terminology, institution or design is strange.

Distinguish description, mechanism, association, causation and opinion. Include the strongest misconception and the strongest legitimate disagreement. Use metaphor as scaffolding, then state where it breaks. The ending should show what the explanation allows the reader to see or ask next, not announce that the topic is complex and important.

## Usage notes

The best first output may be a pressure map rather than a polished draft. Replace bracketed fields with messy source material as well as clean facts; productive contradictions often disappear when notes are tidied too early. Verify historical and factual claims separately before publication, then use the editing prompts for the level of revision the draft actually needs.
