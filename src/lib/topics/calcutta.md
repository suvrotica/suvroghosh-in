Calcutta is not a list of monuments. It is a system that begins negotiating with you before breakfast. Water arrives or does not. Tea establishes a small republic at the corner. A bus conductor discovers more capacity inside the human skeleton than anatomy textbooks admit. Hawkers, pedestrians, motorbikes, dogs, drains, schoolchildren, and parked cars revise the footpath by the minute. Heat presses differently on a worker outdoors, a family under tin, and someone able to close a glass door and switch on an air conditioner.

These pages approach the city through those negotiations. Food is biology, class, river, market, and memory before it becomes cuisine. Public space is livelihood for one person and obstruction for another. A traffic light turns destitution into an intimate encounter. Wet-bulb temperature explains part of a summer that civic endurance alone cannot solve. School photographs, old neighbourhoods, migration, and return show how a city persists inside the body even when its streets and moral vocabulary change.

The voice is affectionate without becoming promotional and angry without pretending every failure is uniquely Bengali. Calcutta can be absurd, cruel, funny, learned, exhausted, generous, vain, and structurally dangerous in the same afternoon. Begin with one life shaped by the city, continue through transport and appetite, then read the harder arguments about work, weather, class, and memory. When prose becomes insufficient, try to cross one procedurally hostile neighbourhood in the footpath simulator. The route is fictional; the negotiations will feel familiar.
