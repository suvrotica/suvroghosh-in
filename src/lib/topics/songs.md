A song is language placed under pressure from time. The syllable must arrive on a beat, the vowel must remain singable, the line must leave room for breath, and the chorus must return without feeling like a clerk stamping the same form. This is why a song is not simply a poem chopped into short lines. On the page, a phrase may appear profound; against melody it may become unpronounceable furniture. Rhythm, prosody, repetition, and the physical shape of a language decide whether words can live in a throat.

The collections here move through Bengali, English, and Hindi, with Calcutta frequently supplying the weather: tram wires, wet heat, tea, damaged public life, small rooms, politics, and the stubborn tenderness of people who remain. Begin by listening rather than by resolving the argument about artificial intelligence. The tracks are the primary material. The surrounding essays then show how a song can belong to an event or mood instead of floating as an anonymous piece of technically competent sound.

The deeper route asks what synthetic production changes. It can make arrangements, performances, and polished surfaces abundant, but abundance does not settle authorship. Human work remains in choosing, rejecting, revising, sequencing, disclosing, and deciding what one is willing to stand behind. Pronunciation still matters. A memorable chorus still needs shape. A listener's attention remains finite. These pages are therefore neither a sermon against machine-made music nor a claim that pressing a button makes everyone an artist. They are an invitation to listen closely enough that the machinery does not become the only interesting thing in the room.
