The calendar square sits there with the arrogance of a minor official.

April 16. A number, a month, a little printed box that once seemed to carry private electricity. I used to imagine the date arrived each year with a message folded inside it, as if the universe had placed a small marker on the road and said, here, this one matters. Age weakens that kind of superstition. Or perhaps it only makes the superstition too expensive to maintain.

A date is a convenience. A clerk's tool. A way for forms to remember what bodies forget.

The planet did not pause when I arrived. The star did not brighten. The galaxy did not adjust its shawl. A human life began somewhere on a small rotating rock, under ordinary gravity, in a universe already billions of years old and professionally indifferent. This is not an insult. It may be one of the few available comforts. The universe does not hate me. It does not bless me. It does not keep a small account book under my name.

Human beings dislike this.

We attach destiny to birthdays, anniversaries, first meetings, last days, exam results, property registrations, job letters, and other paperwork of the soul. We polish dates like brass. We say this day changed everything. Sometimes it did, locally. The body remembers. The family remembers. A bank, a school, a government office, or a social-media platform may remember. But cosmic meaning does not arrive because the calendar has developed sentiment.

Still, birthdays are useful irritants.

They force arithmetic. Not grand arithmetic, not the kind that explains stars, only the small unpleasant calculation of remaining years, spent years, failed ambitions, and the absurd backlog of self-improvement projects still pending in the municipal office of the mind. By a certain age, youth's heroic fantasies begin to look like old posters peeling from a damp wall. The colors remain, but the glue has lost its argument.

The sensible response would be humility.

Unfortunately, humility is difficult to perform in a room with bills, heat, unfinished work, and the stubborn wish to have mattered more. Even after accepting cosmic insignificance, one still wants a decent chair, a little respect, a working fan, and some evidence that the years were not merely used as packaging for worry.

This is where the mind becomes comic.

It can contemplate dark energy, cosmic background radiation, black holes, and the cold possibility of a universe drifting toward silence. Then it can be wounded by a delayed message, a bad bank balance, a stiff knee, or a birthday greeting that arrives with the emotional depth of an automated invoice. Scale does not rescue us from pettiness. It only makes the pettiness better lit.

Perhaps that is the real joke of age.

One grows large enough to see one's smallness, but not small enough to stop caring. The ego loses the court case and continues filing appeals. The body adds evidence. The room adds heat. The year adds another digit.

I do not know what time ultimately is. A line, a loop, a trick of measurement, a human handle attached to something too large to hold. I only know that one more birthday has passed through me like a local train through a station nobody repaired properly.

The date has no crown now.

It is only a square on the calendar, and the ink is already drying.
