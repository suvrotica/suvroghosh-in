The chair remains in the room after the name has been removed from the list.

That is how erasure often happens. Not with thunder. Not with a dramatic announcement. One is not expelled from life by a man in uniform carrying a stamped order. One is simply no longer needed in the places where usefulness is counted. Calls reduce. Invitations thin. Work replies become polite vapour. Old competence sits in the corner like a machine for which the city no longer stocks parts.

Middle age has a bureaucratic imagination.

It does not kill you. It files you under pending. Then under inactive. Then under not applicable. The body continues, because bodies are stubborn clerks. The bank still wants its balance. The electricity company still knows the address. The phone still lights up with offers, scams, delivery alerts, and proof that commerce can locate a person long after society misplaces him.

The cruelty is administrative.

In youth, even failure has a kind of public shape. People ask what you will do next. They assume a next. By fifty and after, the question changes. The world becomes less curious. It does not ask what you might become. It asks whether you still perform a recognized function. If the answer is unclear, the human being begins to fade from the registry.

Calcutta understands registries.

The city is full of files, lists, receipts, certificates, forms, counters, stamps, and people waiting outside small windows for someone to confirm their existence. The same pattern enters private life. Are you earning? Are you useful? Are you invited? Are you consulted? Are you remembered without needing to remind people? If not, a quiet deletion begins.

This deletion is not complete. That is the worst part.

Complete deletion would have dignity. Instead one remains partly visible. The shopkeeper recognizes the face. The delivery person finds the door. The mirror still provides evidence. A former colleague may respond with delayed warmth. Someone may ask for help with a document, an old contact, a technical memory. For a moment, the file is reopened.

Then it closes again.

There is a temptation to turn this into tragedy. I distrust that temptation. Too much suffering becomes decorative when arranged for public display. The more honest picture is smaller: a man in a room, checking messages, noticing the day has passed without requiring him, feeling both insulted and relieved.

Usefulness is a vulgar god, but it is powerful.

When it withdraws, one discovers how many social lights were connected to it. Respect, attention, even ordinary tenderness often travel through the wiring of utility. Be useful and people call. Stop being useful and the silence becomes very well organized.

Still, deletion is not the same as nonexistence.

There is the cup to wash. The page to write. The body to feed. The old skill that has not vanished merely because a market has failed to price it. There is the slightly absurd duty of remaining present without official confirmation.

Maybe the final erasure will also be small.

No music. No stage. Just one evening in a hot room, a phone at five percent, the fan doing what it can, and some tiny nuisance near the ear reminding me, with more accuracy than any institution, that I am still locally detectable.
