The plastic chair is the insult.

Not poverty itself. Poverty in Calcutta has old manners. It sits on the bed, asks for tea, and behaves as if it has known the family longer than electricity. The plastic chair is sharper. It receives the body after America, after serious rooms, after healthcare data systems, after SQL queries, after VA work, after NIH-adjacent discipline, after all the little evidences that one had once belonged to a world where competence had a door and a badge.

Then the chair says: sit.

Returning is often described as an emotional event. That is too soft. It is also an infrastructural demotion. America had its cruelties and absurdities, but it also had systems that made certain kinds of work legible. A calendar invite meant something. A database had an owner. A role had a shape. A project could be flawed and still exist inside an operating machine.

Back here, the old competence begins to feel like an expired passport.

It proves that one went somewhere. It does not guarantee entry now. You can speak of data warehouses, clinical systems, population studies, messy interoperability, and the long discipline of healthcare information technology. The room listens politely, if it listens at all, then asks a smaller question: what is the immediate transaction?

That question has power.

The market likes fresh labels. Age makes the label peel. Experience becomes difficult to package unless it arrives attached to a current title, a visible institution, a clean LinkedIn shape, a salary band, and the emotional tone of someone still eager to perform optimism. Old seriousness without current positioning becomes suspicious. It smells of explanation.

Calcutta does not care that you once handled larger systems.

The lane has its own systems: water timing, repair delays, rent, heat, neighbor noise, invoices, unreliable promises, the long choreography of staying afloat. The city reduces abstraction. It asks whether the fan works, whether the payment cleared, whether the vegetables are affordable, whether the day can pass without another humiliation disguised as a small administrative task.

This reduction can be brutal.

It can also be clarifying. In America, competence had scenery. Here, competence has to survive without scenery. It sits in an ordinary room, aging, irritated, still able to think, still able to arrange a sentence, still able to see through technical nonsense, but without the institutional frame that once made others recognize it quickly.

The danger is to mistake loss of frame for loss of substance.

A passport expires. The person who travelled does not vanish. A title disappears. The work done under it remains part of the mind. A market may fail to value an old skill at the moment one needs it valued. That is painful. It is not a reliable measure of the skill.

I do not want to romanticize this small room.

It is not a monastery. It is not a hidden academy. It is a room with bills, heat, memory, and too much time for the mind to become theatrical. Yet in that room, the old American seriousness has not entirely died. It has changed clothes. It has become less impressive and more stubborn.

After America, before nothing, there is this awkward interval.

A man on a plastic chair, technically alive, not saved, not finished, and not yet willing to agree with the chair's assessment.
