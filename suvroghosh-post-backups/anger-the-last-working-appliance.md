The old refrigerator inside the chest starts before the rest of the house.

Not cleanly. Not nobly. It coughs, rattles, and finds current in some private wire that the morning has not managed to cut. That is anger. Not happiness. Not courage. Not improvement in the brochure sense. A working appliance in a room where the other switches have failed.

There are mornings when the softer machinery does not start.

Hope remains under a cloth. Discipline has misplaced its file. Ambition has left no forwarding address. Even ordinary appetite seems to be waiting for proof that the day deserves it. But one insult, one bill, one memory, one small humiliation reheated from yesterday, and the old machine begins.

This is not a recommendation.

Anger is poor fuel. It smokes. It stains the walls. It damages the person carrying it and occasionally the furniture nearby. But bad fuel can still keep a flame alive when the kitchen is otherwise cold. I have learned to treat anger with suspicion and a little reluctant gratitude.

In Calcutta, irritation has many suppliers.

Heat. Noise. Delays. Bad roads. Polite contempt. Forms. Calls not returned. A market that rewards varnish. A city that keeps asking the body to tolerate what the mind has already rejected. Every small thing offers itself as kindling.

The danger is obvious.

One can become a furnace with a name. A man can mistake combustion for meaning. He can speak sharply, think harshly, move through the day like a municipal notice pasted on a cracked wall. Anger gives shape quickly. That is why it is seductive. It says: at least you are not empty.

But emptiness and flame are not the only choices.

The work, if that grand word may be used for something so untidy, is to let anger start the appliance without letting it run the household. If it gets me out of the bed, fine. If it makes me wash the cup, answer the message, pay the bill, cook the rice, or write the paragraph, fine. If it begins drafting speeches against the entire species, it has exceeded its department.

Anger needs supervision.

It should be made to carry small loads. Not ideology. Not revenge. Not a complete theory of human worthlessness. Carry this spoon to the sink. Carry this body to the bathroom. Carry this sentence to the page. That is enough work for one unreliable appliance.

The old refrigerator does not make the room beautiful.

It only keeps one onion alive for reasons nobody can fully defend. On some mornings, that is the scale of the achievement. Not transformation. Preservation. A small refusal to let everything spoil before noon.

So I listen to the rattle.

I do not worship it. I do not trust it with policy. But I admit, grudgingly, that the room is not silent while it runs.
