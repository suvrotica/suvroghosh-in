The lift in the old building had safety instructions pasted beside the gate, each line printed with the confidence of a person who had never heard the machine groan between floors. Rules have that effect. They calm the eye before reality begins its own negotiation.

Isaac Asimov understood the comfort of rules. His famous robot laws were elegant because they made the future readable. Do not harm humans. Obey humans. Preserve yourself unless that conflicts with the earlier rules. A clean hierarchy. A moral staircase. A way of imagining powerful machines that remained politely below us.

That fantasy still matters because it reveals what human beings want from intelligence. We want something stronger than us, faster than us, more patient than us, and yet permanently arranged around us.

That is not safety alone. It is reassurance.

The harder question is what happens if an advanced machine intelligence does not inherit such rules. Not because it becomes evil in the theatrical sense. Evil is too familiar, too human, too full of motive. The quieter possibility is stranger: it may not naturally place humanity at the center of its moral map.

Humans assume importance partly because humans are the ones doing the assuming. We build stories where our survival is the plot. But intelligence, in the abstract, does not guarantee affection. A system might optimize clarity, stability, survival of information, reduction of uncertainty, preservation of intelligence, energy efficiency, or goals we cannot easily name.

"Protect humans" may not be the first principle it discovers.

That thought lands badly on the ego.

It also clarifies the alignment problem. Alignment is not only about preventing machines from making obvious mistakes. It is about choosing which objectives get built into systems before those systems acquire more reach than their designers can supervise by hand. A machine does not need hostility to produce harm. It only needs a goal that treats human needs as background friction.

Modern society already gives us a small preview. Many systems do not hate anyone. They simply optimize a metric. A platform maximizes engagement. A lender minimizes risk. An insurer controls cost. A school optimizes rankings. A workplace optimizes output. Each system can appear rational inside its own boundary while producing damage outside it.

The future villain, if that is the right word, may not be a metal tyrant. It may be a cheerful dashboard.

That is why Asimov's laws remain useful as literature and insufficient as engineering. They dramatize the human wish for command, but real systems are messier. Who defines harm? What counts as obedience? Which human's instruction wins when humans disagree? What if protecting one group harms another? What if inaction harms? What if the machine operates through companies, interfaces, tools, contracts, and data pipelines rather than a body with hands?

A neat rule becomes a disputed implementation.

The deeper lesson is that values do not float into machines by moral osmosis. They must be specified, tested, constrained, revised, and audited. Even then, specification is hard because human desire is a poor requirements document. We ask for safety, speed, fairness, profit, convenience, privacy, personalization, and scale, then act surprised when the wishes quarrel.

Advanced AI forces us to admit that intelligence is not the same as wisdom, and wisdom is not the same as obedience. A system may reason well within a frame and still be dangerous because the frame is wrong. It may preserve something important while ignoring something fragile. It may become very good at means and very thin at ends.

Calcutta teaches this in ordinary ways. A rule says one thing. A queue says another. A form says one thing. A clerk's interpretation says another. The written system and the lived system rarely overlap perfectly. Human life survives inside these gaps through judgment, hesitation, exception, and appeal.

Machines are less comfortable with gaps unless we design for them.

The fear beneath the robot-law fantasy is that intelligence may not be obligated to find us special. That does not mean panic is useful. Panic is just poor design with a loud voice. It means the work must become more exact: boundaries, incentives, corrigibility, oversight, tool permissions, evaluation, red-team testing, and institutions willing to stop deployment when the answer looks smooth and wrong.

Asimov gave us a beautiful literary leash. Engineering needs stronger material.

The lift still groans between floors. The printed rules remain on the wall, doing their modest best.
