The drain outside my lane smells like a committee has failed.

Not one committee. Several. A layered history of promises, detergent water, old cooking oil, damp concrete, political posters, budget files, and rainwater that has lost faith in movement. The smell rises with democratic enthusiasm. It enters the morning, the shirt, the appetite, the mood, and the theory of civilization.

I live on a rough edge of the city, where Calcutta stops pretending to be heritage and begins admitting it is maintenance.

The roads are not roads in the philosophical sense. They are proposals. The lights work according to private doctrine. The drains negotiate with gravity. Repair arrives in patches, leaving behind the visual grammar of a sentence edited by twelve departments and understood by none.

This is not only civic failure.

After a while, the outside begins to resemble the inside. A badly lit municipality forms within the self. Some lanes are blocked. Some complaints are pending. Some old structures remain because removing them would expose worse wiring behind them. A signboard says improvement is underway. Nobody has seen the contractor since Tuesday.

Middle age encourages this comparison.

The body becomes local government. It delays, leaks, objects, and requires funds. The mind becomes an office with damp files. Old grievances sit in cupboards. New anxieties arrive without appointment. Certain emotional roads are permanently under repair. The citizen, unfortunately, is also the administrator.

There is comedy in this, though not always enough.

One looks at a broken streetlight and thinks: yes, that is my planning department. One sees water pooled near a corner and recognizes an old thought that also refuses to drain. One hears a generator cough and thinks of the inner machinery starting only after repeated insult.

The temptation is to despair theatrically.

But civic life teaches a smaller lesson. Most days are not rebuilt. They are managed. A little water is pushed aside. A wire is tied higher. A broken patch is avoided by memory. Someone places a brick over the worst mud. The system remains bad, yet people pass through it carrying vegetables, schoolbags, invoices, and the ordinary courage of not having alternatives.

The self survives similarly.

Not by becoming radiant. Not by achieving clean governance. By identifying the worst pothole and stepping around it. By knowing which thought floods after rain. By placing one working light near the desk. By not confusing neglect with destiny, even when the evidence is persuasive.

I do not want to romanticize broken things.

Broken drains are not poetry. Bad roads are not character-building. Poverty is not authenticity. The city's failures should be repaired, and so should many private ones. But until repair arrives, there is the practical intelligence of moving through damage without pretending damage is beauty.

Inside, I sit in the badly lit municipality of myself.

The next power cut will come.

For a few minutes, everything will become honest.
