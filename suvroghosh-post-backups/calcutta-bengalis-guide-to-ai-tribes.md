The crossing looked like a diagram drawn by someone who did not believe in diagrams. Buses angled in two directions, scooters found mathematical loopholes, pedestrians invented temporary rights, and every horn insisted it was the main argument. That is artificial intelligence: not one grand machine, but several schools of thought meeting at speed.

People say "AI" as if it means one thing. It does not. It is a quarrel with history.

The oldest dignified family is symbolic AI. This school loves rules, logic, symbols, and explicit reasoning. If this, then that. If a patient has these findings, consider that condition. If a device is in this state, apply that rule. Symbolic systems gave early AI planning, theorem proving, expert systems, and the dream that thought could be written down as clean machinery.

The strength is clarity. The weakness is the world.

Rules work beautifully when the domain is stable and the exceptions are manageable. Unfortunately, the world is full of ambiguous categories, missing context, local habits, informal exceptions, and people who describe the same fact three different ways before breakfast. Symbolic AI can reason once the world has been made clean. Making the world clean is often the actual problem.

Then came the neural-network school, with a different instinct: stop writing every rule by hand; let the machine learn patterns. A neural network is a layered mathematical system that adjusts internal weights after errors. In plain terms, it makes a guess, measures the mistake, distributes blame through the layers, and changes itself a little. Repeat this enough times with enough data, and patterns emerge.

Deep learning is this idea after cheap compute, large datasets, and better architectures arrived. It gave us major advances in image recognition, speech recognition, translation, recommendation, code generation, and large language models.

The gift is representation. The danger is opacity. A deep model may get the answer right without explaining itself in a way a human can inspect. It is like a student who solves a problem but leaves the working hidden. You may be impressed. You may also be nervous.

The Bayesians bring disciplined uncertainty. A Bayesian system begins with prior belief, observes evidence, and updates. This is useful in diagnosis, spam filtering, forecasting, risk estimation, and any domain where evidence arrives incomplete, late, or noisy. Bayesian thinking is humble by design. It does not say "certain" when "more likely" is the honest phrase.

Its weakness is that assumptions matter. A beautiful probabilistic model can still be wrong if its structure misrepresents the world.

The analogical and similarity-based school asks what a thing resembles. Support vector machines, nearest-neighbor methods, and many classification tools live near this instinct. Similarity is powerful because much human judgment works that way too. This looks like fraud. This scan resembles that pattern. This sentence feels like a known class of complaint.

But similarity is not meaning. Two things can be close under one measure and far apart in the way that matters.

Evolutionary methods take inspiration from selection. Start with many candidate solutions. Keep the better ones. Mutate and recombine them. Try again. These methods can search strange spaces and discover solutions no human designer would naturally invent. The danger sits in the fitness function. Whatever you reward, the system will chase. If the reward is badly chosen, the system can become clever in the wrong direction.

Reinforcement learning is about action over time. An agent acts, receives reward or penalty, and learns which choices lead to better outcomes. Games made this famous, but the broader idea applies wherever decisions unfold sequentially. The hard part, again, is reward. Ask for watch time and you may get addiction. Ask for low cost and you may cut the thing nobody measured. Ask for winning and you may dislike how victory arrives.

Fuzzy logic handles degrees rather than clean true-or-false boxes. Is the room hot? Is traffic heavy? Is a patient risk high? Ordinary language has gradients. Fuzzy systems formalize those gradients. The method is not sloppy; it is a way to represent gradual boundaries. But someone still defines the membership functions, and bad definitions produce bad decimals.

Expert systems tried to capture professional knowledge in rules. They remain useful where knowledge is explicit and the domain is bounded. Their old problem was maintenance. Rules accumulate. Exceptions spread. The expert leaves. The rule base begins to resemble a cupboard nobody wants to open.

Explainable AI arrived because black boxes began affecting important decisions. "Because the model said so" is not an explanation. If a system influences loans, hiring, care, fraud review, or public services, people deserve to know what mattered, what would change the outcome, and whether the explanation is faithful or merely comforting.

Modern systems mix these tribes. A large language model may use deep learning at its core, retrieval to fetch documents, symbolic tools for calculation, rules for safety, reinforcement learning for tuning, and explanations layered on top. The old huts have become one crowded kitchen.

That is why beginners get confused. The word AI hides a pipeline.

The practical question is not which tribe is best. That is too childish. Ask what kind of problem you have. If it is rule-heavy, rules help. If it is pattern-heavy, learning helps. If uncertainty is central, probability matters. If decisions unfold over time, reinforcement learning may be relevant. If consequences are serious, explanation and governance must be built from the start.

And if the data is messy, ask why.

Very often the mess is history. A label may be clean and still represent the wrong thing. A table may be complete and still encode a broken process. A model trained on such material learns the shape of the institution, not pure reality.

Outside the window, Calcutta continues its own form of intelligence: improvised, local, memory-rich, and often impossible to formalize neatly. A shopkeeper knows who will pay later. A commuter knows which lane floods. A clerk knows which form needs the stamp before the signature, despite what the printed instruction says.

AI is powerful, but the world remains larger than the model.

Learn the tribes. Use their tools. Distrust their slogans. The crossing is noisy because many roads really do meet there.
