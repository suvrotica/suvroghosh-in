The shirt gives up first. You step outside in Garia, Behala, Dum Dum, or Kasba, and within minutes the cloth has surrendered, the spectacles fog, and the air feels less like air than a damp towel pressed against the face.

Calcutta is not frying like Rajasthan. Calcutta is being pressure-cooked.

That difference matters. A dry 44°C announces itself with a brass-band cruelty. Calcutta's danger is often craftier: 36°C, 37°C, 38°C, heavy humidity, weak wind, warm nights, hard concrete, crowded buses, and rooms that keep the day's heat as if storing a grudge.

IMD means India Meteorological Department. NCDC means National Centre for Disease Control. IHIP is the Integrated Health Information Platform, India's digital disease-surveillance reporting system. HAP means Heat Action Plan. KCAP means Kolkata Climate Action Plan. HI is Heat Index, the "feels like" measure combining heat and humidity. WBT means Wet-Bulb Temperature, a measure of how well sweating can cool the body. ENSO is El Nino-Southern Oscillation, a Pacific climate cycle. KMC is Kolkata Municipal Corporation. AC means air conditioning. ORS means Oral Rehydration Salts.

In April 2026, IMD warned that India faced above-normal heatwave days across parts of east, central, and northwest India, with hot and humid weather expected in coastal and eastern states including West Bengal. That phrase, hot and humid, is where Calcutta enters wearing rubber sandals and carrying a plastic bottle.

The body cools itself through sweat evaporation. Sweat must evaporate. If humidity is high, evaporation slows. Then the body is not cooling efficiently; it is only losing water. In Calcutta, sweat often stays on the skin doing little useful work.

Heat is not democratic. It falls from the same sky but lands on different roofs.

On one roof there is AC, inverter backup, filtered water, a fridge, and irritation expressed on WhatsApp. On another roof there is tin, tar, one slow fan, a shared toilet, and a child trying to sleep while the room holds the day like a sealed tiffin. Same city. Different planets.

The sun is only the famous criminal. The wider gang includes humidity, poor housing, informal labor, weak enforcement, shrinking tree cover, vanishing ponds, traffic heat, indifferent employers, overloaded clinics, and the Indian habit of issuing advisories that dissolve before reaching the pavement.

During the West Bengal election period in April 2026, voters were advised in many districts to cast ballots before 11 a.m. because the late-morning and afternoon heat could become dangerous. Sensible advice. Also revealing. If a voter needs shade, water, timing, and medical readiness for a queue, what happens to a mason on a roof for six hours?

"Stay indoors" is not advice for a delivery rider. "Avoid 11 a.m. to 4 p.m." is not advice for a domestic worker with three houses to reach. "Drink plenty of water" is not a policy for a daily-wage worker who earns only when picked for work.

ORS is useful. Cheap, necessary, lifesaving in the right context. But it is still a sachet. A city cannot make a sachet do the work of labor policy.

Construction workers, street vendors, municipal cleaners, traffic police, security guards, delivery riders, cooks, loaders, rickshaw pullers, brick-kiln workers, farmers, and factory hands live heat as economics. Rice, rent, debt, medicine, phone recharge, school fees. The body negotiates with the wallet, and the wallet often wins until the body stops negotiating.

Heat does not always kill dramatically. It can work like quiet interest. Bad sleep. Dehydration. Dizziness. Kidney strain. Higher blood pressure. A diabetic patient becoming unstable. An elderly person slipping from discomfort into collapse. A worker losing wages because the afternoon became impossible. Then the certificate may say heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, brought dead, and heat leaves the room unrecorded.

That is why NCDC's 2026 advisory mattered. It asked states to track heatstroke cases and deaths, emergency attendance, and total deaths through IHIP from March 1 onward. It asked for health-facility readiness, ambulance preparedness, cooling measures, mortality review, and heat-related death investigation.

That sounds dry. It is not. It is the difference between seeing the danger and counting only the most obvious injuries.

Calcutta does not need only a heatstroke registry. It needs heat-aware hospitals, clinics, ambulances, ward offices, labor enforcement, and death review. Otherwise the numbers will look smaller than the suffering, and someone official will say the situation is under control, which often means it has not yet embarrassed the right person.

KCAP already speaks of ward-level temperature and humidity monitoring, green infrastructure, shaded intersections, reflective roads, hospital heat units, climate-smart settlements, and a formal HAP. Useful ideas. Some excellent. But a plan is not a shaded bus stop. A plan is not a cool roof over a one-room house. A plan is not a drinking-water point that works at 2 p.m. in May.

The city does not need another handsome PDF. It needs boring execution.

Boring is water at bus stops. Boring is shade over markets, clinic entrances, labor pickup points, and voting queues. Boring is public buildings opened as cooling rooms. Boring is school buildings used as daytime relief spaces during extreme heat. Boring is heat warnings in Bangla that ordinary people can understand. Boring is checking whether a cooling center has electricity, water, chairs, fans, and toilets. Boring is enforcement at construction sites when thresholds are crossed.

Boring saves lives.

AC also saves lives. Let us not become saints with hand fans. A hospital ward may need cooling. An elderly person in a sealed room may need cooling. A heat shelter may need cooling. But AC is also a private escape from a public failure. It cools the room and heats the street. It increases power demand and divides the city into cooled boxes and boiling corridors.

A city cannot AC its way out of bad planning.

Calcutta still has, or had, natural advantages: trees, ponds, wetlands, courtyards, shaded lanes, older buildings with high ceilings and thick walls. The East Kolkata Wetlands are not empty land waiting for a brochure. They are part of the city's lungs, kidneys, and sweat glands. Destroying them and then buying more cooling technology is not development. It is stupidity with financing.

The global context is not comforting. ENSO had been shifting toward El Nino conditions in 2026, though scientists are careful about declaring strength too early. Climate change does not need El Nino to create heat risk, but El Nino can add pressure to an already hotter baseline. What used to be extreme becomes rehearsal. What used to be warning becomes season.

So what should Calcutta do?

Measure heat as the body feels it: temperature, humidity, night-time heat, shade, wind, and exposure. Use Heat Index and Wet-Bulb Temperature where they matter. Publish ward-level risk. Tell people not only that it will be hot, but what kind of hot: humid hot, dangerous afternoon, hot night, worker-risk day, elderly-risk night.

Protect time. Move school hours, outdoor labor, municipal work, queues, and public events. In heat, time is infrastructure. A shaded 8 a.m. is not the same city as an unshaded 2 p.m.

Protect place. Shade bus stops, markets, clinic entrances, labor points. Paint low-income roofs white or reflective where feasible. Restore water bodies. Stop cutting trees and calling it beautification. Build cooling rooms where poor people can enter without feeling they have crossed into someone else's class position.

Protect records. Count suspected heat-related deaths. Review patterns. Look at excess deaths. Track emergency visits. Train clinics to ask about exposure. Let the data tell the truth even when the truth is inconvenient.

Protect workers. No heat plan is serious if it does not change the working day of people who cannot afford to stop.

Calcutta's summer of 2026 is not only about climate. It is about whether the city sees bodies: skin, sweat, sleep, wages, kidneys, children, old people, rent, medicine. Heat exposes which neighborhoods have trees, which workers have rights, which homes are ovens, which hospitals are ready, which plans are real, and which comforts are private.

The question is not whether Calcutta can endure the heat. Calcutta endures almost everything. The better question is whether it can stop confusing endurance with policy.

One day the air will be too wet, the room too sealed, the fan too useless, the clinic too crowded, the worker too tired. Then someone will ask who could have known.

We knew. The shirt had already surrendered.
