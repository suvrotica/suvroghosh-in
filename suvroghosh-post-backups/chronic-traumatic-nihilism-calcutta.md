The cracked floor tile outside the bathroom makes a small hollow sound every morning, and after a while the sound begins to feel less like a defect in cement and more like a private bell.

This is how a certain kind of disbelief begins. Not as philosophy. Not as a grand European posture, with books, coats, and magnificent beards arranged around the problem of existence. It begins when ordinary life taps the same weak place every day. Rent. Teeth. Work. The price of fish. A call you do not want to answer. A bill folded under a paperweight. One more polite conversation in which everyone pretends the future is merely delayed and not quietly leaking under the door.

Chronic traumatic nihilism is too large a phrase for such a small room, but the smaller phrases do not hold. Bad mood is too flimsy. Pessimism is too clean. Cynicism sounds almost stylish, as if one chose it in a shop. This is not chosen. It is accreted, like damp on a wall behind a cupboard. At first there is only a stain. Then there is a smell. Then one day you move the furniture and discover an entire climate.

A middle-aged middle-class Bengali learns this in installments. He does not need catastrophe. Catastrophe at least has theatrical dignity. It provides a date, a headline, a before and after. The more humiliating education is partial collapse. Nothing quite fails enough to be finished. Nothing succeeds enough to be trusted. The job market opens like a mouth and then closes. A tooth sends a reminder from the republic of neglected matter. A relative asks a harmless question with the precision of a tax officer. The mind, tired of surprise, begins to write every message in advance.

This is not wisdom. It only wears wisdom's old shawl.

There is a false maturity in expecting disappointment. People mistake it for realism because it speaks in a low voice and keeps receipts. But realism should notice what is actually there. Chronic nihilism edits the evidence before it arrives. The phone rings: bad news. The doorbell rings: demand. A new idea appears: foolishness. A small kindness occurs: temporary accounting error. The mind becomes a corrupt clerk who stamps REJECTED on applications before reading them.

Calcutta is a dangerous city for such thinking because it supplies both proof and contradiction in the same afternoon.

Walk through the city and you will find enough evidence for darkness. A road dug up and forgotten. A wire hanging like a public threat. A young man studying for an exam beside a drain. A shopkeeper doing arithmetic with the patience of a saint and the margins of a trapped insect. The civic imagination is often held together with tape, muscle memory, and shouting.

Then the contradiction arrives. A bookstall with a universe in a plastic sheet. A neighbor who knows which electrician will come in the evening if pressed correctly. A para club that is ridiculous for eleven months and useful in a flood. A tea stall where the glass is not a symbol of purity but the conversation briefly is. These things do not redeem the whole system. Redemption is too big a word. They merely prevent the mind from becoming completely dishonest.

That is the important distinction. Total darkness is also a simplification. It flatters the wounded person by making his wound universal. If nothing matters, then failure loses its particular shame. If every structure is rotten, then one's own small ruin can hide inside the public ruin like a clerk hiding behind a file rack.

But the world remains irritatingly mixed.

The electricity bill matters. The tooth matters. The cracked tile matters because someone may cut a foot on it. A line of song remembered by an old man matters for the five seconds during which the afternoon loosens its grip. A page written badly still matters more than a page not written at all. None of this produces a philosophy sturdy enough to sell. It only interrupts the lazy grandeur of despair.

Perhaps that is why this condition is so exhausting. It has no clean exit. Belief feels naive. Disbelief feels dishonest. Hope sounds like a slogan. Hopelessness sounds like showing off. One is left with maintenance, the least glamorous verb in the language.

Maintain the tooth until it can be fixed. Maintain the bill until it can be paid. Maintain the room. Maintain the sentence. Maintain a small ability to notice contradiction. Maintain enough shame to remain human, but not so much that shame becomes the landlord.

Some mornings, that is all the mind can do. It hears the hollow tile, steps over it, and refuses to promote the sound into a theory of the universe.

In Calcutta, one morning can be a large administrative unit.
