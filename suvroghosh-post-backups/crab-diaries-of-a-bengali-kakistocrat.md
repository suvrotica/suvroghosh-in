At four in the morning, the bathroom floor has the moral seriousness of a courtroom, and a small insect near the drain can look like a witness.

I was thinking about crabs.

This is not a recommendation. No one should stand half-awake in a Calcutta bathroom and allow marine biology to enter civic philosophy. But the mind has its own municipal failures. It lets one pipe leak into another. A sideways creature becomes a political theory. A damp corner becomes a parliament. A bathroom becomes the Republic.

Crabs move sideways because their bodies make sidewaysness efficient. That is already more honesty than most public systems provide. The crab does not issue a vision document promising forward movement. It does not stand before a banner saying transformation. It does not rename the beach and declare reform. It simply goes sideways, beautifully, indecently, with the full authority of its anatomy.

There is a word, kakistocracy, from Greek roots meaning rule by the worst. It sounds like a joke until one has spent enough time with institutions. Then it begins to feel less like vocabulary and more like weather. The worst does not always mean the most evil. Evil is too theatrical. Often the worst are smaller: the lazy, the evasive, the incurious, the clerks of delay, the men who mistake authority for competence, the networks that reward obedience and punish clarity.

The crab is an unfairly noble comparison. A crab at least has a proper exoskeleton and no public relations department.

Still, the metaphor scratches. In a decaying system, everyone learns sideways motion. A file does not move forward. It circulates. A complaint does not get answered. It migrates. An exam leak, a blocked message, a broken portal, a hospital queue, a hotel without enough safety, a road opened and abandoned: each event is treated as isolated, regrettable, unfortunate, under inquiry. But the pattern scuttles. It has legs.

The Indian talent for treating pattern as coincidence deserves its own ministry.

I say this as a Bengali man with no great claim to purity. I too move sideways. I too avoid. I too postpone. I too have learned the crab grammar of survival: do not walk directly toward humiliation if a lateral route is available. Do not answer every question. Do not expose every wound. Do not challenge every absurdity unless you have the money, time, and blood pressure for the consequences.

That is how kakistocracy wins most intimately. It trains the private body to imitate the public disease.

The tired citizen begins to think like a minor official. Later. Not now. Let it pass. Why invite trouble? Who will listen? What difference will it make? The phrases gather in the mouth like old coins. One day you discover that the institution outside and the institution inside have begun exchanging forms.

The crab, meanwhile, remains innocent. It does not believe in progress. It believes in tide, claw, food, danger, and the immediate geometry of its body. There is almost dignity in that. The human being is cursed with abstraction. We can imagine the straight road and then spend a lifetime explaining why the sideways lane is more practical.

Calcutta teaches this too well. The city has survived by improvisation, which is both its genius and its excuse. A broken thing is tied. A missing thing is substituted. A late thing is normalized. A dangerous thing is joked about. If the arrangement holds until evening, it becomes culture by morning.

I do not want to romanticize this. Improvisation is beautiful only when chosen. When forced, it is merely poverty wearing a clever shirt.

The insect near the drain disappeared while I was thinking. Perhaps it found a darker place. Perhaps it understood that no creature deserves to be turned into a symbol before sunrise. I stood there with the tap dripping and the city outside not yet fully awake, though some part of it was already cheating, waiting, bargaining, stamping, forwarding, denying.

The crab would have moved sideways and survived.

I made tea, which is also a kind of sideways movement.
