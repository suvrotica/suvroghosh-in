The answer looked sensible enough to begin. That was the danger. It had steps, headings, caveats, and the calm tone of a person who had repaired this exact problem many times. So you picked up the tool, opened the file, changed the setting, or started the plan.

AI becomes risky at the moment it sounds sensible.

When it talks nonsense, the danger is obvious. When it gives a neat plan, the danger enters politely. A non-expert suddenly feels expert-shaped. The screen has reduced fear. That can be useful. It can also become expensive.

The expert's value is not only facts. Facts are everywhere now. The expert knows what you do not know you do not know. That is the whole quiet profession. The hidden assumption. The unsafe shortcut. The field that looks optional but controls the invoice. The old workaround everyone forgot became a business rule.

AI can answer the question asked. A good expert challenges the question.

You ask how to migrate a database. AI gives a sequence. An expert asks why it is being migrated, who uses it, what reports depend on it, which timestamps matter, what "inactive" means, where the master record lives, and why a column with an innocent name appears in ten critical jobs.

You ask how to integrate healthcare systems. AI explains APIs, HL7, FHIR, mapping, validation, authentication, and testing. An expert says: show me real messages from a bad week.

That is where diagrams meet life.

Real systems are not clean. They carry local compromises. A field may be populated for billing, not care. A status may mean one thing in one department and another thing in another. A date may be entry time, event time, update time, interface time, or the moment a batch job remembered its duties.

Moving data is not preserving meaning.

This is the mistake non-experts make with help from a fluent machine. Transport looks like success. The file moved. The message arrived. The API returned green. The dashboard smiled. But the receiving system may have understood the wrong thing perfectly.

That is not data quality in the simple sweeping-the-floor sense. It is representation failure. The record may be clean and still unable to say what happened without bending reality into the available fields.

The same pattern appears in law, accounting, construction, repair, security, and software. AI can help with first drafts, explanations, comparisons, checklists, and preparation. It can make a meeting with an expert less foolish. That is real value.

It should not become the expert of record where errors are expensive, hidden, irreversible, regulated, safety-related, or dependent on context the user cannot fully describe.

The self-service economy hides the bill. You print your own boarding pass, troubleshoot your own router, scan your own groceries, fill your own forms, and now become your own apprentice consultant. The money saved is paid back in time, anxiety, rework, and sometimes damage. Your time is treated as free because nobody invoices you for your own life.

AI deepens that bargain. It gives you another fix, then another, then a more robust fix, then a revised explanation. It keeps helping. You may be learning. You may also be walking deeper into the swamp with better formatting.

The practical rule is plain. Use AI where errors are cheap, visible, reversible, and reviewable. Bring in expertise early where wrong but plausible answers can cost serious money, create legal trouble, corrupt data, break trust, harm safety, or hide risks.

The best use of AI is often to prepare for the expert: learn the vocabulary, list questions, identify possible failure modes, draft alternatives, and become less helpless. Then talk to someone who carries responsibility and has scars from real failure.

Scars matter. They are memory with consequences.

Do not surrender your brain to experts either. Experts can be wrong, defensive, narrow, or too attached to old chairs. But know when your brain is missing the map.

AI lowers the first rung. It does not remove the height of the ladder.
