The fake jacket hangs in the hill-market light with the confidence of an elected liar.

It has a foreign-looking logo, stitching that will surrender by winter, and the strange dignity of counterfeit goods everywhere: it knows exactly what the buyer wants to believe. Darjeeling is full of such objects now. Not only jackets. Fake certainty. Fake mountain feeling. Fake tea in packets that borrow the name of a place the way a pickpocket borrows your attention.

Darjeeling is not collapsing in a cinematic way. The mountain does not split open in one grand shot. The town does not announce its exhaustion through violins. It frays. That is worse because fraying is easy to deny. One more vehicle. One more plastic packet. One more bad hotel room painted just enough for the season. One more slope cut. One more drain carrying what nobody wants to name. One more tourist saying, "But we came all this way."

Love can be a method of damage.

Bengalis love Darjeeling with a possessive tenderness that is not always tender. We want the toy train, the tea, the cold air, the photographs, the mountain view if the weather cooperates, the shawl, the momo, the myth of escape. Calcutta sends its heat upward in the form of bodies and expectations. We arrive carrying the city inside us: bargaining, noise, impatience, appetite, the belief that a place exists to refresh us because we are tired.

But Darjeeling is not a spa built for our inner weather. It is a living hill town with water, waste, roads, schools, work, fragile slopes, and people who must remain after the tourist has gone back down with a phone full of clouds.

The counterfeit market is not a small detail. It is a symptom. A place becomes famous for the real, then survives by selling the imitation. Darjeeling tea has a Geographical Indication, a legal recognition that real origin matters. Yet the tourist economy teaches the opposite lesson everywhere: if the name is printed, if the packet looks right, if the story sounds pleasant, why ask further?

That is how places are emptied while remaining photogenic.

The hotel problem is similar. A room can have a view and still insult the mountain. Damp walls, careless wiring, bad plumbing, indifferent maintenance, rates inflated by season and scarcity. One pays not for hospitality but for proximity to a dream. The dream does the work. The room merely collects.

Then there is smoke. Vehicle smoke, trash smoke, cooking smoke, damp smoke from things burned because disposal is too difficult and pretending is easier. In a hill town, smoke does not simply vanish upward like a polite guest. It lingers. It sits in valleys. It joins mist and ruins the metaphor. Not all mist is poetry. Some of it is the atmosphere holding a dirty cloth to your face.

Overcrowding has its own sound: horns on narrow roads, luggage wheels, bargaining voices, drivers calculating impossible turns, young men trying to be louder than altitude, shops repeating the same objects until the eye grows tired. A hill road does not become wider because desire has arrived in more cars.

The deeper wound is ecological, but even that word can become too polished. Cut trees. Weakened slopes. Strained water. Waste pushed out of sight. Construction where restraint was needed. The mountain is treated as scenery, but scenery has no arteries. A living hill does.

There are also the quieter social damages, the cheap intoxications that collect around tourist economies, the quick money, the seasonal pressure, the young people learning that performance sells better than patience. This is not a moral lecture from the plains. Calcutta has no right to sneer. We have damaged our own air, water, roads, and nerves with great efficiency. Perhaps that is why Darjeeling hurts. We recognize the pattern. We know what a place looks like when love becomes extraction.

The uncomfortable question is whether Darjeeling can remain Darjeeling while being consumed as Darjeeling.

Nobody wants to answer this because every honest answer reduces convenience. Better regulation will irritate visitors. Better waste systems will cost money. Better building control will anger interests. Better transport planning will reduce someone's easy profit. Better buying habits will require tourists to care whether the thing in their hand is real.

Care is expensive. Sentiment is cheap.

The fake jacket will continue to sell. It is warm enough for the photograph. It carries the right lie. The buyer will descend to the plains and remember the trip kindly.

But the mountain keeps its own accounts.

One day, if we keep taking and naming the taking love, Darjeeling may not protest loudly. It may simply stop offering the Darjeeling we came to find.

The shops will remain open.

The real place may withdraw.
