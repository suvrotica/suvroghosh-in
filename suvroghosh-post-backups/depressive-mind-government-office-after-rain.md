The fan in the office inside my head turns slowly, making no wind, only evidence.

Everything is damp there. The files have swollen at the edges. The ink has spread into little black bruises. A clerk with tired eyes sits behind a wooden desk and stamps the same paper again and again: RECEIVED, PENDING, RETURNED, INCOMPLETE. Nobody knows who appointed him. Nobody knows why he has authority. Yet he has been running the place for years.

This is what rumination feels like to me: not a storm, not a tragic violin, but a government office after rain.

There is a queue. Every old mistake has taken a token. Every foolish sentence spoken too quickly. Every chance missed through pride, fear, laziness, confusion, exhaustion, or that uniquely Bengali talent for postponing action until action becomes archaeology. The line is long. The clerk is patient in the worst way.

He does not shout. Shouting would at least suggest energy. He merely asks for supporting documents.

Why did you not do more? Why did you return? Why did you trust that person? Why did you delay the tooth? Why did you spend the money? Why did you waste the year? Why did you think intelligence would protect you from ordinary consequences?

The questions are not always unfair. That is what makes the office hard to leave. A false accusation can be rejected with some dignity. A partly accurate accusation sits down and asks for tea.

Regret has a bureaucratic genius. It takes a messy life and rearranges it into files. It removes weather, confusion, missing information, bad timing, tiredness, fear, money, and the stupidity of the available hour. Then it presents the past as if it had been a clean form you filled incorrectly.

But life did not arrive as a form.

Life arrived as heat, debt, family pressure, ambition, weak teeth, unread emails, broken sleep, missed buses, half-knowledge, pride, and the strange fog that surrounds decisions while they are still decisions. Later, after the damage is visible, everything looks obvious. The mind becomes a historian with a police baton.

The damp office loves this hindsight. It survives on it. It does not create new facts. It rearranges old ones under bad light.

In Calcutta, one understands bad offices too well. The ceiling fan that has negotiated separate peace with gravity. The chair polished by generations of waiting. The smell of paper, sweat, tea, dust, and human postponement. The counter where your urgency becomes merely another object on the desk. The official who does not hate you, which is worse. He is only the face of a system that learned long ago how to outwait the living.

The inner office works the same way. It outwaits the present.

You sit down to write, and it produces a file from 2009. You try to eat quietly, and it asks for clarification regarding 2014. You look at the morning light, and it reminds you that light has been wasted before. It is not dramatic. It is administrative.

The temptation is to burn the office down. But that too is fantasy. The files are made of memory, and memory does not burn cleanly. It smokes. It gets into the curtains. It returns as smell.

Perhaps the only practical rebellion is procedural.

Do not answer every notice. Do not attend every hearing. Do not treat every stamped document as law. The clerk may continue to sit there, but you are allowed to step out for water. You are allowed to leave one file unopened until tomorrow. You are allowed to say: yes, that happened, and no, we are not spending the entire afternoon under that fan.

This is not forgiveness. Forgiveness is too grand for most Tuesdays. It is closer to office management.

Move one file to the side. Open the window if it opens. Let the damp smell admit it is only smell. Write one paragraph while the clerk searches for the correct stamp.

Perhaps he will find it later.

Perhaps not.

Even in the ugliest office, a man is sometimes allowed to walk out before closing time.
