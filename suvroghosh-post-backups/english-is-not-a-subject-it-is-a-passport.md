A grammar notebook sits open under a ceiling fan, red marks everywhere, and some child is being taught that English is a subject. This is the first mistake. English is no longer only a subject with tenses, essays, comprehension passages, and a teacher who can smell a missing article from three benches away.

English has become a passport.

Not the leather kind. The invisible one. You show it when you read a science article without sweating, write a clean email, understand a software manual, follow a research paper, search a documentation page, or ask an AI system a precise question and receive something better than soup.

AI means Artificial Intelligence, computer systems that can generate language, recognize images, translate, summarize, assist with code, and detect patterns. STEM means Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, in its careful form, says language can influence how we notice, classify, imagine, and think. A corpus is a large collection of written or spoken material used for study, search, translation, machine learning, or AI training.

English being powerful does not make Bengali, Hindi, Tamil, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Assamese, Punjabi, Urdu, or any Indian language small. That quarrel is foolish. A first language carries childhood, jokes, street names, food memory, affection, anger, and the private weather of the heart.

But home and passport are different objects.

History pushed English forward through empire, trade, administration, science, universities, corporations, computers, the internet, and now AI. Fair? Not especially. Useful to know? Absolutely. The British did not spread English as a gift to future Indian students. They governed, extracted, catalogued, traded, and ruled. History left damage and debris. Some debris became useful tools.

Now science speaks heavily in English. Software speaks heavily in English. Aviation, medicine, finance, diplomacy, research, higher education, coding forums, product manuals, legal contracts, startup pitch decks, journal articles, and much of the internet's useful argument lean toward English. Not entirely. Enough to matter.

A language becomes powerful when powerful things keep happening inside it. English has accumulated papers, textbooks, patents, lectures, standards, tutorials, essays, manuals, court judgments, documentation, errors, corrections, jokes, and code comments. Knowledge is not floating in the air. It sits in sentences.

If the sentences are in English, English becomes an access road.

Reading English is therefore not a hobby for polished people with bookmarks. It is a survival skill. A student who reads English badly does not only miss novels. He misses the machinery of the world. He waits for a coaching note, a simplified video, a cousin from Bengaluru, or a confident explainer who may be late, wrong, or selling something.

Writing matters even more because writing catches thought in public. Inside the head, every idea feels respectable. Put it on the page and suddenly it limps. That is not failure. That is education beginning. Writing tells you where the thought has no bones.

Clear English is the goal. Not perfume writing. Not "I hereby wish to bring to your kind perusal" writing. Working writing. The kind that explains what you mean without trapping the reader inside a government circular.

A student who can write one clean paragraph has defeated one small enemy. A student who can write ten can build a career.

Language also sharpens distinctions. "May" is not "must." "Suggests" is not "proves." "Associated with" is not "caused by." "Risk" is not "certainty." "Hypothesis" is not "truth." These are brakes and steering for serious thought. Without them, the mind may have plenty of horn and not enough direction.

Now add AI.

Many people believe translation tools will make English unnecessary. Translation will help. It already helps. It will improve. But the person who knows English well still has an advantage because much AI documentation, prompting practice, research, code examples, model behavior, and technical community discussion are deeply soaked in English.

AI rewards precision. Compare two requests.

"Make project on pollution good points."

"Create a 900-word school-level explanation of air pollution in Kolkata, with causes, health effects, two local examples, and three practical solutions, written in clear English for Class 10 students."

These are not the same command. The first asks for fog. The second gives a machine enough shape to work with. English, used well, becomes not only a language skill but a machine-handling skill.

The problem in India is that English is still treated as a class marker. Accent gets worshipped. School names become social passwords. One Indian judges another Indian's intelligence by the music of his vowels. This is ugly and mostly useless.

Accent is not intelligence. Clarity, accuracy, range, and confidence matter. Do not try to become English. Become dangerous with English.

Dangerous means you can read a contract before signing away your weekends. Dangerous means you can question a chart, write a scholarship email, summarize a paper, draft a complaint, build a resume, describe a product, explain a bug, and notice when polished language is hiding weak thought.

For the Indian middle class, English is not everything. Discipline, curiosity, health, money, luck, and institutions still matter. But English is a lever. A lever does not lift the world alone. Without it, the load becomes unnecessarily cruel.

The habit is simple and difficult. Read a little every day. Real English, not only social media crumbs. Essays, explainers, biographies, manuals, histories, interviews, fiction, technical guides, even product instructions if that is what you have. Notice how sentences move. Notice how good writing says a hard thing clearly.

Write a little every day. One paragraph. One observation. One explanation. One complaint. One memory. One summary of something learned. Do not wait to become fluent before writing. That is like waiting to become strong before lifting a dumbbell.

Make mistakes. Repair them. Continue.

Your first language should stay close. Always. It roots you. English gives reach. A life needs both root and branch.

The future will not pause to ask whether this arrangement is fair. It will ask whether you can read the instructions. Then it will ask whether you can question them. Then, if you are lucky and stubborn, it may ask whether you can write better ones.
