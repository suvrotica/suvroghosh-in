The wall beside the little kindergarten held the stain longer than my courage did.

Before WWA Cossipore English School, there was a small place across the street where very small children were gathered, watched, instructed, and sometimes failed by plumbing. I remember standing beside another boy and urinating against a wall because there was no proper bathroom. I remember the wall. I remember the wet mark. I remember noticing, with the cruel precision of childhood, that his mark seemed larger than mine.

That was all.

But a child can make a prophecy out of almost nothing.

The conclusion arrived before I had any useful knowledge of bodies, comparison, confidence, shame, or the comedy of indoor sanitation. My mark would be small. My place would be small. The world had offered evidence, and I had believed it because children are excellent at building elaborate errors from single facts.

This is how a life can begin its private philosophy: not with Plato, not with the Upanishads, not with a grand school speech, but with a urine stain drying on a Calcutta wall.

The adult decision that sent me into English-medium schooling may have been one of the administrative miracles of my life. At the time it was probably one more household calculation made under the usual Bengali weather of anxiety, aspiration, thrift, gossip, and fear of falling behind. Later, it looked like luck.

Childhood is not designed. It is assigned. You are dropped into a lane, a language, a school, a class position, a set of smells, rules, humiliations, lunchboxes, myths, and prohibitions. Then everyone pretends your personality was an internal project. Mine was assembled more like municipal wiring in old Calcutta: partly planned, partly improvised, partly exposed, and always one monsoon away from sparks.

WWA was supposed to make me useful. English, handwriting, arithmetic, discipline, posture, exams, a little middle-class polish. That was the brochure version, whether or not any brochure said it. Instead it gave me something more troublesome: a durable allergy to certainty.

No school advertises that. No prospectus says, send us your child and we may return a half-formed skeptic with mythological images in his head, weak social confidence, and a lifelong suspicion of public toilets. But that is roughly what happened.

The school failed beautifully.

It tried to educate me into society and left a window open through which doubt entered.

The oddity is that doubt did not arrive cleanly. It came through embarrassment, comparison, bodily panic, language, superstition, class signals, and those strange childhood conversations where boys with no knowledge spoke with priestly authority. Childhood has a whole underground curriculum. Officially there is grammar, multiplication, dictation, history. Unofficially there is who is strong, who is laughed at, who speaks better English, who has money, who is feared, who is ignored, who can use the toilet without incident.

The unofficial syllabus often goes deeper.

India teaches certainty early. Religion offers certainty. Politics offers certainty. Family pride offers certainty. Class anxiety offers certainty. Television later adds certainty at a volume that makes thought unnecessary. School, at its best, should interrupt this. It should teach a child that a claim needs evidence, a story is not fact because it is old, and authority is not truth wearing shoes.

WWA did not do this perfectly. No institution does. But it leaked enough for curiosity to escape.

Mythology was one of the first places where the leak mattered. I loved the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, the vows, disguises, weapons, exiles, arguments, and impossible scale of those stories. They remain great narrative machinery. They contain pride, revenge, tenderness, absurdity, duty, appetite, and power. But stories are not facts simply because they are old. Age gives a story texture, not proof.

This is where many Indians become trapped. We inherit magnificent stories and then try to convert them into science textbooks, land records, medical claims, and proof of ancient supremacy. It is not enough to enjoy the epic. The epic must become aviation, surgery, nuclear physics, moral purity, national destiny, and prime-time shouting.

What a waste.

A story can be powerful without being literal. A myth can tell the truth about human behavior while being false as history. That distinction is not an insult to tradition. It is respect for the correct instrument. A hammer is useful. It is not a thermometer.

English-medium schooling complicated this further. English was not only a subject. It was a passport office for the imagination. It did not guarantee escape, but it gave access to maps. It opened shelves. It made other ways of thinking available. It also brought status, insecurity, accent games, and the small embarrassment of being one kind of Bengali at home and another kind on paper.

The language enlarged me and judged me at the same time.

That is often how class works in India. It does not merely deny. It also invites, permits, mocks, trains, and marks. English could make a lower-middle-class child feel possible. It could also make him feel unfinished. Every sentence became both tool and measurement.

The school years were full of such measurements. Marks, height, handwriting, pronunciation, clothes, confidence, classroom speed, toilet control. The body was never separate from education. For some children, school anxiety is not a concept. It is a pressure in the abdomen during arithmetic, a calculation of distance to the nearest usable bathroom, a fear that dignity may fail in public before the sums are done.

I remember soiling my school clothes more than once. There is no graceful way to write that sentence. It is not decorative childhood material. It does not belong on a cushion. But it matters because it belongs to the actual child, not the edited one. Education happens in bodies. A child is not only a mind with a satchel.

Public toilets in India remain a hard argument against our claims of civilization. They exist in theory, like many noble things. In practice, anyone with remaining self-respect plans around them. Much of childhood outside the home involved risk assessment that no timetable acknowledged.

And yet the school lit something.

It gave me English, which gave me access not merely to status but to thought. It gave me teachers whose imperfections taught me that authority was human. It gave me classmates who became evidence that people are never as simple as categories. It gave me enough structure to resist structure.

That is not a small gift.

The deeper failure of education is not that it fails to produce employable people. It often does that adequately for the mills of society. The deeper failure is that it produces too many people who become certain too early. Certainty is the cheapest luxury good of the educated classes. The truly educated person should be harder to fool, but also harder to harden.

Calcutta, meanwhile, remains overcrowded, tender, brutal, funny, decaying, argumentative, full of gods, short on drains, and rich in wounded egos looking for a microphone. A mob can form here with alarming speed. Religion supplies fuel. Politics supplies matches. Poverty supplies dry material. Then everyone behaves surprised when something catches.

Against all this, what can one small urine stain do?

Not much.

But perhaps that was always the wrong measurement. A small stain is still evidence that someone stood there. A child stood there, frightened, comparing himself, already building a theory of inadequacy from a wall in Calcutta. That child went on to read, doubt, desire, fail, return, write, mock, mourn, and keep thinking.

The mark was small.

It was not nothing.
