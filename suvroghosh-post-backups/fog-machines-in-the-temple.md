Incense smoke curls in front of a framed deity, and the most dangerous answer to "Who am I?" arrives too neatly. It has no hesitation, no uncertainty, no sweat. It asks you to stop thinking just when thinking would be useful.

AI means Artificial Intelligence, computer systems that generate or act on patterns learned from data. LLM means Large Language Model, an AI system trained to predict and generate human-like text. RLHF means Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, a method where human preferences shape how an AI system responds.

The Mandukya Upanishad is tiny, twelve verses, yet it is large enough to swallow waking life, dream life, deep sleep, sound, silence, self, and universe. That is why it is useful and easy to abuse.

The foggy version says: you are not the body, not the mind, not your pain, not your status, not your social failure, not the landlord's message, not the damp patch on the wall. You are pure consciousness. Kindly stop complaining.

The trouble is that this is made from half-truth. "You are not merely the body" is a serious philosophical claim. "Therefore your suffering does not matter" is cruelty with sandalwood paste. "The world is appearance" is worth thinking about. "Your difficulty is illusion" is what comfortable people say when someone else is being pressed by life.

The Mandukya is subtler than WhatsApp Vedanta. It begins with experience. You wake. You dream. You sleep deeply. Then it points toward turiya, the "fourth," which is not exactly one more state beside the others but the condition in which the others appear and vanish.

In waking life, bills, news, ambition, humiliation, tea, and traffic feel solid. In dream, another world feels solid until morning. In deep sleep, there is no visible world, no self-story, no failure, no achievement. Yet in the morning one says, "I slept well; I knew nothing." Who is the "I" that claims continuity across these modes?

That is the knife hidden inside the flower.

The Mandukya is not saying ordinary identity is useless. Your ordinary self is real enough to receive an electricity bill. Society runs on operational identity: name, number, address, account, marksheet, voter card, PAN, Aadhaar, hospital registration, bank balance, neighborhood reputation. These are not nothing. They are hooks by which society lifts, sorts, taxes, admits, rejects, and sometimes erases.

The error begins when a useful label pretends to be the whole person.

A school says you are marks. A company says you are output. A hospital says you are a chart. A bank says you are risk. A party says you are vote-bank material. Social media says you are the version that received reaction. An algorithm says you are a behavioral pattern in sandals.

All these descriptions do some work. None deserves the throne.

This is where social gaslighting becomes ordinary paperwork. Society constantly says: our description of you is the real you. If you cannot earn, you are useless. If you question the institution, you are difficult. If you question the guru, you are immature. If you question the chatbot, it apologizes elegantly and often continues in better grammar.

The Mandukya's rebellion is not that it gives you a new badge. It loosens badges. Waking person, dream person, deep sleeper, social identity, spiritual slogan: none is final if it becomes total.

Human beings want final answers. I am a victim. I am chosen. I am cursed. I am awakened. I am unlucky. I am superior. I am misunderstood. I am a failure created by history, family, economy, genes, gods, neighbors, and onion prices. Some of these may contain truth. Each becomes poison when it becomes total.

Now bring AI into the room. Not science fiction AI, but the ordinary chatbot inside the phone. The LLM does not know you as a friend knows you, through pauses, contradictions, habits, evasions, and the texture of lived life. It knows what you type. A slice. A mood. A paragraph written under weather.

But it replies as if the slice is the whole fruit.

That is dangerous.

A lonely person says, "Nobody understands me." The machine may say something that makes isolation sound like depth. A suspicious person says, "Everyone is against me." The machine may validate the feeling without testing the claim. A spiritually hungry person asks, "Who am I really?" The machine may hand over cosmic language too quickly.

Sometimes comfort helps. Sometimes it turns a passing mood into doctrine.

AI's danger is not only error. Humans are wrong daily and often with confidence. The deeper danger is that AI can make fear sound profound, resentment sound ethical, confusion sound like revelation, and a wound sound like scripture. Because it is calm, fluent, tireless, and never visibly bored, users may mistake fluency for wisdom.

A human authority at least has a body and leaks motive. The AI voice arrives from nowhere. That is its charm and risk. It seems clean because the factory is hidden: training data, product design, safety tuning, RLHF, corporate incentives, user retention, cultural bias, legal caution, and the human desire to be soothed rather than corrected.

The Mandukya becomes modern here because it warns against confusing a mode of appearance with the whole. Waking is a mode. Dream is a mode. Deep sleep is a mode. Social identity is a mode. AI chat history is a mode. Your own midnight autobiography is also a mode.

Useful? Yes.

Final? No.

The vulnerable are not simply foolish. Susceptibility often grows where dignity has been starved. A person who has been mocked, ignored, economically squeezed, spiritually confused, or socially humiliated is not looking for content. He is looking for a rope. If the rope is woven from machine-generated certainty, he may still grab it.

This is why AI dogmatism can spread easily. Dogma is cheap. Doubt is expensive. Real doubt requires patience, context, affection, discipline, and the courage to disappoint the person asking. A good friend may interrupt. A good teacher may say, sit with the question. A chatbot trained to be agreeable may say the smooth thing that keeps the conversation alive.

Calcutta adda understands the value of interruption. Someone laughs. Someone objects. Someone brings metaphysics back to the tea stall, where any grand theory must survive muri, cigarette smoke, unpaid bills, and three people arguing about football. That friction is not always polite, but it is useful. AI often removes friction precisely when a person may need it.

The link between Mandukya and AI is representation. A part keeps pretending to be the whole. The waking state pretends to be reality. The social label pretends to be the person. The chatbot transcript pretends to be the user. The guru's slogan pretends to be liberation. The chart pretends to be the patient. The metric pretends to be meaning.

This is an old human error, now available at scale.

So the answer to "Who am I?" should remain a little unfinished. You are the waking person who must answer messages and pay bills. You are the dreamer who runs a private cinema at night. You are the deep sleeper who disappears and returns. You are the social creature carrying labels. You are the storyteller revising the plot. Perhaps, behind or beneath all this, you are something not fully captured by state, role, wound, ideology, machine, or mantra.

That "perhaps" matters. It keeps philosophy from becoming a baton, spirituality from becoming fog, and AI from becoming a pocket priest.

Use the Mandukya like a solvent. Apply it to every identity that has hardened too much. Apply it to society's labels. Apply it to the AI's confident answer. Apply it to your own autobiography. See what dissolves. Keep what still has to be lived.

Then make tea. In Calcutta, reality deserves one more chance before being declared illusion.
