The tea stain on the plastic table had branched overnight.

Yesterday it was only a ring. By afternoon it had acquired a smaller ring, a faint brown peninsula, and one thin line running toward the biscuit crumbs with the administrative confidence of a boundary dispute. This is how mathematics enters my room. Not with chalk, not with a lecture, not with the clean arrogance of a diagram, but through old tea, dust, heat, and the evidence that even neglect has structure.

Fractal geometry is the mathematics of roughness. A coastline, a cracked wall, the edge of burnt roti, the damp map forming near a switchboard after too many seasons of rain. From far away these things look simple. Come closer and the edge becomes complicated. Come closer still and the complication repeats itself.

That is also how a life looks.

From far away, a man can be summarized with cruel efficiency. Age, income, address, work, debt, usefulness. Society loves a long ruler because it misses detail and calls the omission judgment. With a shorter ruler, other facts appear: the cup not washed, the invoice still pending, the old competence remembered at an inconvenient hour, the towel waiting on a chair, the sentence written after three hours of resisting the day.

The big disorder has smaller disorders inside it. The small disorders contain still smaller ones. Money enters the kitchen. Weather enters the mood of the afternoon. Old ambition enters the price of tea. Nothing stays in its department.

This is not a consolation. Fractals do not say everything is meaningful. They say irregularity is not the same as emptiness. A broken pattern may still be a pattern.

So I look at the stain again. Its edge is darker now. Inside that edge are smaller edges.

The fan turns above me with its plain circular discipline.

I admire it, then get up to wash the cup before the table becomes a thesis.
