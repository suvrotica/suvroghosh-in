By eleven in the morning, the sweat under my arm had formed a small civic problem.

The rice cooker clicked. The fan moved heat from one side of the room to the other, a symbolic act with no measurable policy effect. I sat in that damp interval between tea and lunch and thought of Srinivasa Ramanujan, which is one of the strange taxes paid by a mind educated beyond its domestic arrangements.

Ramanujan wrote about highly composite numbers. A highly composite number has more divisors than any smaller positive integer before it. Take 60. It accepts 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, and 60. Everything finds a way into it. A crowded crossing. A tram depot. A ledger with too many signatures.

Some mornings are like that.

Everything divides them. Heat, money, memory, hunger, messages, old work, new embarrassment, the price of oil, the body asking for maintenance, the mind asking for a better room. Every small factor enters. Nothing remains prime.

Then comes afternoon.

Afternoon can become a prime number. Hard, solitary, indivisible. You cannot break it into useful parts. You cannot share it. It sits there with only itself and 1, refusing company. A prime afternoon is not noble. It is merely inaccessible.

This is why mathematics is not escape. It is sometimes the most accurate insult available. It lets one say: the day has factorized badly. The morning had too many divisors. The afternoon has none.

I make tea and forget the sugar. The mistake annoys me, then amuses me. A small spark of interest survives the error.

No theorem rescues the room.

But for one minute, while the pan hisses and the spoon rests on the saucer, I remember that a number can look plain and still contain hidden architecture.
