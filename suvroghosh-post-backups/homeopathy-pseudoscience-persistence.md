Acronyms used: AI means Artificial Intelligence. EHR means Electronic Health Record. FHIR means Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources, a standard for exchanging health data. HL7 means Health Level Seven, a family of healthcare data standards. IT means Information Technology.

The small glass bottles in a Calcutta shop window have a strange authority. They sit in neat rows, labelled in a Latin-ish vocabulary, clean and harmless-looking, as if order itself were evidence. Outside, the pavement is broken. Inside, the shelves are disciplined.

That visual discipline is one reason homeopathy has survived so well.

It looks like a system. It has names, rules, rituals, histories, practitioners, colleges, prescriptions, and patient stories. It does not present itself as random hope. It presents itself as a complete parallel architecture of healing.

The problem is that internal architecture is not the same as truth.

Homeopathy began with Samuel Hahnemann in the late eighteenth century. Its two central ideas were "like cures like" and potentization. A substance that produces symptoms in a healthy person was believed to treat similar symptoms in a sick person. The substance was then diluted repeatedly, with shaking at each stage, until many preparations contained no plausible molecule of the original material.

That is where the scientific problem becomes unavoidable.

Modern pharmacology is not perfect, but it is not vague about dose. It asks how a substance enters the body, how much reaches a tissue, how it binds to a receptor or pathway, how the body changes it, how long it remains active, and what measurable effect follows. Homeopathy's high dilutions collide with this basic logic. Once dilution passes beyond the point where molecules of the starting substance are likely to remain, the claimed effect has to be carried by something else.

"Water memory" is often offered as that something else.

But a phrase is not a mechanism. A mechanism must survive measurement. It must explain why the effect appears reliably, why it is specific, why it scales, why controls do not show the same result, and why the ordinary chemistry of water does not erase the supposed imprint. Homeopathy has never supplied that level of evidence.

Clinical trials have not rescued it. When studies are well controlled, blinded, and large enough to reduce noise, homeopathic preparations do not show reliable benefit beyond placebo for the conditions where they have been properly tested. There are always individual anecdotes, but anecdotes are easy to generate in health. Symptoms rise and fall. People recover naturally. Attention itself changes experience. A kind practitioner can feel like relief, and relief can be remembered as proof.

This is not a small point. A patient may feel better after a visit for many reasons. The visit may be calm. The practitioner may listen. The patient may finally have uninterrupted attention. The complaint may have been self-limiting. The act of doing something may reduce anxiety. None of this proves that an ultra-diluted preparation contains a disease-specific force.

So why does the system persist?

Because systems do not survive only on evidence. They survive on trust, convenience, cost, identity, regulation, habit, and the failures of the official system.

In India, that matters. Many people meet mainstream healthcare as expense, crowding, brusque communication, long queues, confusing reports, and a sense that nobody has time to hear the whole story. Homeopathy often offers the opposite performance: long questioning, a soft voice, a personal narrative, and a promise that the body is being understood as a whole. When formal medicine behaves like a railway counter during rush hour, a slower room can feel profound.

This is where healthcare systems become morally complicated. A pseudoscientific framework may still expose a real service failure. Homeopathy is not validated because patients dislike rushed clinics. But rushed clinics help create the emotional market in which homeopathy thrives.

There is also regulatory ambiguity. If a product is treated as low risk, tolerated as tradition, and lightly scrutinized, it can occupy a comfortable zone. It can borrow the language of medicine while avoiding the evidentiary demands placed on medicine. It can say "individualized" when trials fail, "holistic" when mechanisms are challenged, and "gentle" when efficacy is missing.

For healthcare IT, this is a useful warning.

A coherent data model is not necessarily a true model of reality. A clean screen is not a correct workflow. A standard such as FHIR or HL7 can move structured information beautifully, but the fact that a concept is coded does not make the concept scientifically sound. Bad knowledge can be interoperable. False assumptions can have excellent APIs.

That is the uncomfortable lesson. Healthcare systems are full of things that look valid because they have forms, labels, categories, and institutions. Homeopathy is the older, softer version of a modern technology problem: mistaking consistency for correctness.

The right response is not sneering. Sneering is cheap and usually ineffective. The right response is sharper: demand evidence, improve ordinary care, listen without endorsing false mechanisms, and build systems where patients do not have to choose between scientific validity and being treated like a full person.

The bottles in the shop window will probably remain. They have survived empires, reforms, journal articles, and generations of argument. But survival is not proof. In Calcutta, plenty of things survive because the alternative has not been made humane enough.

That may be homeopathy's real lesson. It is not a triumph of hidden science. It is a symptom of a healthcare environment where narrative attention, low cost, and institutional permission can keep a failed idea on the shelf for a very long time.
