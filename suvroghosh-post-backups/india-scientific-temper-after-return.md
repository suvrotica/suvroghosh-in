The rocket rises cleanly on the screen, and then the family chat forwards an astrological warning with equal confidence. That is India in one glowing rectangle: orbital mechanics in one window, planetary appeasement in another.

The problem is not that India lacks intelligence. It does not. India produces engineers, physicians, mathematicians, space scientists, statisticians, coders, and students who can endure examination pressure that would flatten many calmer societies.

The problem is that technical skill is often mistaken for scientific temper.

They are cousins, not twins. Technical skill can build a bridge, write software, operate a machine, manage a supply chain, or clear a test. Scientific temper asks a colder question: how do I know this? It is the discipline of subjecting belief to evidence, even when the belief is comforting, old, popular, patriotic, or spoken by someone senior.

That habit remains thin in everyday life.

India's Constitution asks citizens to develop scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform. It is a beautiful civic sentence. It also asks for something dangerous. Inquiry does not stop politely at the family table, party office, clinic, classroom, temple gate, textbook, television studio, or government speech.

Indian society praises questioning in ceremonies and punishes it in rooms.

A child asks why. The answer is often: because it is written, because elders said so, because this is culture, because do not be oversmart. A student asks for evidence and is treated as rude. A citizen asks for proof and is branded difficult. A junior worker asks why a process exists and becomes troublesome.

So people learn survival: memorize in public, doubt in private, conform at home, calculate at work, and avoid the wrong question at the wrong table.

That produces examination success without intellectual adulthood.

The confusion between culture and mechanism is especially damaging. Myth can be profound without being physics. Ritual can be meaningful without being a clinical trial. A story can hold psychological truth without becoming genetics, aviation, or engineering. Respecting the past does not require making it omniscient.

A confident civilization would say: our ancestors knew remarkable things, misunderstood many things, and lived before the instruments and methods that make modern science possible. That is not humiliation. It is chronology.

But insecurity wants the past to have already known everything. It wants every discovery to be hidden in scripture, every modern result pre-owned by ancestry, every correction treated as insult. That habit does not honor the past. It pickles it.

Superstition among the poor often has practical roots. If care is costly, courts slow, schools weak, work unstable, and public communication unreliable, magical thinking becomes emotional insurance. The astrologer appears where planning fails. The quack appears where trust has collapsed. The rumor appears where institutions are not believed.

So scientific temper is not only a mental habit. It is infrastructure. Clean water teaches biology better than a poster. A fair exam teaches evidence better than a slogan. A transparent investigation teaches engineering better than a condolence speech. A functioning clinic teaches science better than a debate show.

The ordinary Indian is not short of curiosity. Anyone who has sat in a train compartment, tea stall, or queue knows the country talks, probes, argues, compares, and theorizes constantly. What is missing is disciplined method: the pause before forwarding, the willingness to say maybe, the habit of checking whether a claim flatters us too perfectly.

The dangerous question remains small.

How do you know?

If India can make that question socially respectable, the rockets will not be lonely above such a foggy ground.
