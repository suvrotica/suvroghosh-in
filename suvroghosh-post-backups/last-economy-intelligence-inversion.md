The browser tab sat open long after the argument inside it had cooled. The title was grand enough to make a sensible person suspicious: the last economy. Not the next economy, not the changed economy, not a difficult transition, but the last one, as if history had finally reached the counter and asked for the bill.

I do not trust final nouns.

Still, some phrases remain because they point toward a pressure one has already felt. Artificial intelligence is not only another tool in the office drawer. It is a proposal to make a certain kind of intelligence cheap, fast, tireless, and available through ordinary interfaces. If that continues, many markets built around expensive cognition begin to tremble.

The old economy priced scarcity. Skill was scarce. Credentials were scarce. Coordination was scarce. Drafting was scarce. Translation was scarce. Pattern recognition was scarce. Software development was scarce. Market research was scarce. Professional language was scarce. Even confidence was scarce, because a person who could speak in formatted paragraphs and clean diagrams seemed to possess authority.

AI presses on these scarcities. Not evenly. Not completely. Not honestly in every demo. But enough to unsettle the room.

Call it intelligence inversion. The valuable thing used to be the ability to produce the answer. Increasingly, the valuable thing may be the ability to know what answer should be requested, trusted, checked, rejected, connected, and owned.

That is a smaller labor market in some places and a stranger one in others.

The common mistake is to imagine one clean replacement. Human does task. Machine does task. Human goes away. Real economies are messier. A machine lowers the price of a task. The lowered price changes demand. Demand changes workflows. Workflows change institutions. Institutions change power. Somewhere in the chain, a human remains, but not necessarily the same human, with the same bargaining power, doing the same work for the same pay.

The typist did not simply vanish into the word processor. The office rearranged itself.

What happens when the cost of first drafts approaches zero? Perhaps we get more drafts, not better judgment. What happens when everyone can generate strategy language? Perhaps strategy language loses value. What happens when code becomes cheaper? Perhaps the bottleneck moves to product taste, security, maintenance, distribution, trust, liability, and the stubborn question of what should be built at all.

The last economy is not the end of buying and selling. People will still need food, energy, shelter, care, transport, entertainment, status, comfort, and tools. They will still need one another in inconvenient ways. The body remains a serious economist.

But the market for abstract output may change brutally.

A report nobody reads becomes cheaper. That does not make it more valuable. A presentation nobody trusts becomes faster. That does not make the decision sounder. A hundred proposed product ideas can appear in minutes. The hard part remains picking the one that meets a real human need, can be delivered, can be supported, and can survive contact with reality.

AI may make the visible surface of work more abundant while making accountability rarer.

That is the part I keep returning to. In an economy of abundant generated intelligence, the scarce asset may be not brilliance but responsibility. Someone must sign. Someone must choose. Someone must explain failure. Someone must understand consequences beyond the prompt window. Someone must notice when a smooth answer has no roots.

The optimistic version says humans move upward. Less drudgery, more judgment. I hope that happens in many places. But the labor market does not automatically promote people because a tool became better. Employers may simply expect more output, faster turnaround, lower headcount, and permanent availability.

A worker is told to use AI. A manager is told to reduce cost. A client is told everything is faster now. The savings travel upward before the dignity travels anywhere.

This is why the last economy, if the phrase means anything, is not a technical event. It is a political and social argument disguised as a product upgrade. Who owns the models? Who controls the data? Who captures the productivity gain? Who carries the risk when generated work fails? Who gets retrained, and who gets quietly discarded?

From a room in Calcutta, these questions do not feel abstract. They feel like bills, school fees, rent, old laptops, uncertain clients, and younger people trying to guess which skill will still have a price next year.

No economy is last while humans remain needy and inventive. But some forms of earning can end before people are ready for them to end.

The tab is still open. I close it. The screen goes dark, and the room looks more ordinary than the future promised.
