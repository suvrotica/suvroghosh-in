The profile photograph smiles too brightly for the post underneath it. "Open to opportunities." "Grateful for the journey." "Excited to learn." "Please support." Behind the polished words sits a quieter sentence: I need work, and I do not know where the door is.

LinkedIn has become one of India's national X-rays.

It shows a country performing confidence while looking for income. Young people, mid-career workers, displaced specialists, certificate collectors, recruiters, coaches, founders, consultants, and quietly frightened adults all move through the same public bazaar. Everybody networks. Everybody builds in public. Everybody tries to look composed before invisible judges.

The problem is not that Indians are on LinkedIn. The problem is why so many must turn desperation into professional theatre.

There may be too many qualified people for too few good jobs. Or too many certified people whose training does not meet industry need. Or there may be jobs that are imaginary, already reserved, internally filled, posted for optics, or used to collect resumes. The modern ghost job sits on a career page like fruit made of plastic. It suggests growth while feeding silence to applicants.

India has long loved gates. Exams, interviews, coaching pipelines, rankings, cutoffs, application portals. The gate becomes sacred, and education becomes preparation for the gate rather than preparation for work. A student learns to clear filters but not always to understand the system, the user, the customer, the patient, the road, the ledger, the machine, or the problem that needed solving.

This is not the fault of the young. It is the fault of a society that made selection more sacred than education.

Nepotism adds the older technology. No software tool has yet improved upon the well-placed relative. Public opportunity becomes private comfort through family, caste, club, party, school network, and the person who knows a person. India did not invent favoritism. It gave it a tropical climate and excellent distribution.

The country does not lack work in any meaningful sense. Walk outside. Roads, clinics, drains, records, schools, ports, courts, water systems, public health, city planning, climate adaptation, logistics, documents, repairs, translation, nursing, electrical work, software, data, teaching, auditing: everything needs doing.

What India lacks is enough honest connection between need, skill, payment, and dignity.

There are bridges missing between education and work. Real bridges would mean apprenticeship, industry-linked training, laboratories that touch actual problems, teachers allowed to teach reasoning rather than recital, English not used as a class weapon, and employers who stop pretending freshers should somehow have years of experience before the first real task.

Instead we produce performances. Colleges perform relevance. Employers perform meritocracy. Candidates perform preparation. Coaches perform rescue. Government performs development. The platform performs opportunity.

The applicant receives silence.

Climate adds another layer. Indian cities will need people who can shade streets, manage water, protect workers from heat, keep power stable, design public health systems, and build civic infrastructure that survives stress. These are not decorative problems. They are work. Yet the labor market too often channels anxious graduates toward whatever title currently sounds employable.

The practical answer is dull, which is why it rarely receives enough applause: real hiring numbers, penalties for fake recruitment, apprenticeships that pay, public infrastructure as employment and resilience, skill assessment that measures usable work, colleges tied to actual systems, and less worship of degrees as moral certificates.

Most of all, stop telling young people their private failure explains a public malfunction.

A job market reveals the bones under national slogans. If the image looks bad, do not blame the film. Blame the fractures.

The smiling profile remains on the screen. It is trying to look employable. It is also asking whether the country has quietly abandoned it while requesting applause.
