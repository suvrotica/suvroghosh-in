The phone is full, and the room is empty.

This is supposed to be a modern contradiction, but I suspect it is merely an old one with better lighting. People now say loneliness belongs to this generation or that generation, to phones, to cities, to apps, to atomized work, to the collapse of neighborhood life. All of this is partly true. Partial truths are the most energetic kind.

But loneliness is older than the device on the table.

A person can be unseen in a joint family, in a crowded office, in a para club, in a classroom, in a festival crowd, at a dinner table, or inside a suitability conversation arranged by people who think biography is a checklist. The phone did not invent the ache. It gave it a screen and a battery percentage.

What has changed is the performance around it. Everyone appears available. Everyone can be messaged. Everyone has a profile, a last seen, a status, a photograph, a little public proof of existing. The visible surface is busy. The inner fact may remain untouched.

Middle age has its own loneliness. It is not the loneliness of being young and waiting for life to begin. It is the loneliness of seeing which beginnings did not continue. Friends become schedules. Relatives become obligations. Professional contacts become transactions. The old unplanned adda thins out into messages sent between errands.

Calcutta still offers crowds, but crowds are not company. A tram of bodies, a market of voices, a lane full of evening gossip: these can warm the air without entering the person. The city may surround you and still leave you personally unvisited.

I do not romanticize company. Too much company can be another kind of noise. Some solitude is necessary for thought, writing, and the repair of the self. The problem is not being alone. The problem is having no reliable bridge between aloneness and being known.

No generation owns that problem.

The old had it under different names. The young have it with faster tools. The middle-aged have it with better excuses.

The phone lights up. A forwarded message arrives, cheerful and useless.

The room remains exactly as it was.
