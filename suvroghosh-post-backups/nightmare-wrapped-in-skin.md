The sofa-cum-bed gives a small wooden protest when I sit down.

It has earned the right. Cheap furniture has a moral patience that expensive furniture never develops. It accepts heat, weight, dust, neglect, bad sleep, and the private weather of a man who has not always known what to do with himself. In this room, it is less a bed than an agreement between cloth, wood, gravity, and resignation.

Every life needs such agreements.

There are days when the self feels poorly assembled. Not broken in a grand cinematic way. Merely loose. A screw missing here, a hinge tired there, a drawer swollen from damp. The world continues, of course. Calcutta is not interested in one person's inner architecture. The street shouts. The phone lights. The bill waits. Tea must be made. The fan must turn. A person must carry his body from one hour into the next with no orchestra and very little ceremony.

Books have been my legal tunnel through this arrangement.

As a child I did not read to become literary. Children rarely have such fraudulent motives. I read because the page opened a door in the day. Tintin moved with brisk certainty through danger and absurdity. The Phantom, Mandrake, Rip Kirby, Flash Gordon, and the Bengali comics of Narayan Debnath gave the ordinary room a second geography. Satyajit Ray and Shirshendu Mukhopadhyay added intelligence, wonder, odd visitors, old houses, puzzles, and the sense that the visible world was not the whole inventory.

This mattered.

A child does not need a manifesto. He needs proof that the lane outside is not the limit of reality. A book can provide that proof without making false promises. It does not cure. It does not rescue. It does not pay the future. It simply lets air move through a closed place.

Even now, reading works in that modest way. I enter a sentence and leave myself for a while. The return is not always pleasant. The room is still here. The furniture still complains. But a little interval has been made in which the mind was not required to stare directly at its own wall.

Writing and drawing do something similar, though with more labor. A private unease becomes a line, a paragraph, a face, an image. Not solved. Located.

There is dignity in location.

The sofa creaks again. Outside, the city resumes its argument with the afternoon. I open a book, not because it will save me, but because elsewhere is sometimes enough for one hour.
