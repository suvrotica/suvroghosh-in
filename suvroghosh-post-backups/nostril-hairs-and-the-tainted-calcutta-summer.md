One white hair has appeared inside my right nostril with the confidence of a foreign consultant.

It stands there apart from the local population, bright, unnecessary, and pleased with itself. I noticed it in the mirror while leaning too close, which is one of the punishments of middle age. Youth looks into mirrors for promise. Middle age looks for maintenance issues.

The face is only the front office. The rest of the body has its own departments, each filing complaints at different speeds. Some regions remain overgrown. Some have thinned. Some produce small surprises without clearance. Age does not arrive as a noble visitor. It arrives as a damp patch on the wall. First one corner. Then another. Then the house begins to smell of pending repairs.

And then Calcutta summer arrives.

This heat is not the heat of childhood. Childhood heat had mangoes, school holidays, evening hope, and the idea that a storm might come after sunset. The present heat feels more administrative. It sits on the city and processes us. The walls grow warm. The drains breathe upward. The lane loses mercy. A person opens the door at noon and receives the afternoon directly in the chest.

The air conditioner, bought second-hand, becomes less an appliance than a small republic. It rattles, leaks, coughs, grumbles, and still does its work with more honesty than many institutions. I know the privilege in that sentence. Many people face this city under tin, plastic, concrete, and faith in ceiling fans that move only the idea of air.

So I cannot complain.

Naturally, I complain. Complaining in Calcutta is not weakness. It is a respiratory practice.

But behind the comedy is a plain fact: the body is where public failure arrives privately. Bad air enters one chest at a time. Heat is endured room by room. The city may discuss climate, infrastructure, planning, and urban resilience in large nouns, but the individual body receives the bill as sweat, cough, fatigue, and a strange irritation with its own nostril hair.

This is the proper scale of middle age. Young men think life is destiny. Older men learn it is maintenance. Trim the nails. Pay the bill. Avoid the worst hour of heat. Keep the old machine running. Do not turn every small bodily revolt into a prophecy.

The white hair remains.

I leave it there for now, a flag planted by time in a ridiculous territory.
