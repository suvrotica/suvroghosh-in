At five-thirty, the nuisance is still private.

The city has not fully joined it. The lane is dim. The tea shop is not yet authoritative. Somewhere a bucket moves, a door bolt slides, and a pressure cooker considers its first public statement. But inside the room, irritation has already taken a chair.

It may be the heat. It may be the stomach. It may be bad sleep. It may be the fan producing a sound just irregular enough to become a legal matter. It may be nothing. Nothing is often the most successful nuisance because it cannot be cross-examined.

The day begins at the wrong scale. Not with a crisis, which would at least provide drama, but with a small misalignment. The body does not fit the morning. The mind opens one eye and immediately objects to management. The cup is not where it should be. The towel is damp. The phone has a message that can only reduce faith in human planning.

It is tempting to despise such smallness. A serious person should think of serious things. Democracy, science, work, poverty, history, the future of AI, the price of rice. Yet a human being does not enter the day through abstractions. He enters through the room. Through heat, thirst, digestion, cloth, noise, light, and the exact moral condition of the tea.

Small discomforts are not small while they are happening.

This is one reason public life is so badly understood by people who speak only in large nouns. A policy becomes real as waiting, walking, sweating, filling, paying, repeating. A city becomes real as a drain smell at the wrong hour. A life becomes real as a private nuisance before breakfast.

By seven, the city will provide company for my irritation. Traffic, voices, messages, errands, dust. The private nuisance will dissolve into the public supply.

For now it sits beside me, early and precise.

I boil water. Some days begin not with courage, but with damage control.
