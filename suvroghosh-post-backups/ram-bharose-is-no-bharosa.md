The rice stuck to the bottom of the cooker in one pale stubborn layer.

Too much heat, too little water, and the machine did what all systems do when neglected: it produced residue. I scraped it with a spoon and thought of that old phrase, Ram bharose. Leave it to divine care, to fate, to the invisible hand that is always praised after visible hands have failed.

In India, this phrase is not only religious. It is administrative.

The drain is Ram bharose. The file is Ram bharose. Payment is Ram bharose. The repairman, the road, the job application, the election promise, the cousin who said he would call, the client who said next week, the institution that exists mostly as a board outside a building. All floating in the same mild fog of deferred responsibility.

But bharosa means trust, and trust requires performance.

A cooker earns trust by cooking. A bridge earns trust by standing. A person earns trust by doing what he said he would do when nobody is clapping. Without that, trust becomes a decorative word. It looks good in speeches and collapses under weight.

Fatalism has its comforts. It removes the shame of agency. If everything is fate, no one has to answer for the cracked wall, the unpaid worker, the missing form, the quiet theft hidden under procedure.

From a small room, this comfort looks expensive.

I cannot afford too much fatalism. Rice must be measured. Water must be added. The switch must be pressed. Even then, things go wrong. But at least the failure has an address.

The burnt layer comes loose in fragments.

I eat what can be eaten and leave the rest soaking in water, which is sometimes the most rational theology available.
