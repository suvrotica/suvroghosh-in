Acronyms used: AI means Artificial Intelligence. API means Application Programming Interface. EHR means Electronic Health Record. FHIR means Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources. HL7 means Health Level Seven. HL7 v2 means Health Level Seven version 2. IT means Information Technology.

Bad explanations breed fastest where uncertainty is real, consequences are personal, and nobody has been taught the grammar of chance.

Calcutta is full of small evidence. A neighbor's son got a job after one coaching center. A shop failed after changing its signboard. A patient recovered after a ritual and a tablet and three anxious phone calls. A road flooded after one party came to power. A rumor crossed five neighborhoods before the tea cooled. The city produces stories at a speed no fact-checking office can match.

The problem is not that people are stupid. The problem is that life is noisy.

Human beings cannot live inside uncertainty without trying to compress it. A myth is often a cheap compression of anxiety. A superstition is a control ritual invented under pressure. A just-so story explains backward from the result and makes the result feel deserved, inevitable, or morally tidy. The mind meets a foggy system and wants a clean handle.

Randomness is the first insult to that mind. It says that not every event is a message. A person may smoke for decades and live long. Another person may never smoke and still develop lung cancer. That does not mean smoking is harmless. It means individual lives are noisy samples from larger distributions. A distribution is the pattern made by many possible outcomes. One case is not a law.

Dense social life makes this harder. Everyone knows someone. Everyone has an example. Anecdotes move faster than denominators. "My neighbor tried this and recovered" becomes health advice. "My cousin invested there and doubled money" becomes finance. "That student never studied and passed" becomes education theory. A city rich in stories can become poor in causality.

Chaos is the second insult. In everyday speech, chaos means mess. In mathematics, a chaotic system can follow rules and still become practically unpredictable because tiny differences at the start grow rapidly over time. Weather is the polite textbook example. Traffic is the street version. One stalled bus, one bad turn, one impatient rider, one broken signal, and a whole crossing becomes a lesson in sensitive dependence.

Chaotic systems are not magic. They are lawful systems that outrun simple prediction. That distinction matters. If a hospital delay, market panic, price spike, family argument, or public rumor has many interacting causes, saying "everything happens for a reason" is not an explanation. It is wallpaper.

Complexity is the third insult. A complex system is not merely complicated. A jet engine is complicated. A city is complex. A hospital is complex. A family is complex. A healthcare network is complex. Many agents interact, adapt, hide, cooperate, overload, improvise, and route around constraints. The whole begins doing things no single part intended.

This is where bad stories become dangerous. They assign a complex outcome to the nearest visible object. A patient worsened, so the doctor must be careless. Sometimes true. Often incomplete. The real system may include missing history, delayed labs, overcrowded wards, weak handoffs, poor software, exhausted staff, contradictory accounts, reimbursement pressure, and nobody owning the entire process.

Healthcare IT shows this clearly. A lab result arrives late and people call it a data quality problem. Maybe. But the deeper issue may be representational. Was the timestamp specimen collection time, accession time, result time, verification time, interface transmission time, or display time in the EHR? These are not clerical trivia. They are the bones of meaning.

Transport is not meaning.

HL7 v2 can move a message from a laboratory system to an EHR. FHIR can expose structured data through an API. Neither guarantees that the receiving system understands the context. A rumor on WhatsApp also has excellent transport. Its meaning may still be garbage.

This distinction is civic as well as technical. Calcutta has superb rumor transport: families, tea stalls, para networks, apartment groups, clinics, shops, political clubs, and phones. Semantic validation is weaker. Who knows? How do they know? What is the denominator? What case is missing? Who benefits if the claim spreads? What would change our mind? These questions are the public equivalent of schema validation.

A single story is not a system. That is a useful rule. One community is not poor because of one trait. One city is not broken because of one party. One disease surge is not caused by one gathering. One child's success is not caused by one tutor. Complex systems usually have braided causes. Pulling one thread and declaring victory is how bad policy and bad advice are born.

Another rule: coincidence is not communication. A phone call after a dream, a recovery after a ritual, an improvement after three interventions, a victory after a shrine visit - timing seduces the mind. Without comparison, coincidence dresses itself as causality.

A third rule: ask about the missing cases. We hear from winners, survivors, loud relatives, visible families, successful migrants, confident frauds, and people who enjoy narrating themselves. We hear less from people who followed the same advice and sank quietly. In data, missingness often has meaning. In social life, it has even more.

A fourth rule: incentives are stronger than declarations. If a platform rewards engagement, it will produce outrage. If a school rewards rank, it will narrow learning. If a hospital rewards volume, it will optimize throughput. If a software project rewards launch dates over semantic correctness, it will ship brittle systems and call the result modernization.

None of this requires contempt for tradition. Some inherited practices carry observation, hygiene, ecology, coordination, or comfort. Humans need rituals. The mistake is confusing ritual with mechanism, comfort with evidence, age with truth, and identity with causality.

The cure for myth is not blind faith in numbers. Numbers can lie by omission. Models can fail. Algorithms can scale old injustice. Experts can be captured by incentives and institutions. The cure is disciplined doubt, better representation, open correction, and the patience to say: this is what we know, this is what we do not know, and this is what would change our view.

A society that cannot reason about chance becomes cruel to the unlucky. A society that cannot reason about chaos becomes addicted to prediction sellers. A society that cannot reason about complexity becomes easy prey for demagogues, miracle claims, bad software, and loud family certainty.

The mature mind does not stop telling stories. It tells better ones. It keeps the story porous enough for evidence, humble enough for uncertainty, and precise enough to guide action. That is not the death of wonder. It is wonder with better plumbing.
