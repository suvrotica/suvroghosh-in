There is a stone under the skin.

Not a real stone, naturally. The doctor would have found something more billable. This is a private stone: pressure without shape, irritation without a proper address, a little weight that moves from chest to stomach to throat and back again as if the body has become a rented room with poor storage.

Sadness is too soft a word for it.

Sadness suggests a chair by a window, a little rain, perhaps a song behaving respectfully in the background. This is not that. This has heat in it. Shame in it. Memory in it. A sudden anger at harmless objects. A dislike of one's own face in the mirror. A sense that every unfinished task has become an accusation with stationery.

Calcutta heat does not help.

Heat makes the body too present. The shirt clings. The fan argues with humidity and loses. The lane smells of frying oil, drain water, wet dust, and overripe fruit. Small discomforts become large because there is no cool surface on which the mind can rest. The city presses, and the stone presses back.

There are days when the irritation is mistaken for character.

You speak sharply, then regret it. You avoid people, then feel abandoned. You want kindness, then mistrust it when it comes. You remember old failures with new precision. The mind becomes a government office reopening closed files because someone misplaced a stamp in 1998.

That is the cruelty of private suffering. It recruits memory.

A harmless present moment becomes crowded with older material: a humiliation, a lost chance, a room where you should have spoken, a door you should not have entered, a version of yourself that still stands in the corridor waiting for correction. None of this appears on the face in a useful way. The face merely looks tired or rude.

People prefer clean explanations.

They ask what happened. The answer is not clean. Nothing happened. Everything happened. The weather happened. The body happened. Memory happened. The economy happened. The small lonely arithmetic of being older in a city that respects usefulness happened. A hundred minor pressures gathered and pretended to be one feeling.

I do not romanticize it.

There is no nobility in snapping at a cup, avoiding a call, or letting a whole afternoon become a courtroom. But there is a little usefulness in naming the texture accurately. Not sadness only. Not anger only. A stone under the skin. A pressure that must be carried carefully until it becomes smaller or the day becomes larger around it.

Care, when needed, should be real care. Not gossip, not miracle food, not internet advice shouted through a keyhole. A qualified person is sometimes necessary because the private room can become too echoing. That is not weakness. It is maintenance.

Meanwhile, the ordinary work continues.

Wash the face. Change the shirt. Eat something. Do not make theology from the mirror. Do not punish the body for reporting weather badly. Let the city continue outside, noisy and imperfect, as proof that pressure is not the only force in the world.

The stone remains for a while.

Then, on some evenings, it is only a stone again.
