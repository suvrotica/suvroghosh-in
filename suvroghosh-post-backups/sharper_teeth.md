The bathroom mirror at three in the morning can make any grand theory of history look faintly ridiculous.

There you are, older than expected, less heroic than planned, staring at a face that has begun making private arrangements with time. Then the phone lights up with another story about AI, automation, billionaires, layoffs, productivity, disruption, and the future of work. The mirror gives you the body. The phone gives you the system. Neither is flattering.

The old predator-prey equations of Lotka and Volterra describe how one population feeds on another: the prey rises, the predator follows, the prey collapses, the predator suffers, and the cycle continues. The equations are not a full model of society, but they offer a useful discomfort.

In the modern labor market, the predator is not the machine.

The predator is capital.

AI is a sharper tooth.

This matters because people often argue as if the tool itself has moral intention. The chatbot is coming. The robot is coming. The algorithm is coming. Fine. But tools do not deploy themselves at scale. A model does not decide on its own to reduce headcount, weaken bargaining power, compress creative labor, monitor workers, or convert every human skill into a cheaper service layer. Owners, boards, markets, managers, investors, and institutions make those choices.

The machine is not innocent, but it is not sovereign.

It is wielded.

Workers have always been asked to adapt. Learn the machine. Change the skill. Move to the city. Accept the factory. Accept the computer. Accept the platform. Accept the rating. Accept the new contract. The difference now is speed and scope. AI does not automate only one muscle. It moves toward language, image, code, classification, summarization, customer service, analysis, design, and parts of judgment itself.

The teeth have become finer.

They do not need to bite the whole person. They bite the task, the role, the invoice, the bargaining power, the quiet confidence that a skill will remain scarce long enough to support a life.

This is why the optimistic language around technology can feel indecent. Innovation, disruption, efficiency, transformation, future-ready workforce: these words often arrive before the person being transformed has paid rent. It is not wrong to build tools. Many tools help. AI can assist learning, research, writing, medicine, accessibility, and tedious work that no one should romanticize.

The question is who captures the gain.

If AI increases productivity but workers lose income, stability, or dignity, then the argument was never really about intelligence. It was about ownership. If machines make society richer while ordinary people become more replaceable, then the new teeth are serving an old hunger.

This is where the old equations break. In a natural predator-prey cycle, the predator depends on the prey. In human economies, wealth can sometimes buffer itself against the damage done below. Capital can store gains, move jurisdictions, automate dependence, buy influence, and explain harm as transition.

The person below receives a seminar.

He is told to reinvent himself.

Reinvention is easier when the pantry is full.

Still, fear alone is not useful. One should not hate the machine because the owner has bad incentives. That is like hating the knife while ignoring the hand. The better demand is structural: ownership models, labor protections, public investment, retraining that is not a joke, safety nets, bargaining power, transparency, and a culture that treats human beings as more than temporary wrappers around tasks.

In Calcutta, the abstractions become concrete quickly. A person works on a small laptop in a hot room, trying to understand whether his own skill will still buy groceries next year. A new tool appears. It is impressive. It is useful. It is also a message from the future saying: the terms are changing.

The teeth are sharper.

That does not mean the field is empty.

People still learn, organize, build, refuse, adapt, and surprise systems that thought them simple. The human creature is not merely a cost center, though many spreadsheets have tried to prove otherwise. It carries memory, context, embarrassment, improvisation, care, humor, and the inconvenient capacity to ask who benefits.

The mirror shows an aging face.

The phone shows an accelerating machine.

Between them sits the old question, not solved by any model: who gets eaten, who eats, and who writes the terms on the menu?
