A village shrine beside a damp path can be read as theology, but it can also be read as infrastructure.

That is the uncomfortable way to think about Maa Sitala and Maa Manasa. They are not only figures of belief. They are old public-health interfaces built from fear, ecology, memory, ritual, gendered labor, caste, poverty, and the need to give catastrophe a shape before clinics, vaccines, antivenom, laboratories, and ambulances became reliable parts of life.

Sitala belongs to the world of pox, fever, sores, cooling, household fear, and epidemic memory. Manasa belongs to the world of snakes, poison, monsoon fields, ponds, paddy, and rural vulnerability. To call these figures merely quaint is too easy. To call every believer foolish is easier still, and even less useful. Terror asks for form. If a child burns with fever and the skin erupts, the household needs an explanation. If a farmer collapses after a snakebite far from a stocked health facility, the village needs action. A goddess can be wrong biologically and still be socially usable.

That is why mockery cures nothing.

Smallpox is the clearest corrective. It was not defeated by sneering at Sitala worshippers. It was defeated by vaccination, surveillance, case detection, containment, logistics, record-keeping, fieldwork, and political will. WHO describes smallpox as the only human disease to have been eradicated, with the intensified global effort using immunization and surveillance-containment strategies. Science won because it became operational. It arrived as needles, cold chains, local persuasion, records, repeated visits, and people willing to do difficult work in difficult places.

The lesson is severe. Truth must become reachable.

Snakebite shows the unfinished version of that lesson. WHO describes snakebite envenoming as a neglected public-health issue, especially in poor rural communities in tropical and subtropical countries. High-quality antivenom can prevent or reverse many severe effects, but access, distribution, training, regulation, and data remain uneven. A practical public-health system would not waste time insulting Manasa worshippers. It would map risk, stock antivenom where needed, train first-contact providers, build referral pathways, teach communities what not to do, and record the delays that decide outcomes before the patient reaches formal care.

The superstition survives partly because the system is late.

A villager who visits a shrine, then an informal practitioner, then perhaps a government clinic, may not be conducting a clean philosophical referendum between science and belief. He may be choosing what is open, nearby, affordable, socially legible, and willing to touch him. The qualified clinician may be far away. The public facility may be under-staffed. The ambulance number may not answer. The informal practitioner may know the family, speak the local idiom, extend credit, and arrive at night with the confidence formal systems often lack.

That confidence can be dangerous. Informal treatment can delay referral, hide severity, encourage unnecessary injections, worsen antimicrobial resistance, and teach people that seriousness must look dramatic. But its power is not mysterious. It occupies the gap between formal design and lived availability.

Public health often fails by pretending the official map is the territory.

A report says a facility exists. The village experiences a locked building. A dashboard says a doctor is posted. The patient experiences absence. A stock register says antivenom is available. The family discovers shortage after the journey. A data field says "snakebite." The real story includes time of day, transport, first aid done badly, advice from neighbors, money borrowed, and fear turning minutes into waste.

This is not only a data-quality problem. It is a representation problem. The system has chosen too small a container for reality.

In that sense, the old goddesses understood something modern dashboards often miss. Illness is social before it becomes administrative. Sitala rituals created a grammar for household fear. Manasa worship encoded seasonal danger in a landscape where snakes and human labor shared the same wet geography. The scientific problem is that ritual does not neutralize virus or venom. The system problem is that modern care may neutralize mechanism while failing to replace the social functions of ritual: proximity, explanation, trust, continuity, and accountable presence.

A serious rural health architecture would therefore be part clinic, part logistics network, part trust engine, part data system, and part listening practice. It would distinguish pathogen from deity, but also distinguish a database row from a lived event. It would know that belief is not merely an error in the head; it can be a record of repeated institutional absence.

The task is not to romanticize superstition. Superstition can delay vaccination, obstruct antivenom, protect quackery, and turn suffering into a test of faith. It harms. The task is also not to sneer from an urban room with broadband and a pharmacy within walking distance. Sneering is cheap, and rural people have already paid enough for other people's cheapness.

People abandon bad explanations faster when good institutions arrive on time.

Until then, the shrine remains visible. The quack remains available. The dashboard remains clean. The path stays damp after rain. Somewhere in the village, fear still needs a form, and the health system has not yet learned how to stand there reliably in the evening light.
