The shoes shine beside the open drain.

That is the whole essay, almost. A man steps carefully over black water in polished footwear, holding his phone away from the smell, speaking in a voice that suggests national progress is proceeding well. The drain does not argue. It bubbles.

Indian society has perfected this split-screen life. Public decay and private polish. Bad roads and expensive watches. Weak institutions and confident speeches. Education without curiosity. Religion without compassion. Professionalism without payment discipline. Clean collars above dirty systems.

The temptation is to laugh. One should, a little. Laughter prevents the mind from becoming a government file. But the comedy is built on injury. The open drain is not a metaphor if you live beside it. It is smell, illness, mosquitoes, damp walls, shame, and the quiet instruction that some neighborhoods are expected to tolerate what others would call a crisis.

Polish is not the enemy. I like clean shoes. I like order, dignity, pressed clothes, correct forms, punctual meetings, and the fragile civilizing hope that people can present themselves decently in public. The problem begins when polish becomes camouflage. When the shoe matters more than the street. When the ability to step around rot is mistaken for having solved it.

Class teaches this movement early. Step around. Do not look too long. Do not mention the smell in the drawing room. Discuss values. Discuss culture. Discuss development. Discuss the importance of discipline among the poor. Then lift the trouser cuff and cross.

A society can live for a long time on such choreography. It can train citizens to avoid noticing the relationship between comfort and neglect. It can make the visible failure belong to nobody in particular. The drain is municipal, political, historical, economic, technical, and therefore conveniently orphaned.

Meanwhile the shoes continue.

The honest question is not why there is dirt. Every society has dirt. The question is why we become so skilled at arranging respectability around it. Why does the polished person not feel implicated? Why does stepping over the drain feel like intelligence rather than defeat?

The answer is not simple, which is why we prefer speeches.

A scooter passes. Water splashes. The man looks down, annoyed, checking whether the shine survived.

The drain keeps its own account.
