The peg does not become round by being scolded.

This is one of the obvious truths that society keeps forgetting with excellent confidence. It builds round holes, praises roundness, promotes roundness, funds roundness, networks roundness, then looks at the square peg and asks why it is making everything awkward.

Some people do not fit the rooms that reward performance.

They may be intelligent, honest, technically capable, even useful in crisis. But they cannot smile at the correct frequency. They cannot pretend the meeting is meaningful when it is only a ritual with chairs. They cannot produce the easy optimism expected by people who have mistaken manner for competence. They cannot turn themselves into a pitch deck.

The world calls this a personality problem.

Sometimes it is a survival problem.

In Kolkata, the misfit does not float in philosophical air. He has bills. Groceries. Rent or maintenance. A body that ages. Relatives who ask practical questions. A market that rewards presentation. A professional culture that often prefers pliability to depth. The square peg is not only lonely. It is economically exposed.

Income fear changes the mind.

It makes every delay louder. Every rejection becomes a diagnosis of the future. Every successful fool on a screen becomes a small insult from the universe. You know comparison is corrosive and still compare, because the electricity bill does not accept spiritual maturity.

There is also a particular misery in being honest when performance wins.

The person who exaggerates moves faster. The person who flatters survives longer. The person who says "this is structurally broken" is treated as negative. The person who says "great opportunity" gets invited back. A world of round holes does not merely exclude square pegs. It asks them to apologize for having corners.

This can bend the inner weather.

Not dramatically. More like slow pressure on wood. One begins to doubt not only the world but one's own shape. Perhaps I should have been smoother. Perhaps I should have spoken less. Perhaps I should have become more marketable, more cheerful, more lightweight. Perhaps depth itself was a mistake.

Those thoughts are understandable. They are not always trustworthy.

A shape can be inconvenient and still real. A corner can bruise and still contain structure. Not every failure to fit is a failure of worth. Sometimes the room is cheaply made. Sometimes the hole was designed for obedience. Sometimes the peg is asked to become round because the carpenter was lazy.

This is not consolation. It does not pay a bill.

But it prevents one additional injury: the injury of turning social mismatch into total self-condemnation. A person can be in trouble without being worthless. A life can be badly matched to its market without being empty. The distinction is thin on certain mornings, but it is worth defending.

The practical response is modest.

Find the few rooms that need corners. Build one if possible. Reduce performance where it is unnecessary. Preserve the part of the self that still knows the difference between usefulness and applause. Keep the accounts honest, both financial and moral.

The peg remains square.

The weather remains bad.

Somehow the day still requires a shape.
