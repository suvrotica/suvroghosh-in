There is a kind of smile now that looks as if it came pre-installed with the phone.

You know the one. Teeth out, eyes tired, cheeks lifted with professional discipline. It appears in office photographs, school functions, apartment gatherings, prize distributions, religious ceremonies, LinkedIn posts, festival selfies, birthday videos, family WhatsApp albums, and every public moment where the camera arrives before the emotion does.

This is not happiness.

Happiness is less obedient. It arrives with bad timing. It forgets posture. It laughs before the photo is ready. It makes tea spill. It interrupts solemnity. It does not ask whether the lighting is flattering.

What we have now is synthetic jest.

The laugh is not born. It is installed.

The social machine begins, and everyone knows the cue. Someone makes a small remark about salary, weight, marks, age, career, complexion, English fluency, foreign placement, or some other approved category of private anxiety. The target smiles. The room laughs. The cruelty has been dipped in syrup and served as affection.

This is how a society becomes modern without becoming honest.

The old world was not golden. Nostalgia is often bad lighting applied backward. Still, there was a time when people were allowed to look disappointed. A retired teacher could sit on a balcony and disapprove of civilization. A shopkeeper could complain about fish prices with full philosophical commitment. A para elder could dislike your haircut without wrapping the remark in wellness vocabulary.

Now everyone must be positive.

Positive means smiling while sinking. Positive means saying "all good" when the bank balance looks like a pond in May. Positive means posting "new beginnings" when life has merely moved the old difficulty into a new room. Positive means laughing at remarks that are not jokes but tiny social blades.

The fake smile itself is not the whole problem. Humans have always performed a little. Society requires some varnish. Nobody wants each gathering to become a court of absolute truth. But the fake smile has become a moral duty. If you do not smile, you are arrogant. If you do not laugh, you are jealous. If you sit quietly, people assume something is wrong with you.

Something may indeed be wrong. You may be trapped in a room where one person explains mutual funds, another asks intrusive questions, another displays vacation photos like evidence in a civil case, and someone else is preparing to sing after dinner.

The face has limits.

The phone makes the problem worse because the phone converts performance into proof. Every certificate, outing, meal, purchase, gathering, temple visit, award, office event, and domestic milestone is now evidence that life is moving correctly. Everything is decorated. Everything is announced. Everything must become a public exhibit of well-being.

But look carefully.

The eyes often refuse the script.

The mouth performs. The eyes audit.

There is a reason fake laughter becomes ugly after a point. It pulls the face in one direction and the inner life in another. It is like stretching a bedsheet over broken furniture. The shape still shows.

Indian modernity has brought useful things. Digital payments, online tickets, delivered groceries, video calls, better access to information, easier appointments, and the strange pleasure of buying a pressure-cooker gasket at midnight from a phone. This is real convenience. It should not be mocked simply because it is new.

But convenience arrived with performance as a companion.

Every child is talented. Every house is blessed. Every job is exciting. Every event is grand. Every ordinary thing is iconic. Every awkward gathering is memory. Every minor success must be photographed, filtered, captioned, and released into the small aquarium of public approval.

The Bengali middle class adds its own flavor. We do not merely perform happiness. We analyze the performance while performing it. We stand inside the social circus and deliver a lecture on declining standards. We know the smile is fake. We know the compliment is hollow. We know the speech is too long. We know the cultural program has become a test of endurance. Still we say, "Wonderful."

Why?

Because honesty has a cost.

Say, "I am tired." Too negative.

Say, "I am broke." Too embarrassing.

Say, "This job is eating me." Too heavy.

Say, "That joke was cruel." Too sensitive.

Say, "I do not enjoy these gatherings." Too antisocial.

So we do the safer thing.

We grin.

We grin at the successful person whose methods are best discussed near a closed window. We grin at the relative who measures human worth by salary. We grin at the boss who calls overwork ownership. We grin at the neighbor who asks personal questions with the confidence of a census form. We grin at the event where boredom is being distributed with snacks.

Slowly the grin becomes a mask.

Then the mask becomes the face.

This is not a complaint against laughter. Real laughter is one of the cleanest pleasures left. It arrives when someone says something both accurate and foolish. It arrives when a serious person slips into absurdity. It arrives when life drops its files and tries to keep dignity while bending to pick them up.

Real laughter loosens the knot.

Fake laughter tightens it.

A society full of fake laughter becomes dangerous quietly. People pretend not to feel what they feel, so the unsaid things leak out sideways: gossip, sarcasm, road rage, family drama, office politics, WhatsApp poison, passive cruelty, sudden harshness over lunch.

The problem is not simply bad manners.

It is emotional congestion.

Too many unsaid things. Too little legal parking.

At a certain age, one begins to value the honest face. Not the beautiful face. Beauty has sponsors. The honest face can be tired without apologizing. It can sit with tea and silence. It can refuse to turn itself into festival lighting just because someone has raised a camera.

There is a small rebellion available.

Do not laugh at every cruel joke.

Do not smile for every demand.

Do not call every performance wonderful.

Do not reward every loud person with attention.

Do not convert your face into public property.

This does not require rudeness. Rudeness is easy. The harder art is gentle refusal: a small nod, a quiet "hmm," a change of subject, an honest pause, a refusal to join the chorus when the chorus is behaving badly.

Perhaps one honest face in a room gives permission to another. Then someone may finally say the speech is too long, the child is exhausted, the food is cold, the remark was unnecessary, and everyone can stop clapping as if applause were a tax.

The fake smile is not harmless. It is a small daily lie that teaches larger lies where to sit. The honest face, even a tired Calcutta face under a slow fan, has a different dignity.

It says: I will laugh when laughter comes.

Until then, the mouth is closed for maintenance.
