The page is blank for about twelve seconds before it begins misbehaving.

I sit down intending to write something balanced, respectable, and useful. A clean paragraph. A careful point. The kind of writing that could enter a meeting room without alarming the upholstery. Then the sentence looks out at India, at the pothole, the slogan, the ceremony, the invoice, the speech, the decorative optimism, and immediately refuses to become well-behaved.

This is a recurring problem.

The modern world is built like a competition stage. Everyone must perform advancement. The nation must perform greatness. The professional must perform confidence. The writer must perform relevance. The citizen must perform gratitude for improvements still pending in another department. Even failure is expected to polish itself into a lesson.

I am bad at this.

I admire achievement. Bridges that hold, databases that do not collapse, public toilets that can be entered without legal courage, trains that arrive, clinics that function, teachers who teach, engineers who test twice: these things move me more than speeches. Civilization is not a mood. It is maintenance.

But spectacle often borrows the costume of achievement. The camera pans. The music swells. A clean road is found, usually after careful scouting. The nation admires itself in edited light while ordinary people continue negotiating with broken drains, delayed salaries, missing accountability, and the old Indian talent for converting a rule into a suggestion.

Competition, in such a world, becomes morally confusing. What exactly are we competing for? A chair closer to the air-conditioner? A title on a card? The right to speak in a tone that makes other people smaller? The chance to say "merit" while standing on a ladder built partly from accident?

There are contests worth entering. A difficult problem. A serious craft. A public service. A piece of work that can be inspected in daylight. But many contests are simply queues with better lighting. The contender is asked to run, smile, flatter, adapt, advertise, and pretend the track is level.

I have run badly. Sometimes from principle. Sometimes from fatigue. Sometimes because I mistook refusal for dignity when it was merely fear wearing a clean shirt. A man should not flatter himself too easily.

Still, there is relief in not competing for every shiny token waved by the age. One can step aside and ask a simpler question: what is worth doing even if nobody claps?

The answer is usually smaller than ambition prefers.

Write the sentence. Fix the thing. Tell the truth where possible. Leave the stage to those who need the light.
