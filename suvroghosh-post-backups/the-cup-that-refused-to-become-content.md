The tea had grown a roof.

A thin brown skin gathered on top of the cup, wrinkled and faintly oily, like a small republic formed by neglect. I had left it too long. Leave anything too long and it begins to document you.

The internet would like such evidence.

It is hungry for conversion. Pain into content. Failure into lesson. Embarrassment into thread. Private weather into shareable climate. Even the cup, if photographed correctly, could become a symbol of burnout, resilience, authenticity, or whatever word is currently standing near the cash counter with clean shoes.

I distrust this machinery.

Not because I am above it. I am here, writing on a website, arranging sentences for strangers and search engines, pretending the act is both personal and public in some morally acceptable ratio. I know the bargain. But some things resist conversion. The cup on the table is not an asset. The old receipt under it is not narrative design. The heaviness in the room is not a brand position.

Writing can preserve experience.

It can also flatten it.

That is the line I keep trying to feel with my foot in poor light. Say enough to make the thing legible. Do not say so much that it becomes a product. Leave some awkwardness in the room. Leave some stains uncaptioned.

The tea skin tears when I touch it with the spoon.

No metaphor behaves perfectly under pressure.

Outside, someone hammers something unseen. Inside, the cup waits to be washed, and I feel a small, unreasonable pride that it has not yet been made useful to anyone.
