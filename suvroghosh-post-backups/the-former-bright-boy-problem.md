The certificate folder smelled of damp paper and old glue.

One corner broke in my fingers. Not tore. Broke. A small brown flake fell to the floor, and I looked at it with the respect usually reserved for evidence. There it was: the residue of promise.

Every Bengali family knows the former bright boy. He was good in school. He wrote well. He solved sums. Someone at WWA Cossipore English School said something hopeful. Later, St. Xavier's and Park Street added polish to the forecast. A boy becomes a projection before he becomes a man.

That is the danger.

Promise is not achievement. Promise is a loan taken in the name of the future. It grows interest quietly. By middle age, the debt collector arrives with soft shoes and a folder.

The old marks were real. I will not insult the boy by pretending otherwise. He worked. He read. He had energy, curiosity, and that foolish clean belief that intelligence could open the proper door if it knocked in good English.

But life is not an exam paper.

An exam rewards the answer. Life asks whether you can arrange money, weather, digestion, work, sleep, fear, timing, and other people's dishonesty into one functioning day. Intelligence helps. It does not govern.

That was the lesson nobody printed on the certificate.

I put the papers back in the folder. The fungus in the cupboard waited with more patience than any teacher I ever had. Outside, Calcutta continued its usual arithmetic of heat, traffic, and unpaid expectations.

The former bright boy is still here.

Less bright perhaps. More accurate.

He closes the folder gently because some old evidence, even when useless, deserves not to be scattered.
