Waiting is harmless only when it has a chair and a known ending.

A doctor's waiting room has a door. A railway waiting room has a timetable, even if the timetable is a polite fiction. A government office waiting room has at least a counter where the next disappointment will appear in human form.

But what if the waiting room has no door?

That is the problem with a stalled life. At first one waits for a specific thing. A payment. A call. A piece of work. A reply. A clearer morning. Then the days begin stacking quietly. One week becomes a month. A month becomes furniture.

Soon waiting is no longer an activity.

It becomes the address.

In a rented room on the edge of Calcutta, this can happen without drama. The fan turns. The kettle mutters. The laptop opens. The phone does not ring in the useful way. Life does not stop. It simply delays its own evidence.

The dangerous part is adaptation.

A man begins arranging himself for waiting. He keeps the shirt nearby but not ironed. He keeps the application half-ready. He keeps the sentence "things may improve" on a shelf within reach. He becomes available to the future without being visited by it.

No one can live like that without becoming partly furniture.

So the rebellion must be small. Open the document. Send one message. Wash the cup. Step outside before the room convinces you it is the whole world.

The door is not symbolic after a point.

It is wood, latch, heat, corridor, and the unpleasant fact that outside may still refuse you.

I open it anyway.
