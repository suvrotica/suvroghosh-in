By nine in the morning the heat had already joined the meeting.

I was trying to solve one clean problem: income. A respectable word. A word with a belt and a file. In theory, one man, one laptop, one invoice, one problem. In practice, the room filled with other forces.

Rent pulled from one side.

The body pulled from another.

Old work, delayed payment, memory, electricity, food, fatigue, pride, and the peculiar Calcutta humidity that turns thought into damp cloth all joined the calculation.

In physics, the two-body problem can be handled. One object pulls another; the mathematics behaves. Add a third body and the neatness starts wobbling. Add enough bodies and prediction becomes a kind of educated pleading.

Indian life is a many-body problem.

Nobody lets you solve the main equation. Every variable arrives with relatives. Income is not only income. It is tax, delay, bargaining, unpaid time, electricity, data, repair, credibility, and the performance of sounding calm on calls when the room is boiling.

The mind wants simplicity because simplicity is restful. The world keeps adding mass.

This is why advice often fails. Advice imagines one force. Work harder. Network. Apply. Save. Exercise. Sleep. Each verb is sensible in isolation. But the system is not isolated. It is a small body among large gravities.

By noon I have opened the laptop twice and done less than planned.

Still, less is not nothing.

The many-body problem is famous partly because it resists exact solution. It does not therefore stop moving.

Neither, apparently, do I.
