The white kurta is clean enough to make the hunger look respectable. That is the first rule of a predator society: appetite must arrive dressed as development.

In H. G. Wells's The Time Machine, the future is not a shining staircase to wisdom. Humanity has split into the delicate Eloi above and the Morlocks below, keeping machinery alive and returning with appetite. Wells looked at industrial inequality and imagined class separation becoming almost biological.

India has its own version, though naturally with more dust, paperwork, speeches, and television noise.

Here the predators are not underground. They sit behind compound walls, tinted glass, party offices, real-estate boards, police barricades, investment summits, and studios where people in fine clothes explain sacrifice to those who have been sacrificing quietly for years.

The common citizen is asked to be patient, patriotic, flexible, digital, compliant, grateful, and occasionally silent. He must pay fees, fines, bills, informal costs, school charges, health deposits, platform charges, and invisible tolls that arrive under respectable names. Then he is told the nation is rising.

Rising from whose back?

The predator-prey metaphor stings because the system increasingly treats ordinary life as extractable material. The vote is extractable. Attention is extractable. Religious emotion is extractable. Waiting time is extractable. Data is extractable. Land, labor, fear, anger, caste, language, and memory can all be packaged, mobilized, and sold.

Predation does not always look like violence. It may look like a circular, a loan app, a hospital billing desk, a party worker smiling too closely, a contractor not paid for months because every layer above him is waiting for another layer.

The ordinary person is praised in the abstract and neglected in the particular. The farmer is noble until price matters. The worker is heroic until wage matters. The student is the future until the exam leaks. The patient is sacred until the deposit counter opens. The poor are the soul of India as long as they do not appear in visible numbers at the wrong gate.

The powerful are treated differently. A large failure becomes systemic risk. A small failure becomes discipline. A big borrower receives philosophy. A small shopkeeper receives recovery calls.

But it is too easy to pretend the predators are monsters and everyone else innocent. A society does not become this way overnight. It evolves by habit. We admire power when it bullies someone else. We forgive corruption when it feeds our side. We want justice, but also shortcuts. We curse the predator while dreaming of becoming a smaller predator with better parking.

That is the democratic bargain nobody likes to name.

The common person's deeper injury is uncertainty. You cannot plan a life if every institution behaves like weather. School, rent, police, hospital, court, license, job, drainage, bank, municipality: each may work, may not work, may work for someone else, may work if pushed, may work if paid.

Uncertainty creates patronage. Patronage creates preyhood.

A citizen becomes prey when rights become favors. A democracy becomes theatre when the public is invited to clap, shout, vote, and go home emotionally charged but structurally hungry.

The answer is not dramatic despair. Citizens are not prey when they organize. The real antidotes are dull: transparent procurement, courts that work, public health, labor protections, municipal competence, independent unions, reliable data, local accountability, and a culture less easily hypnotized by spectacle.

The predator loves glamour. The citizen should love boring machinery.

The horror is not only that the predator eats. It is that the prey may begin to believe being eaten is national service.
