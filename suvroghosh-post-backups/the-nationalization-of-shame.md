The phone vibrated beside the tea pan with the confidence of bad news.

The number was not saved. That made it worse. A saved number is at least a known problem. An unknown number contains the whole country.

Before I answered, my stomach began its own paperwork.

This is how shame enters the morning. Not as drama. As vibration, heat, uncertainty, and a question one does not want to answer in a polite voice.

Private shame is never fully private in India. It is socialized early. The family knows. The neighbor guesses. The banker calculates. The client delays. The old classmate updates his profile. The landlord speaks in numbers. The government form asks for proof. Even silence becomes a public document if enough people lean toward it.

This is what I mean by nationalization.

A personal failure is quickly acquired by the larger system. It is processed, stamped, narrated, compared, and redistributed as common knowledge. A man may own very little, but his embarrassment becomes public infrastructure.

Education makes this worse. A trained mind can see the pulleys. It notices each polite insult, each rising price, each delayed reply, each cheerful phrase used to cover extraction. Ignorance may not be bliss, but clarity is not always power.

The phone keeps vibrating.

If I answer, I become available. If I do not, I remain accused by my own table.

For a few seconds this resembles choice.

Then I let it ring out.

Outside, Calcutta continues its long experiment in making endurance look normal. Inside, the tea is too hot, and I burn my tongue on a simpler pain.
