The first useful sign of intelligence may be the private suspicion that you are not as clever as advertised.

Not theatrical self-abuse. Not a rain-soaked cinema scene. Something smaller. Morning. Fan noise. Tea too sweet. A bill waiting. A vegetable seller outside using his lungs like public infrastructure. In the middle of this ordinary arrangement, you ask: what if I am wrong?

That question is unpleasant.

Good.

The dangerous person is not the one who says, "I don't know." That person may yet learn. The dangerous person knows everything by breakfast. He knows the economy, medicine, politics, climate, history, education, morality, foreign policy, and why every other person is disappointing. Force replaces evidence. Volume replaces thought.

Certainty feels strong from inside. From outside it often looks like a plastic chair pretending to be a throne.

Even the physical world refuses our appetite for perfect confidence. Measurements have limits. Models have assumptions. The deeper one looks, the more reality says: not so fast. If the universe itself resists being pinned down completely, what business does a person have treating every opinion as a gold coin?

Ask why.

Why do I think I know this?

What would prove me wrong?

Can I explain the other side fairly?

Do I have evidence, or only repetition?

Am I learning, or decorating myself with words?

These questions are soap for the mind.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is cruel because it hides the measuring instrument. When skill is low, the person may not yet have enough skill to notice the low skill. The beginner sees simplicity because the machinery is still invisible. The expert sees the machinery and becomes nervous.

A beginner sees a harmonium and thinks pressing keys makes music. A musician sees breath, timing, fingers, listening, practice, failure, and the audience's dangerous relatives. A beginner sees politics and says remove the bad people. Someone older asks who controls money, police, land records, contracts, courts, schools, television, and the little office where files vanish.

The same applies inward.

The beginner says, I am clever.

A better mind asks: compared with whom, in which domain, under what conditions, after how much sleep, with whose checking?

The mind is not a neutral judge. It is often a lawyer hired by the ego. It hides evidence, attacks witnesses, explains away repetition, and says everyone does this. It loves comparing downward. At least I am not that person. This is a comfortable cushion and usually full of dust.

The cure is not self-hatred. Self-hatred is just ego wearing torn clothes. The cure is practical humility.

Leave one door open.

I may be wrong.

Not always. Not performatively. Just enough to check before speaking, posting, deciding, accusing, or building a whole moral palace on one shaky fact.

This matters now because every phone is a small factory of certainty. Headlines, clips, slogans, summaries, outrage, confident experts, less confident experts pretending otherwise, and people who have watched three thumbnails and are ready to instruct civilization. The mind feels informed because it has been poked repeatedly.

Being poked is not education.

A mosquito also gives repeated notifications.

Life still requires action. Doubt cannot become a swamp. You must choose, pay, answer, submit, repair, decide. So doubt should be a handrail, not a cage.

Use it before certainty hardens.

Enough to listen.

Enough to ask for proof.

Enough to avoid becoming that magnificent public nuisance: the person who mistakes his own echo for applause.

The mind, like a Calcutta balcony, gathers dust faster than expected.

Sweep daily.
