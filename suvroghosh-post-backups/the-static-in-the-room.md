The room is quiet, but not silent.

There is the fan, of course. There is always the fan, cutting the air into tired circles. There is a wire hum, a faint traffic residue, a scooter somewhere, a pressure cooker two walls away, and the tiny electrical complaint of devices waiting to be touched. But beneath these ordinary noises is another static, harder to locate.

It sits between intention and action.

I open the laptop. The work is clear enough. Move this file. Answer that message. Read that page. Write the paragraph. None of these tasks is heroic. Yet the hand pauses. The mind fills with interference. Not thought exactly. Thought has shape. This is more like dust on a signal.

Modern life produces static efficiently. News, bills, unfinished work, old mistakes, future costs, social comparison, technological change, public disorder, and the private irritation of being a person who must keep explaining himself to himself. Each item is small. Together they create a weather.

Calcutta adds its own frequency. A drill starts without warning. A vendor calls. Someone quarrels near a gate. The electricity flickers just enough to remind you that confidence is premature. The city is a shared radio with too many hands on the dial.

The problem with static is that it imitates meaning. It feels important because it is loud inside. But much of it is only interference. The body hears threat where there is only clutter. The mind hears verdict where there is only fatigue.

So the work becomes technical in the smallest sense. Reduce noise. Close tabs. Put the phone face down. Drink water. Choose one task too small to respect. Complete it badly if necessary. Let action create a cleaner signal.

This is not a cure. It is tuning.

The fan continues. The city continues. The static does not vanish, but a sentence comes through.

I write it before the signal breaks again.
