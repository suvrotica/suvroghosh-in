Some events leave the room, but their chair remains.

That is the simplest way I can describe long-lasting distress. The incident is over. The person is gone. The office closed. The conversation ended. The financial wound acquired paperwork. The betrayal found new clothing. Yet something still sits in the room, occupying space, making ordinary movement awkward.

People prefer suffering with a clear date.

This happened, then that happened, then recovery began. The mind should love such timelines because they are tidy and suitable for forms. But life often behaves more badly. Acute shocks become background weather. A hostile environment teaches the body to expect insult. A failed business, a broken trust, a social humiliation, a long period of instability: these do not always remain in the past simply because the calendar moved.

The body remembers in practical ways.

A message tone tightens the stomach. A meeting invitation produces dread before thought. A familiar phrase reopens an old corridor. A small conflict feels larger than its size because it resembles earlier danger. The mind says, "This is now." The body says, "I have seen this road."

Both may be partly right.

That is what makes recovery difficult. Not every fear is false. Not every memory is wisdom. A person who has lived through enough hostility can become a poor predictor and an excellent witness at the same time. The problem is deciding which role is speaking.

Calcutta, with its closeness and noise, gives little privacy to old pain.

You can be buying vegetables and suddenly remember a sentence from years ago. You can sit in traffic and feel an old helplessness return with the smell of petrol and dust. You can pass a building and find that the body has already reacted before the mind has named the memory. Cities store us in inconvenient places.

The worst advice is usually "move on."

Move where? The body is already here. The room has a chair. The memory has keys. Movement is not a command; it is a slow reorganization of trust, habit, environment, and interpretation. Sometimes care is needed. Sometimes distance is needed. Sometimes money is needed. Sometimes sleep, food, legal clarity, better company, or simple time is needed. Often several are needed and none arrives neatly.

The task is not to become untouched.

That is impossible and slightly inhuman. The task is to prevent old shocks from becoming the entire constitution of the self. A past event can be evidence without being law. A wound can explain a reaction without owning every future reaction. The room can contain the chair without arranging all furniture around it.

Writing helps me because it gives shape without pretending to cure.

On paper, distress becomes less like fog and more like furniture. This chair is old fear. That table is anger. That cupboard is shame. This window is still available. Naming the furniture does not remove it, but it reduces the chance of walking into it in the dark.

I distrust triumphant endings here.

Some suffering does refuse to leave the room. Some chairs remain. But a room can be rearranged slowly. A path can be made between the bed and the door. A window can be opened. One can learn which chair not to sit in after midnight.

The event is over.

The room is not empty.

Still, the door is not sealed.
