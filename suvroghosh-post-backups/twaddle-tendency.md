Twaddle is what happens when language puts on a clean shirt and escapes before meaning can ask where it is going.

It begins innocently. A mouth opens. Air rises. The tongue stamps a few sounds and sends them into the room. This is how we ask for tea, warn someone about an open manhole, or say something kind before life resumes its regular habit of stepping on toes.

Then someone says, "At a high level, going forward, we must leverage cross-functional synergies."

The air becomes poorer.

Twaddle Tendency is the urge to fill silence with word-soup because silence asks dangerous questions. What do you mean? Why are we here? Why did the sentence "fix the billing problem" become "a multi-stakeholder revenue-cycle optimization initiative"?

Silence is risky.

So people talk.

They do not say. They talk.

The difference matters. Saying carries meaning from one mind to another. Twaddle splashes around and calls itself infrastructure.

I have sat in rooms where words gathered like damp clothes. Framework. Alignment. Visibility. Roadmap. Transformation. Stakeholder. Ecosystem. Robust. Scalable. Future-ready. Each word entered with confidence and immediately collapsed into a plastic chair.

And everyone nodded.

Nodding is the exercise of the frightened employee. It is cheaper than disagreement and safer than clarity.

Twaddle is not always villainy. Often it is fear. I know because I have used it. I have said complexity when I meant fear. Constraints when I meant laziness. Process gap when I meant nobody cared enough to repair the thing. Resource challenge when I meant poor and pretending otherwise.

Still, twaddle becomes dangerous when it hides harm.

Nobody says, "We will make your life harder." They say transitional adjustment. Nobody says, "You are fired." They say talent optimization. Nobody says, "The project failed because the assumptions were false and nobody wanted to admit it." They say learnings have emerged.

Bad language softens the edge of cruelty.

Science suffers too. Real scientific words are difficult little machines. Quantum, neural, energy, frequency, DNA, algorithm: these words came from labor, measurement, and argument. Then public nonsense steals them, rubs them with confidence, and uses them to sell superstition, management fog, or personal branding.

The result is camouflage.

Rotten facts arrive perfumed.

Plain sentences are feared because they can be checked.

The money is gone.

The meeting is useless.

The bridge failed.

The promise was false.

The room smells.

These sentences are small bombs. They clear the air. They also make enemies, which is why people prefer abstraction.

But brevity alone is not virtue. A slogan can do in four words what a bad lecture does in forty minutes. The cure is not fewer words. The cure is honest words. A long sentence can be clear if it has a spine. A short sentence can be a con if it has a hidden knife.

Test language against the body.

Put the sentence beside a toothache, an unpaid bill, a hot room, a flooded lane, a person waiting for service at a public counter. If the sentence survives there, it may be real. If it cannot stand beside sweat, hunger, fear, work, and the price of fish, it may be only perfume.

Somewhere today, a projector hums. Tea cools. A presenter says meaningful engagement. The front row nods with disciplined faces. Outside, actual life continues without slides.

For one second, someone in the back row refuses to add anything to the air.

That small silence may be the clearest sentence in the room.
